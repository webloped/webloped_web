Old Monk can't be sold as "rum" anymore: what India's food safety regulator actually ruled

India's food safety authority told the Bombay High Court that the iconic Old Monk fails the legal definition of rum. Added flavourings and a "7 years old" claim that didn't hold up in lab tests are at the centre of it.

Old Monk is one of the most recognisable liquor brands in the world - the squat, monk-embossed bottle has been a fixture in India since 1954. In September 2026, India's Food Safety and Standards Authority (FSSAI) told the Bombay High Court something that stunned its loyal drinkers: as currently made, Old Monk cannot legally be sold as "rum" at all.

What the regulator found

FSSAI's laboratory tests found that Old Monk contains added artificial and nature-identical rum flavouring substances. Under India's Food Safety and Standards (Alcoholic Beverages) Regulations, 2018, a standardised spirit like rum is supposed to get its characteristic taste and aroma from fermentation, distillation, and maturation - not from flavourings added afterwards.

The regulator's argument, put before a bench of Acting Chief Justice Ravindra Ghuge and Justice Gautam Ankhad, was blunt: adding "rum flavour" to rum is a misrepresentation, comparable to adding tea flavour to tea. The added flavourings, FSSAI said, mask the product's natural characteristics and render it "substandard" under the regulations. Products made this way must be front-labelled as "rum-flavoured spirit" or "flavoured rum", not simply "rum".

The "7 years old" claim didn't survive testing either

The second blow was about age. Old Monk XXX Rum was sold as a "7 years old blended" rum. FSSAI's testing reportedly found that less than 5% of the blend was actually matured rum - more than 95% was unaged neutral spirit. Under FSSAI rules, when a drink is a blend, the age on the label must reflect the youngest spirit in the blend.

Three Old Monk variants were caught up in the enforcement action: The Legend, Gold Reserve, and XXX Matured Rum. The maker, Mohan Meakin, has agreed to re-label the products with an "Added Flavour" tag and to drop the "7 years old blended" claim, keeping the liquid itself unchanged.

It wasn't just Old Monk

The crackdown swept across the industry. Also hit were McDowell's No.1 Rum, Bagpiper Deluxe Whisky, Old Cask Deluxe XXX Rum, Royal Challenge Whisky, and Antiquity Blue - all flagged for the same pattern of neutral spirit plus added flavourings, or misleading label claims. Company lawyers told the court the sales prohibition was costing roughly INR 1 crore per day.

To be clear: this isn't about "fake" bottles

It is worth separating two different things. Old Monk is a genuine, historic brand made by Mohan Meakin - this ruling is about regulatory classification and labelling, not counterfeiting. Nobody alleged the bottles were fake. The dispute is whether a rum made with added rum flavouring and mostly unaged spirit can still be called "rum" under food safety law. FSSAI's answer: no.

The Canada connection

The same brand has a history on Canadian shelves. Old Monk was sold through Ontario's LCBO - in November 2007 the LCBO issued a Class I recall of 750ml bottles (production dates July 18-19, 2007) after minute glass particles were found in some bottles. It remains a cult favourite among Indian-Canadians: a Change.org petition has even asked the LCBO to stock it more consistently, calling it a winter comfort for the diaspora. As of early 2026, LCBO listings showed no stores carrying it.

Whether the FSSAI re-labelling affects bottles sold abroad will depend on each country's own labelling rules - Canada's food and liquor labelling requirements are separate from India's. But the underlying facts about how the rum is made don't change at the border.

Why it matters

For drinkers, the takeaway is simple: the label is about to become more honest. "Rum-flavoured spirit" tells you exactly what FSSAI's labs found - a mostly neutral spirit with rum flavouring added. For the industry, it's a warning shot - India's regulator has shown it will test what's actually in the bottle, not just read the label. And for one of India's most loved brands, it's the biggest identity crisis in its 70-year history.
