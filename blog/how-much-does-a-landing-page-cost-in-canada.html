How much does a landing page cost in Canada in 2026?

A landing page can cost less than a tank of gas or more than a used car. This guide explains what a landing page actually costs in Canada in 2026, in Canadian dollars, what drives the price up or down, and how to tell whether a quote is fair before you sign anything.

The short answer

How much does a landing page cost in Canada? It depends on who builds it and how much is custom. As a starting point for 2026, in Canadian dollars:

  • Do it yourself on a website builder: about $60 to $180 in the first year for the platform, plus your domain and your time.
  • Freelancer or small studio: commonly about $800 to $2,500 for a single professionally built landing page.
  • Small agency: commonly about $2,000 to $5,000 for a single landing page with strategy, design, and copy support.
  • Monthly plans: some providers bundle the page into a monthly website plan instead of a one-time build fee. Always compare the first-year total, not just the upfront number.

Those are typical market ranges you will see published in Canada, not a quote for your project and not our prices. A landing page with custom copywriting, original design, a booking or payment integration, and several test variants will cost more than a clean one-page site built from a good template. A page that reuses your existing brand, copy, and photos will cost less. The rest of this guide shows you how to read a quote and where the money actually goes.

What a landing page is, and what it is not

A landing page is a single web page built around one offer and one action. Someone arrives from a Google ad, a Facebook ad, an email, or a QR code, reads a focused pitch, and does one thing: requests a quote, books a consultation, downloads a guide, registers for an event, or buys a specific product. Everything on the page supports that one action. There is usually no full menu, no blog, and no list of every service you offer.

That focus is the point, and it is also why a landing page is not a replacement for a full website. A website answers many questions for many kinds of visitors and builds search visibility over time. A landing page converts one kind of visitor for one campaign. If you are deciding between the two, our guide on landing page vs website breaks down when each one wins. Many Canadian small businesses end up with both: a website for credibility and search, and landing pages for specific campaigns, services, or locations.

Common landing page jobs in Canada include a Google Ads page for a trades business in one city, a consultation booking page for a clinic or coach, a lead magnet page for a free guide or checklist, an event registration page, and a product launch page for an online store. Each one has the same basic parts, which is why quotes are easier to compare once you know what should be included.

How much does a landing page cost in Canada, by who builds it?

Who builds the page is the single biggest cost driver. The same one-page brief can produce three very different quotes, and none of them is automatically wrong. They are different products.

1. Do it yourself with a website builder

Platforms such as Squarespace, Wix, and GoDaddy let you build a landing page from templates for a monthly or annual plan fee. In Canada, a basic builder plan for a single page commonly works out to roughly $60 to $180 in the first year, with higher plans for more features. Your domain, usually about $15 to $25 per year in CAD for a .ca or .com, is often extra after the first year. AI-assisted builders can cost a bit more per month and can draft a first version quickly, but you still need to edit the copy, check the mobile layout, connect your form, and set up tracking.

DIY makes sense when your budget is tight, your offer is simple, and you are comfortable learning the tool. The hidden cost is your time, and the hidden risk is a page that looks fine on your laptop but loads slowly or reads poorly on a phone, where most ad clicks land. You also rent the page rather than own it outright: stop paying the plan and the page goes offline.

2. Freelancer or small studio

This is where most Canadian small businesses land for a campaign page. A freelancer or small studio landing page commonly runs about $800 to $2,500 CAD for one page. At the lower end you usually get a clean, mobile-first page built from a strong template, using copy and images you supply, with a contact or quote form and basic analytics. At the higher end you get custom design matched to your brand, help tightening the copy, a booking or CRM connection, and tracking set up properly for ads.

You are paying for judgement as much as building: what goes above the fold, how short the form should be, what proof to show, and what to leave out. For a page that will carry paid traffic, that judgement is usually worth more than the build hours. Ask for examples of landing pages, not just homepages, and ask what happened after launch in plain terms, such as whether enquiries increased. Be wary of anyone who guarantees a specific conversion rate or a specific number of leads. Nobody can promise that honestly.

3. Agency or campaign specialist

Agencies and paid-media specialists commonly charge about $2,000 to $5,000 CAD for a single landing page, and more for a set of variants or a full funnel with a thank-you page and email follow-up. You are paying for a team: strategy, copywriting, design, development, and often ad management and testing after launch. Timelines are usually one to three weeks for a single page once content is ready, and longer for campaign sets.

An agency page makes sense when the ad spend behind the page is large enough that a small improvement in conversion rate pays for the build, or when the page must meet strict brand, accessibility, or bilingual requirements. If your ad budget is a few hundred dollars a month, a solid freelancer page is usually the better value. If you are spending thousands a month on ads, the maths flips: the page is the cheapest part of the campaign and the part that decides whether the spend works.

4. Monthly plans that include the page

Some Canadian providers sell a landing page as part of a monthly website plan with little or no upfront build fee. That can be a fair model, but compare like with like. Add up twelve months of the plan, plus domain and any tool fees, and compare that first-year total with a one-time build quote. Also ask what happens if you cancel: do you keep the page, the copy, and the domain, or does everything disappear? Ownership matters more than the payment shape. What drives the price up or down?

Within any tier, these are the levers that move a landing page quote. Use this list to understand a quote, and to decide where to spend and where to save.

  • Copywriting. If you hand over finished, tight copy, the build is faster and cheaper. If the builder has to interview you, write the page, and revise it with you, expect that time in the quote. Good landing page copy is short, specific, and written for one reader. It is also the part most owners underestimate.
  • Custom design vs template. A well-chosen template adapted to your brand costs less than a fully custom design. Custom design earns its keep when your brand must look distinct, when you have strict brand guidelines, or when the template fights your content.
  • Form and integrations. A simple form that emails you is cheap. A form that creates a lead in your CRM, sends an automatic confirmation, books a calendar slot, or takes a deposit takes setup and testing. Each integration is a small project of its own.
  • Booking, payment, or ecommerce. Taking a booking with availability rules, or a payment with taxes and receipts, adds real complexity. In Canada that includes GST and HST handling where it applies, and clear pricing in CAD.
  • Tracking and analytics. A page without tracking is a guess. Proper setup means Google Analytics, ad conversion tracking, and a thank-you page or event that fires only when someone actually enquires. It takes an hour or two to do well and saves you from flying blind.
  • Variants and testing. One page is one price. Three versions for three audiences, or an A/B test setup with ongoing reporting, is more. Start with one strong page and add variants once you have traffic worth testing.
  • Speed and mobile quality. Most landing page traffic is on phones. Compressing images, keeping the page light, and testing on a real phone takes time, and it is time well spent. A slow page burns ad money.
  • Accessibility and language. Canadian businesses may need to meet accessibility standards such as AODA in Ontario, and some need English and French versions. Both are worth doing properly and both add work. Say so in the brief, not after the quote.
  • Timeline pressure. A page needed live this week for an event or a sale will cost more than the same page with a month of lead time. Rush work displaces other work.
  • Revisions. Most quotes include a set number of revision rounds. Endless tweaks are where budgets leak. Approve the copy before design starts and you will save real money.

What should be included in the price?

A fair landing page quote should spell out, in writing, at least the following. If any item is missing, ask whether it is included or extra before you compare prices.

  • A mobile-first design that matches your brand, tested on a real phone
  • One clear headline, one offer, and one main call to action
  • A short form, booking flow, or checkout that actually works, tested end to end
  • A thank-you page or confirmation message, plus an email notification to you
  • Basic on-page setup: page title, meta description, and a clean URL
  • Analytics and conversion tracking connected to your ad account where relevant
  • Fast loading, with images sized and compressed for phones
  • Your domain connected, with HTTPS working
  • Ownership terms: the domain in your name, and clarity on what you keep if you part ways
  • A short handover showing you how to edit text and where enquiries go

Two things are almost never included in a landing page build price, and that is fine as long as you know it. First, ad spend: you pay Google, Meta, or another platform directly for clicks, separate from the build. Second, ongoing optimisation: testing headlines, rewriting sections, and reporting on performance after launch is usually a monthly service, not part of a one-time build. Ask for both numbers up front so the first month holds no surprises. The ongoing costs people forget

The build price is not the total price. Budget for these running costs in CAD:

  • Domain: roughly $15 to $25 per year. Register it yourself so you own it.
  • Hosting or builder plan: from a few dollars a month on shared hosting to a higher monthly plan on a builder or managed host with backups and security.
  • Tools: email marketing, CRM, booking, or form tools often carry their own subscriptions, sometimes free at low volume.
  • Maintenance: updates, backups, and small edits, handled by you or through a care plan.
  • Ad spend: separate from the page, and usually the larger number. A cheap page carrying expensive clicks is false economy.

Ask every provider this question before you sign: what will this page cost me per year after launch, not counting ad spend? A clear answer is a good sign. A vague one is not.

Landing page vs full website: what changes the cost?

A landing page costs less than a full website because there is less of it: one page, one audience, one action, no menu of services, no blog, no search strategy. A full small business website in Canada commonly runs into the low four figures and up with a freelancer or small studio, and more with an agency, because every extra page needs design, copy, and testing. That does not make the landing page the cheaper strategy in the long run. If you need five landing pages for five services, a proper website with strong service pages may cost less and rank better in search. The honest comparison is total cost for the job you need done, not price per page.

A practical pattern for many Canadian small businesses is to start with the website for credibility and search, then add landing pages for campaigns that need a tighter pitch than a service page can give. The reverse also works: launch one landing page to test an offer, prove people want it, then build the full site around what you learned.

When a landing page is worth paying for, and when it is not

Pay for a professional landing page when you are sending paid traffic to it, when the offer is valuable enough that a few extra enquiries cover the build, when you need a booking or payment flow that must not fail, or when your brand has to look credible to a cold audience that has never heard of you. In those cases the page is not a brochure. It is the salesperson for the campaign, and it should be built like one.

Build it yourself, or use a simple page on your existing website, when you are testing an idea with no ad spend, when the audience is warm, such as your own email list, or when the page is temporary for a single event. You can always upgrade the page once the offer proves itself. What you should not do is spend heavily on ads pointing at a homepage that was never designed to convert, or at a DIY page you have never tested on a phone. That is where money quietly disappears.

How to get an accurate quote

You will get far better landing page quotes if you prepare a one-page brief first. Cover these points and send the same brief to every provider so the quotes are comparable:

  1. The one action. What should a visitor do on this page: call, request a quote, book, buy, or download?
  2. The audience and source. Who is it for, and where will they come from: Google Ads, social ads, email, or a QR code?
  3. The offer. What exactly are you offering, at what price or starting price in CAD, and is there a deadline?
  4. Your content status. Do you have a logo, photos, testimonials you have permission to use, and draft copy, or do you need help creating them?
  5. Integrations. Does the form need to reach a CRM, a booking calendar, or a payment processor? Name the tools you already use.
  6. Examples you like. Two or three landing pages you admire, with one sentence each on what you like. This prevents most miscommunication.
  7. Deadline and budget range. A realistic date and a budget range help a provider scope the right product instead of guessing.

Then ask each provider the same questions: what is included, what is extra, who owns the domain and the page, how will tracking be set up, how many revision rounds are included, and what will it cost per year after launch? Our guide on what to ask a web designer before hiring goes deeper on the hiring side. Compare the answers, not just the totals. The cheapest quote that excludes copy, tracking, and mobile testing is not the cheapest page. It is an unfinished one.

How to keep the cost down without hurting results

There are honest ways to spend less on a landing page. Supply finished copy and real photos. Reuse your existing brand, logo, and colours instead of redesigning everything for one page. Keep the form short: name, contact detail, and one question about the job is usually enough. Launch with one page and one offer, and add variants only after you have traffic and data. Use the tools you already pay for instead of adding new subscriptions. And approve copy before design starts, because rewriting a designed page costs more than writing it well once.

There are also false savings to avoid. Do not skip tracking, because then you cannot tell whether the page or the ad is the problem. Do not skip mobile testing, because that is where your visitors are. Do not let the builder register your domain in their name. And do not buy a page with no thank-you step, because you will never know which enquiries came from the campaign. A landing page is a small build with an outsized job. Spend on the parts that decide whether a visitor becomes a customer, and save on the rest.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a landing page cost in Canada?

In Canada in 2026, a do-it-yourself landing page on a website builder typically costs about $60 to $180 in the first year for the platform alone. A freelancer-built landing page commonly runs about $800 to $2,500 CAD. A small agency landing page commonly runs about $2,000 to $5,000 CAD. Custom copy, design, integrations, and multiple variants push the price higher. These are typical market ranges, not a quote for your project.

How long does it take to build a landing page?

A simple landing page built from a template can be live in a few days once your copy, logo, and photos are ready. A custom landing page with original design, copywriting, and integrations usually takes one to three weeks. Campaign sets with several variants and testing take longer. The biggest delays are usually waiting for content and approvals, not the build itself.

Do I need a full website if I have a landing page?

Not always at first, but most businesses need both eventually. A landing page is built for one offer and one action, such as booking a consultation from an ad. A full website answers many questions, builds trust, and ranks in search over time. Many businesses start with a landing page for a campaign and add a full website when they need services, about, pricing, and search visibility.

Can I build a landing page myself?

Yes, if your needs are simple and you have time to learn the tool. Builders like Squarespace, Wix, and WordPress with a landing page plugin can produce a clean one-page site. You will still need clear copy, a fast mobile layout, a working form, and tracking so you know whether the page is working. If the page will carry paid ad spend, a professional build often pays for itself by converting more of the clicks you already paid for.
