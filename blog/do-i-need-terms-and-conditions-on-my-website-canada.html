Do I need terms and conditions on my website? A Canadian guide

No Canadian law forces a simple business website to have terms and conditions. But the moment visitors buy, book, create an account, or post content on your site, terms stop being optional and start being your safety net. Here is when you need them, what to include, and how to make them enforceable in Canada.

The short answer

Do I need terms and conditions on my website? For a simple brochure-style site in Canada - a few pages describing your services, a phone number, a contact form - the honest answer is no, no law requires them. Terms and conditions, also called terms of use or terms of service, are not like a privacy policy, which PIPEDA effectively requires the moment you collect personal information. There is no general legal rule that says every website must publish terms.

But "not required" and "not worth having" are very different things. Terms and conditions are the rules for using your website, and they become genuinely valuable the moment your site does more than display information. If visitors can buy something, book an appointment, create an account, leave a review, upload a photo, or sign up for a paid service, you now have real conduct to govern and real liability to manage. That is when terms move from nice-to-have to something you should sort out before the first dispute, not after it.

One thing to say up front: this article is general information about Canadian law, not legal advice. If your business handles sensitive information, operates in a regulated profession, or sells across provinces, talk to a lawyer who works in Canadian business law before you finalize anything.

What terms and conditions actually are

Terms and conditions are a contract between your business and the people who use your website. They set the ground rules: what visitors can and cannot do on your site, who owns the content, what happens if something goes wrong, and which province's law applies if there is a dispute.

Think of them as the house rules for your online storefront. A physical shop does not need a posted list of rules to stop someone from shoplifting, but a website is different: visitors are interacting with your business from another province or another country, often without ever speaking to you, and the terms are the only record of what was agreed. They cover things like:

  • Acceptable use. Visitors may not misuse the site, scrape your content, post abusive material, or try to break into your systems.
  • Intellectual property. Your text, photos, logo, and design belong to you, and visitors may not copy or reuse them without permission.
  • Accuracy and disclaimers. You are not guaranteeing that every detail on the site is error-free, and you are not responsible for the content of external websites you link to.
  • Liability limits. If the site goes down, a price is listed incorrectly, or a link breaks, the terms set out the limits of what you are responsible for.
  • Governing law. Which province's law applies, and which courts handle disputes. For an Ontario business, that is usually Ontario law and Ontario courts.
  • Changes. That you may update the terms, and that continued use of the site means accepting the updated version.

For a simple informational website, these clauses are a reasonable protection but not urgent. For a site where users can do things, they become much more valuable, because now there is real conduct to govern and real liability to manage.

Terms and conditions vs privacy policy: two different jobs

Business owners often confuse the two, and website builders sometimes bundle them together, but they do different jobs:

  • A privacy policy explains what personal information you collect, why you collect it, and what you do with it. Under PIPEDA, the federal privacy law, this is effectively mandatory for any Canadian business website that collects personal information - and almost every site does, even if only through a contact form. Our guide to whether you need a privacy policy on your website covers that side in detail.
  • Terms and conditions govern how people use your site and what happens when things go wrong. They are about behaviour, ownership, and liability, not about personal information.

You need a privacy policy first, because the law expects it. Terms and conditions come next, because your risk position calls for them. A site with a privacy policy but no terms is common and usually fine for a brochure site. A site with terms but no privacy policy is backwards: the legally expected page is the one that is missing. If you are planning your site's pages generally, our guide to what pages a small business website should have shows where both pages fit.

When you definitely need terms and conditions

There is no single law that draws the line, but in practice the line is clear: if your website takes money, takes bookings, or takes user content, you need terms. Specifically:

  • You sell products or services online. This is the biggest one. Customers making a purchase should be able to find clear terms of sale, a refund and return policy, shipping information, and details of what they are actually buying before they pay. Consumer protection is largely provincial in Canada, and several provinces have specific rules about what online sellers must disclose and about a buyer's cancellation rights. Our guide to starting to sell online in Canada covers the practical side of setting up a store.
  • Visitors create accounts. Accounts mean passwords, personal profiles, and user data. Terms should cover what users are responsible for, what happens if an account is misused, and when you can suspend or close one.
  • Visitors post content. Reviews, comments, photos, forum posts, or uploaded files. Terms should say who owns the content, what you may do with it, and that you can remove anything that breaks the rules.
  • You take bookings or appointments. Cancellation and no-show policies, deposits, and rescheduling rules belong in your terms, not buried in a confirmation email. A clear cancellation clause is what protects a home-based bakery, a salon, or a trades business when a client cancels at the last minute.
  • You offer a paid service, membership, or subscription. Billing cycles, renewal, cancellation, and what happens when payment fails all need to be written down before the first charge, not after the first complaint.
  • You run a blog, community, or app. Any place where the public interacts with your business or each other needs rules, if only to give you a basis for removing abusive content.

If none of these apply and your site is purely informational, terms are a sensible precaution rather than a pressing need. But they cost very little to add, and the day you add a store or a booking form, they are already there.

What Canadian law actually says

Three pieces of Canadian law matter most for website terms:

There is no general legal requirement. No federal or provincial law forces an ordinary brochure-style business site to publish terms of service. What the law does require depends on what your site does: collecting personal information triggers PIPEDA's privacy-policy expectation, and selling to consumers online triggers provincial consumer protection rules.

Ontario's Consumer Protection Act, 2002 applies to online sales. If you sell to Ontario consumers online, sections 37 to 39 of the Act require specific disclosures before an internet agreement is made, and a copy of the agreement afterwards, where the consumer's total potential payment is more than $50. That means your checkout flow has to show the price, the total cost, delivery details, and the terms before the customer pays, and the customer has cancellation rights if those disclosures are missing. Other provinces have their own consumer protection rules, so if you sell across Canada, the stricter standard is the safe one to meet.

Electronic contracts are enforceable in Ontario. Section 19 of Ontario's Electronic Commerce Act, 2000 recognizes that contracts can be formed electronically, including by clicking "I agree". Canadian courts have backed this up: in Rudder v. Microsoft Corp. (1999), an Ontario court upheld a clickwrap agreement, confirming that users who click "I agree" are bound by the terms. In Kanitz v. Rogers Cable Inc. (2002), the court upheld changes to online terms where users had notice and an opportunity to accept or reject them. The lesson from both cases is the same: terms are enforceable when users can see them and clearly accept them, and they are vulnerable when users never had a real chance to notice them.

What to include in your terms and conditions

A solid set of terms for a Canadian small business website usually covers these sections. Go through them one by one and describe what your site really does. If a section does not apply to you, leave it out rather than copying generic text:

  • Who the terms apply to. Your business name, and that using the site means accepting the terms.
  • Acceptable use. What visitors may and may not do: no scraping, no spam, no abusive content, no attempts to interfere with the site.
  • Intellectual property. Your content, branding, and design belong to you and may not be copied or reused without permission.
  • Products, pricing, and orders. Prices are in Canadian dollars, taxes are extra where applicable, prices can change, and an order is only accepted once you confirm it. This matters more than most owners realize: a pricing error on a live site is far easier to handle when the terms say prices are subject to correction.
  • Payment, refunds, and returns. How payment is taken, when a refund is available, how returns work, and any restocking or cancellation fees. For services, this is where deposits and cancellation deadlines live.
  • Bookings and appointments. Cancellation windows, no-show policies, and what happens to deposits.
  • User accounts and content. What users are responsible for, who owns posted content, and your right to remove content or close accounts that break the rules.
  • Disclaimers. That the site is provided as-is, that you do not guarantee it will be error-free or always available, and that you are not responsible for third-party websites you link to.
  • Limitation of liability. A reasonable cap on what you are responsible for if something goes wrong.
  • Governing law and disputes. That the terms are governed by the laws of your province (for example, Ontario) and that disputes go to the courts there.
  • Changes to the terms. That you may update them, and that continued use of the site after an update means accepting the new version.
  • Contact information. How someone reaches you with questions about the terms.

One practical tip: write the terms in the same plain language as the rest of your site. A refund clause a customer can actually understand prevents more disputes than a dense paragraph nobody reads.

How to make your terms enforceable: clickwrap beats browsewrap

Having terms on your site is only half the job. The other half is making sure a court would actually hold a user to them, and that comes down to how users accept them. There are two common approaches, and they are not equal:

  • Clickwrap: the user actively agrees. A checkbox or an "I agree" button next to the sign-up, checkout, or booking form, with a clear link to the full terms. This is the stronger approach, and it is the one Canadian courts have upheld. Have users actively accept the terms, with a checkbox or a clear "by clicking, you agree" statement next to the sign-up button, and keep a record of each acceptance.
  • Browsewrap: the terms sit in the footer. A link in the site footer that says "by using this site you agree to our terms". This is weaker, because a visitor can use the whole site without ever seeing the link. It is fine as a baseline for a brochure site, but it is not enough on its own for a store or a service where money changes hands.

The practical rule: the more a visitor does on your site, the more visible the acceptance has to be. A brochure site can rely on a footer link. A checkout page should require an active click before payment. A sign-up form should show the checkbox before the account is created. And whatever you do, keep a record: which version of the terms was live, when the user accepted, and what they clicked. That record is what turns your terms from a page on your site into evidence of an agreement.

How to get terms and conditions without overpaying

You have three realistic options, in increasing order of cost and confidence:

  • Write them yourself from a Canadian template. Several Canadian legal publishers sell terms-of-use templates written for Canadian law at modest prices, and some free ones exist. This works well for simple sites, but read every line and delete what does not match your business. A template that promises things you do not do, or describes a store you do not run, is worse than useless.
  • Use a terms generator. Online generators ask questions about your site and produce a customized set of terms. They are better than a blind template because the answers force you to inventory what your site actually does. Prefer generators that know Canadian law rather than US-only ones, and review the output carefully - a generator cannot know your refund policy or your cancellation rules unless you tell it.
  • Have a lawyer draft or review them. Worth it if you run an online store with customer accounts, handle sensitive information, operate in a regulated field, or sell across provincial rules. A one-time review of terms you drafted yourself is often the sweet spot for a small business budget: you do the inventory, the lawyer checks the wording and the enforceability.

Whatever route you take, three habits keep the terms honest over time: link them in the footer of every page, revisit them whenever you add a new feature to your site (a new store means new sale terms, a new booking tool means new cancellation rules), and note the "last updated" date when you change them. Terms written in 2026 that describe the site you had in 2023 are a gap waiting to become a dispute.

Common mistakes small business websites make

  • No terms at all on a site that takes money. The most common mistake, and the one that hurts most when a refund dispute or a chargeback arrives.
  • Terms that describe someone else's business. Copied terms that mention services you never offered, jurisdictions you do not operate in, or contact details of another company. Visitors notice, and so do courts.
  • Burying the terms. Terms that take three clicks to find might as well not exist. Footer link, every page, done - and an active acceptance step at checkout or sign-up.
  • Using US-only templates. Terms written for US law reference the wrong statutes, the wrong consumer rights, and the wrong governing law. Canadian consumer protection, PIPEDA, and CASL are different, and your terms should reflect that.
  • Forgetting the refund and cancellation policy. Many disputes are not about the terms at all - they are about a refund or a cancelled booking that was never written down anywhere. Put it in the terms and show it at checkout.
  • Never updating them. The site gains a store, a booking system, or a membership area, and the terms still describe a five-page brochure site. Review the terms every time the site changes.

FAQ

Are terms and conditions legally required for a website in Canada?

No. There is no general Canadian law that requires a website to have terms and conditions. But other laws create requirements that terms help you meet: PIPEDA expects a privacy policy when you collect personal information, and provincial consumer protection laws, like Ontario's Consumer Protection Act, require specific disclosures before an online sale. Terms are how you put those disclosures in one clear place.

Can I just copy terms and conditions from another website?

Copying is risky for two reasons. First, their terms describe their business, their products, and their risks - not yours, and terms that do not match your site are hard to enforce. Second, website text is copyrighted, so copying another business's terms can itself create a legal problem. Use a proper Canadian template or generator as a starting point, then customize every clause to match what your site actually does.

Do terms and conditions need to be signed to be enforceable?

No. Website terms do not need a handwritten signature. Canadian courts have upheld terms accepted by clicking "I agree" - the clickwrap approach - as long as the user had a real chance to see the terms and clearly accepted them. What matters is notice and acceptance, not ink on paper.

What is the difference between terms of service, terms of use, and terms and conditions?

Almost nothing in practice. Terms of service, terms of use, and terms and conditions are different names for the same document: the rules for using a website. "Terms of service" is common for apps and platforms, "terms of use" for informational sites, and "terms and conditions" for online stores. Pick one name, use it consistently, and make sure the content matches what your site actually does.
