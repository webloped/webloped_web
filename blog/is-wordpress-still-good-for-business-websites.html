Is WordPress still good for business websites in 2026?

Between AI website builders, hosted platforms, and a decade of "WordPress is dead" headlines, business owners keep asking whether WordPress is still a sensible choice. The honest answer: yes, but only for the right reasons. Here is where it still wins, where it genuinely struggles, and what it really costs to run one in Canada.

WordPress has been declared dead more times than most websites have been redesigned. Yet two decades in, it still powers roughly 43% of all websites on the internet, according to long-running surveys by W3Techs. That is not a dying platform. But the question business owners actually ask is narrower: is WordPress still good for a business website in 2026, when Wix will build your site for $20 a month and an AI builder will generate one in an afternoon?

This article gives the straight answer we give our own clients in Cambridge and across Ontario. WordPress is still an excellent choice for many businesses, but the reasons to choose it have changed. It is no longer the easy default. It is a deliberate choice that trades convenience for ownership, and that trade is worth it for some businesses and a bad deal for others.

The short answer

Yes, WordPress is still good for business websites in 2026, provided it is set up properly and actually maintained. A well-built WordPress site on quality hosting is fast, secure, fully under your ownership, and flexible enough to grow from a five-page brochure site into a full ecommerce store without switching platforms.

But a poorly set-up WordPress site is also one of the most common sources of slow, hacked, and abandoned business websites on the internet. WordPress gives you freedom, and freedom includes the freedom to build a mess. The platform is not the problem in those stories; the setup and the upkeep are. If nobody on your team will ever update anything, a hosted platform that handles maintenance for you is the smarter buy. That is the entire decision, really: pick the platform whose upkeep you can actually cover.

Where WordPress still wins

You own everything

This is WordPress's biggest, least appreciated advantage. Your WordPress site is files in a hosting account you control. You can move it to any host in the world, hand it to any developer, and nobody can shut it down because you stopped paying a platform subscription. With Wix, Squarespace, or Shopify, you are renting: stop paying, and your website ceases to exist. For a business that expects to exist for decades, owning your digital storefront outright matters. This is the number one reason we recommend WordPress to businesses that plan to grow.

It grows without a platform switch

Most businesses do not stay the same size. The five-page service site you need today might need online booking next year, an ecommerce section the year after, and a customer portal after that. On WordPress, that growth happens on the same platform: add WooCommerce for selling, add a booking plugin, add a membership system. On a hosted builder, growth often means hitting the ceiling of what the platform allows and then paying for a full rebuild on something more flexible. Choosing WordPress up front is buying yourself the right to grow without starting over. Our comparison of website builders versus developers walks through exactly where the DIY ceiling sits.

Full SEO control

WordPress gives you complete control over the technical SEO details that hosted platforms abstract away: URL structure, schema markup, redirect management, sitemap control, caching, and the ability to fix any technical issue a developer identifies. To be clear, Google has said there is no fundamental SEO advantage to one platform over another - a well-built Wix site can outrank a badly-built WordPress site. But when something needs fixing, WordPress lets you fix anything. On closed platforms, some things simply cannot be changed, no matter who you hire. If you are investing in SEO, our guide on getting your business on Google covers the foundations that matter.

The plugin ecosystem is unmatched

Need bookings, multilingual content, an events calendar, a quote calculator, or integration with a specific Canadian payment processor? There is almost certainly a well-maintained plugin for it, battle-tested by thousands of businesses. No other platform offers the same depth of options, and most of the good ones are free or cost less than a takeout dinner per month. This is how small businesses get enterprise-style features at small-business prices.

Where WordPress genuinely struggles in 2026

An honest assessment means admitting the weaknesses too. WordPress's problems are real, and they are the reason plenty of businesses are better off elsewhere.

Maintenance is not optional

This is the single biggest reason WordPress sites go bad. The core software, themes, and plugins need regular updates: security patches arrive constantly, and an unpatched site is a hacked site waiting to happen. Hosted platforms push updates silently in the background; with self-hosted WordPress, someone has to click the button, and someone has to check that nothing broke afterward. Most hacked WordPress sites we hear about were not attacked because WordPress is insecure. They were attacked because nobody updated them for two years. Our Canadian website maintenance guide breaks down what proper upkeep costs and includes.

Performance takes deliberate effort

A fresh WordPress install with a lightweight theme is fast. A real-world WordPress site with a heavy premium theme, a page builder, and twenty plugins is often slow, and speed affects both rankings and conversions. Hosted builders tend to pass Google's Core Web Vitals out of the box because the platform optimises everything centrally; WordPress passes them when someone competent configures caching, image optimisation, and a lean theme. Our article on why websites are so slow explains the usual culprits, and on WordPress the culprits are almost always theme bloat and plugin sprawl.

Plugin sprawl is a real risk

The plugin ecosystem is a strength and a hazard at the same time. Every plugin is code running on your site: code that can slow things down, conflict with other plugins, get abandoned by its developer, or introduce security vulnerabilities. Security reports consistently show that the overwhelming majority of WordPress vulnerabilities are found in plugins, not in WordPress itself. The discipline of a good WordPress build is using as few plugins as possible, only from reputable developers, and removing anything that is not actively doing a job. If that kind of discipline does not sound like something your business will maintain, say so now.

The learning curve is real

The WordPress dashboard is more complex than a hosted builder's editor, and a self-hosted site asks you to think about hosting, domains, SSL, backups, and updates. None of this is hard, but all of it is yours. For an owner who just wants to change business hours and add a photo now and then, a simpler platform is genuinely the better tool.

What does a WordPress site actually cost in Canada?

WordPress software is free and open source. Running a WordPress business site is not free. Here are realistic 2026 Canadian numbers for a typical small-business site:

  • Domain: $15 - $25 CAD per year for a .ca domain.
  • Hosting: $10 - $40 CAD per month for decent shared or entry managed WordPress hosting from a reputable Canadian or international host. The $3-a-month bargain hosts are where slow WordPress sites are born; do not build a business on them.
  • Premium theme or builder: $0 - $150 CAD per year. A good free theme is fine; premium themes typically run $60 - $100 USD per year.
  • Plugins: $0 - $300 CAD per year. Most small-business sites can run on free plugins; premium ones (forms, security, SEO pro versions) add up.
  • Build cost: the same as any other professional site. See our breakdown of how much a website costs in Canada - the platform does not change the design and development labour much.
  • Ongoing maintenance: $50 - $200 CAD per month for updates, backups, monitoring, and small fixes, whether you pay a plan or value your own time. Skipping this is not saving money; it is deferring a much bigger bill.

Add it up and a WordPress business site typically costs $500 - $1,000 CAD per year in running costs after the build, plus maintenance time or a maintenance plan. That is very reasonable, but it is not the "free" that the word free suggests. Anyone quoting you a WordPress site with zero ongoing cost is either planning to maintain it for free forever or planning for you to learn the hard way.

WordPress.org versus WordPress.com: which one are we talking about?

Half the confusion in this debate comes from there being two WordPresses. WordPress.org is the free, self-hosted software: you install it on your own hosting, you own everything, you maintain everything. This is what most people mean by "WordPress", and everything in this article refers to it unless stated otherwise.

WordPress.com is a commercial hosted service by Automattic: closer to Wix or Squarespace, where hosting, updates, and security are bundled into a subscription. It is simpler and lower-maintenance, but you give up the full ownership and flexibility that make WordPress attractive, and the good features (plugins, themes, custom domains without ads) require the higher-priced plans. If you want WordPress for its freedom, you want WordPress.org. If you want simplicity, compare WordPress.com against the other hosted builders on equal terms.

When to choose WordPress, and when to skip it

Choose WordPress when: you want to own your site outright; you expect the site to grow significantly (booking, ecommerce, memberships, multiple languages); you are investing in SEO and want full technical control; you publish content regularly and want the best publishing tools; or you have someone - a developer, a maintenance plan, or your own time - who will keep it updated.

Skip WordPress when: you want a website you never have to think about; the site will always be simple (a few pages, contact form, done); you have no one to handle updates and will not pay for a maintenance plan; or speed-to-launch matters more than long-term flexibility. In those cases a hosted builder is the honest recommendation, and any developer who tells you otherwise is selling you what they sell, not what you need.

FAQ

Is WordPress free?

The WordPress software is free and open source. A business website built on it still costs money to run: hosting ($10 - $40/month CAD), a .ca domain ($15 - $25/year), and typically some premium plugins or theme costs, plus either your own time or a maintenance plan for updates and backups. Budget roughly $500 - $1,000 CAD per year in running costs for a small-business site, before the cost of building it.

Is WordPress secure enough for a business website?

Yes, when it is maintained. WordPress core is well-maintained and actively patched; the vast majority of hacked WordPress sites were running outdated plugins or themes, not a flaw in WordPress itself. The formula is simple and non-negotiable: quality hosting, a reputable theme, a small number of well-maintained plugins, regular updates, automated backups, and strong admin passwords. Do all of that and a WordPress site is as secure as anything else a small business would reasonably run.

Is WordPress good for SEO?

Yes. WordPress is one of the most SEO-friendly platforms available because it gives you full control over the technical details: URLs, redirects, schema markup, sitemaps, and speed optimisation. Google has said the platform itself does not inherently rank better or worse than alternatives; what matters is how the site is built. A well-built WordPress site with proper SEO plugins and a fast theme is an excellent SEO foundation. A bloated WordPress site with a page-builder theme and 30 plugins is not.

Should I use Elementor or Gutenberg for my business site?

For most small-business sites, the built-in block editor (Gutenberg) with a lightweight theme is now the better choice: it is faster, simpler, and produces cleaner code. Heavy page builders like Elementor make elaborate designs easy but add significant page weight and lock your layouts into their system, which makes future changes harder. If you need complex custom layouts that Gutenberg cannot produce cleanly, a custom theme from a developer will outperform both options.
