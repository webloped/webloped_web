How long does it take to build a website in 2026?

The honest answer up front: a typical small business website takes two to six weeks from kickoff to launch. Here is where that time goes, what makes projects run late, and how to keep yours on schedule.

You have probably seen the ads: "Launch your website this weekend." "Go live in 24 hours." Those timelines are real, but they describe something very specific - a pre-built template with your name and logo dropped in, usually with stock photos and placeholder text you are expected to "fill in later."

A website that is designed for your business, written for your customers, and built to actually bring in work takes longer. Not because developers are slow, but because the work has distinct stages and several of them depend on you. This guide breaks down where the weeks go, so you can plan around the real thing instead of the ad copy.

The one distinction that explains almost everything: build time is not the same as calendar time. The hands-on work on a typical small business site might total a week or two of effort. The calendar time - from the day you hire someone to the day the site goes live - is longer because feedback rounds, content gathering, and your own schedule all sit in between. When a developer quotes you a timeline, they are quoting calendar time.

Typical timelines by website type

The ranges below are what you will commonly see quoted across the market - they vary by developer and scope, so treat them as orientation, not promises from us:

  • One-page or landing page (1 page): a few days to 1 week. There is simply less to design, write, and test.
  • Simple business site (up to 5 pages): 1 to 2 weeks. Home, About, Services, Contact, maybe a simple blog - straightforward and template-friendly.
  • Standard small business site (5-15 pages): 2 to 4 weeks. This is where most Canadian small businesses land: service pages, a quote or contact form, brand-matched design, and basic on-page SEO.
  • Custom-designed small business site (5-15 pages): 4 to 8 weeks. Custom design adds design rounds, and custom features (booking, quote calculators, CRM integrations) add build and testing time. Industry surveys of web development firms in 2026 consistently land in this 4-to-8-week band for mid-sized projects.
  • E-commerce store: 6 to 8 weeks or more. Product catalogs, checkout, payment gateways, taxes, shipping rules, and security testing are a different league of complexity than a brochure site.
  • Custom web applications: 10 to 16 weeks and up. Logins, dashboards, and databases are software projects, not website projects, and should be planned as such.

The six stages and where the time goes

Almost every professional build follows the same six stages. Understanding them is the best defense against both unrealistic promises and projects that drift:

1. Discovery and planning (1-2 weeks)

This is where a good developer asks you a lot of questions: what you sell, who the site is for, what action a visitor should take, which competitors you admire, and what "done" looks like. Skipping or rushing this stage is where most failed projects go wrong - the site gets built for the wrong audience or the wrong goal, and the fixes happen late, expensively.

2. Design (1-3 weeks)

The look and feel of the key pages gets created and shared for your approval before any code is written. This is your chance to react to the actual design, not to a description of it. Most projects include one or two revision rounds here. A stage that should never be skipped: approving design on paper (or screen) is cheap; redesigning after development is not.

3. Content gathering (runs in parallel, often 1-3 weeks)

Your text, photos, logo files, and any documents the site needs. This stage runs alongside design and development, and it is the single most common source of delay. Designers and developers can work around a lot, but they cannot invent your service descriptions, your team photos, or your pricing. More on this below.

4. Development (2-4 weeks)

Design becomes a working site: pages are built, forms are wired up, integrations are connected, and the site is made to work on phones, tablets, and desktops. The more custom functionality - booking systems, payment flows, AI automation - the longer this stage runs, because each feature needs building and testing.

5. Review, testing, and revisions (1-2 weeks)

You click through everything as a visitor would. The developer tests across browsers and screen sizes, checks forms actually deliver messages, measures load speed, and fixes what you both find. Rushing this stage creates problems you inherit after launch, so a good developer will not skip it - and neither should you.

6. Launch and post-launch (a few days, then ongoing)

DNS gets pointed, SSL is set up, analytics are installed, and the site goes live. What follows is not a stage so much as a reality: a website is never truly "done." Search engines need weeks to index it, content needs updating, and small fixes appear. Ask what is included after launch before you sign - a warranty period for bugs, and who handles updates.

What actually makes projects run late

In our experience watching projects (ours and others), the build itself is rarely the bottleneck. The delays almost always come from one of these:

  • Content is not ready. The number-one delay, by a wide margin. The developer is waiting on your service descriptions, your bio, your photos. Every week content waits, the launch moves a week. If you want one lever that controls your timeline, it is this: have your copy and images ready before development starts.
  • Too many approvers. Two people reviewing is fine. Six people, three of whom join halfway through, is how a two-week design stage becomes a two-month one. Name one decision-maker before the project starts.
  • Scope creep. "While you are at it, can we add a booking system?" Mid-project additions need re-scoping, re-design, and re-testing. Keep a "phase two" list for everything that is not essential to launch, and add it after.
  • Feedback in batches. Sending one long consolidated round of feedback keeps things moving. Sending comments one by one, over days, restarts the developer's context every time.
  • E-commerce specifics. Online stores have a hidden timeline killer: product data. Photographing products, writing descriptions, setting prices, configuring taxes and shipping for Canada (GST/HST/PST by province) - for a 50-product store, data entry alone can take longer than the build.

The pattern: the stages you control - content, decisions, approvals - determine your timeline more than the stages the developer controls.

How to speed things up without cutting corners

If you are working against a deadline, these are the moves that genuinely compress a timeline without producing a worse website:

  1. Prepare your content first. Write your page text (or at least rough drafts) and gather photos and your logo before the kickoff call. This alone can shave weeks off the calendar.
  2. Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves. Write the non-negotiables down - pages, forms, checkout, booking - and agree that everything else waits for phase two. A focused scope is a fast scope.
  3. Pick three example sites you like. Sending examples of websites you admire, with one sentence on what you like about each, prevents the most expensive kind of miscommunication: building something beautiful that you hate.
  4. Name one decision-maker. One person approves design, one person approves content. Committees do not ship websites on time.
  5. Give feedback fast and in batches. Set aside time for review rounds when the project starts, not when the developer asks. A 48-hour turnaround on feedback keeps the schedule tight.
  6. Choose a developer with a defined process. Vague timelines usually mean a vague process. Ask how they run projects - stages, milestones, how revisions work - and get the timeline in writing as part of the quote.

And the red flags, since they matter when you are in a hurry: anyone who promises a full custom site in 48 hours is selling you a template; anyone who cannot tell you what happens after launch is not planning for it; and "we will figure out the content later" is how a four-week project becomes a four-month one.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a website built in a week?

For a simple site, yes - but only if everything is ready: final copy, photos, logo, and one person who approves quickly. What most one-week builds actually are is a template-based site. A custom-designed small business site realistically takes two to six weeks. If a deadline is real (a launch event, a seasonal business), say so up front - a good developer can phase the work so the essential pages go live first.

What takes longer to build, an online store or a booking website?

An online store, almost always. A store means a product catalog, checkout, payment gateways, Canadian tax and shipping configuration, and security testing - typically six to eight weeks or more, with product data entry as the hidden time sink. A booking website (services, calendar, confirmations, reminders) is simpler and usually fits the standard small-business timeline of two to six weeks.

Why does my developer keep asking me for content?

Because waiting for content is the most common reason website projects run late. Design and development can be scheduled; your text, photos, and decisions cannot be made by anyone else. A project where the content is ready on day one almost always finishes on schedule. If writing is the blocker, ask your developer whether copywriting help is available - many offer it as part of the project.

Does a website redesign take as long as building from scratch?

Often about the same, sometimes longer. A redesign reuses your existing content, which helps, but it adds a migration stage: redirecting old URLs, preserving search rankings, and untangling years of accumulated plugins or outdated code. A redesign of a typical 10-page small business site usually lands in the same two-to-six-week range as a new build.
