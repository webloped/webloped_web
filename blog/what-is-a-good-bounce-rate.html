What is a good bounce rate for a small business website?

You open Google Analytics, see a bounce rate of 62%, and wonder if your website is broken. Probably not. For most small business websites, 40 to 55% is normal, and some pages are supposed to bounce. This guide explains what the number actually means in 2026, what counts as good for your type of business, and the fixes that actually move it.

The short answer

What is a good bounce rate for a small business website? For a service business, such as a plumber, electrician, accountant, or agency, 40 to 55% is normal. Under 40% is strong. Between 55 and 70% is worth investigating. Over 70% on a key page, like your homepage or a service page, deserves a closer look. But those ranges only make sense with context: a blog post that fully answers a question can bounce at 80% and be doing its job perfectly, while a checkout page bouncing at 60% is a real problem.

The single most useful comparison is not against a benchmark at all. It is against your own site last month. If your homepage bounced at 52% last month and 74% this month, something changed, and that is the signal worth chasing. Benchmarks tell you whether you are in a normal range. Trends tell you whether something broke.

What bounce rate actually means in 2026

Bounce rate is the percentage of visits where someone lands on your website and leaves without engaging with it. The important detail is that the definition changed. The old version of Google Analytics, Universal Analytics, counted a bounce as any visit that viewed only one page. Under that definition, a visitor who read your entire 2,000-word guide and left satisfied was still a bounce.

Google Analytics 4 (GA4), the current version, fixed that. A visit now counts as a bounce only if it fails all three engagement tests:

  • It lasted less than 10 seconds
  • It viewed only one page
  • It triggered no key event, such as a form submission, phone click, or purchase

In other words, bounce rate in GA4 is simply the opposite of engagement rate: bounce rate = 100% minus engagement rate. A visitor who spends three minutes reading one blog post and leaves is now counted as engaged, not as a bounce. That is why GA4 bounce rates usually look lower than the numbers in older guides, and why most published benchmarks, which were built on the old definition, run higher than what you will see in your own GA4 reports.

If you do not have Analytics set up yet, our guide on how to set up Google Analytics walks through the whole thing in under an hour, including marking phone clicks and form submissions as key events. That last part matters more than most owners realise, and we will come back to it below.

Good bounce rate benchmarks by page type

There is no single good number, because different pages have different jobs. The ranges below are widely cited across analytics studies and are best treated as orientation, not targets:

  • Ecommerce and online stores: 20 to 45%. Shoppers browse several products and categories, so a low bounce is normal. A store bouncing at 70% is losing shoppers fast.
  • Service business homepages and service pages: 40 to 55%. Visitors compare services and prices before contacting you. This is the range most Canadian small businesses should expect.
  • Lead generation and landing pages: 30 to 55% for multi-step pages, 60 to 90% for single-purpose pages. A landing page with one offer and one button is designed to be a single view. A high bounce there is often the design working, not failing.
  • Blog posts and informational articles: 60 to 80%. Readers arrive from Google, get their answer, and leave. That is a successful visit, even though GA4 may still count some of them as bounces if they read in under 10 seconds, which almost never happens for a full article.
  • Contact pages, hours pages, and location pages: 60 to 85%. The visitor came for a phone number or an address, found it, and left. Job done.

A useful rough scale for the pages that matter most to your business: under 40% is strong, 40 to 55% is average, 55 to 70% is worth investigating, and over 70% deserves a closer look. The exception is any page whose whole job is answered in one view, such as a blog post, a contact page, or a landing page. Judge those pages on whether visitors did the thing they came for, not on whether they clicked a second page.

Why your number may not match the benchmarks

Before you panic about a number, check these four things. Each one can move your bounce rate by 10 or 20 points on its own, and none of them means your website is broken.

Traffic source. Visitors behave very differently depending on where they came from. Direct visitors and Google search visitors usually bounce least, because they came with intent. Social media visitors bounce far more: someone tapping a link from Instagram is browsing, not buying. In one typical small business account, direct visitors bounce around 27% while Instagram visitors bounce near 57%. If you recently ran a social campaign, your overall bounce rate will climb, and that is expected.

Device. Mobile visitors almost always bounce more than desktop visitors. A phone screen is smaller, attention is shorter, and a slow mobile page is punished hardest. If your mobile bounce rate is 20 points higher than desktop, the fix is usually mobile speed and layout, not your content. Our guide on why websites get slow covers the usual causes, and most of them hit mobile first.

Page intent. A visitor who searched "emergency plumber near me" and landed on your contact page is one tap from calling you. They may never view a second page, and that is a win. A visitor who searched "how much does a kitchen renovation cost" and landed on a 3,000-word guide may read for five minutes and leave. Also a win. Both count as bounces under some definitions, and neither is a problem.

Missing key events. This is the most common measurement mistake we see on Canadian small business sites. If phone number clicks and form submissions are not marked as key events in GA4, a visitor who calls you from your homepage after 20 seconds still counts as a bounce, because GA4 saw one page, no key event, and a short visit. That inflates your bounce rate and hides your best traffic. Setting up key events takes about five minutes, and our Google Analytics setup guide shows exactly where to do it.

Does bounce rate affect SEO?

Google has not confirmed that bounce rate is a direct ranking signal, and Google Analytics data is not directly used by Google's ranking systems. You will not drop in search results just because your bounce rate is 65%.

That said, a high bounce rate is often a symptom of problems that do affect rankings and enquiries. If visitors leave your service page in under 10 seconds, the page is probably slow, hard to read on a phone, or mismatched to what they searched for. Slow pages and poor mobile experience are ranking factors. Content that does not match search intent does not rank well either. So treat bounce rate as a warning light, not as the engine problem itself. Fix the page, and the number usually follows.

One more thing to check: if a page suddenly starts bouncing much more than it used to, look at what changed. A new pop-up, a video that autoplays, a form that broke, or a page that started loading slowly after a plugin update can all push visitors away in seconds. The trend matters more than the absolute number.

How to find your bounce rate in GA4

Open Google Analytics and go to Reports, then Engagement, then Pages and screens. This shows every page on your site with its views, average engagement time, and bounce rate. Sort by bounce rate to find your worst pages, then filter to the pages that matter: your homepage, your service pages, and any page you send paid traffic to.

To see bounce rate by traffic source, go to Reports, then Acquisition, then Traffic acquisition. This shows how each channel performs. If one source bounces at 80% while the rest sit at 45%, the problem is probably the traffic, not the page: the wrong audience, a misleading ad, or a link that promises something the page does not deliver.

To compare mobile and desktop, add a device comparison in the same report, or look at Reports, then Tech, then Tech details. A big mobile-desktop gap is a strong hint that your mobile experience needs work.

Check monthly, not daily. Daily bounce rates jump around for meaningless reasons, such as one social post going mildly viral. Monthly numbers show real trends. Record the number for your top five pages each month in a simple spreadsheet. After three months you have a baseline, and you can tell whether a change you made actually helped.

8 fixes that actually lower bounce rate

Work through these in order. The first three fix most high bounce rates on small business sites. The rest are refinements.

1. Speed up the page. If your site takes more than three seconds to load, a large share of visitors leave before they see anything at all. Compress images, remove unused plugins, and test with Google's free PageSpeed Insights tool. Our guide on how to make your website load faster walks through the biggest wins first.

2. Match the page to the search. If someone searches "accountant for small business Toronto" and lands on your generic homepage, they bounce. If they land on a page titled exactly that, with Toronto pricing and a clear next step, they stay. Every ad and every important search phrase deserves a page that answers it directly.

3. Put a clear next step above the fold. Visitors decide in seconds whether to stay. The top of the page should say what you do, who it is for, and what to do next: call, get a quote, book a consultation. If the first screen is a vague slogan and a stock photo, people leave. Our guide on writing website copy that converts covers this in detail.

4. Mark phone clicks and form submissions as key events. As above, this does not change visitor behaviour, but it stops your best visitors from being counted as bounces. It also gives you a real conversion number to watch instead of a vanity bounce rate.

5. Make the page readable on a phone. Text that is too small, buttons that are too close together, and layouts that force sideways scrolling all push mobile visitors away. Test every key page on a real phone, not just by shrinking your desktop browser.

6. Add internal links where the reader naturally wants more. A visitor reading about your services should see a link to pricing, to a related case, or to your contact page, right where the question occurs to them. Internal links are also good for SEO, so this is a two-for-one fix. If you want more visitors to become enquiries, our guide on how to get more leads from your website covers the full playbook.

7. Kill the pop-up, or delay it. A pop-up that appears two seconds after landing is the fastest way to create a bounce. If you use one, delay it until the visitor has been on the page for at least 30 seconds or scrolled halfway down, and make it easy to close.

8. Check the traffic, not just the page. If one source bounces at 80% and the rest do not, the audience is wrong. Check what the ad or social post promised, and whether the landing page delivers it. Fixing the message is cheaper than redesigning the page.

When a high bounce rate is fine

Not every high bounce rate needs fixing. Leave it alone when:

  • The page answers a single question, such as your hours, your address, or a price list, and visitors leave with what they came for.
  • It is a blog post that people read for several minutes. Check average engagement time, not bounce rate, for these pages.
  • It is a landing page with one offer and one action, and the conversion rate on that action is healthy.
  • Most of the traffic comes from social media or a viral post. Those visitors were always going to bounce more.

The question to ask about any page is not "is the bounce rate low?" but "did the visitor do the thing this page exists for?" If the answer is yes, the bounce rate is a footnote. If the answer is no, work through the eight fixes above, starting with speed and search intent.

What is a good bounce rate for a small business website?

Blog posts, landing pages, and contact pages routinely run 60 to 90% without anything being wrong, because their job is often finished in a single view.

Why is my bounce rate different from what other guides say?

How do I lower my bounce rate?

Start with the page, not the metric: speed up loading, make sure the page matches what the visitor searched for, put a clear next step above the fold, and mark phone clicks and form submissions as key events so engaged visitors are counted properly. Then check bounce rate by page, traffic source, and device, and fix the worst combination first.
