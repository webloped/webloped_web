Landing page vs website: which does your small business need?

A landing page asks for one action. A website answers many questions. Picking the wrong one wastes ad money or leaves customers guessing, so here is how to choose.

Imagine a physiotherapy clinic in Hamilton that is about to run its first Google Ads campaign for back pain treatment. The owner has a homepage, a services page, an about page, and a blog, and the obvious move seems to be sending every ad click to the homepage. That is also the move that wastes the most money. A person who searched for back pain treatment lands on a general homepage, has to work out where to click next, gets distracted by unrelated services, and leaves without booking. A focused page that speaks only to back pain, explains the first visit, shows the location and hours, and offers one clear booking step would have given that same visitor a much shorter path to yes.

Now imagine the opposite problem. A new bakery in Ottawa launches with a single beautiful page: a photo, a short description, and an order button. It works well for friends who already know the bakery and click a link from Instagram. But a stranger searching for a wedding cake baker in Ottawa finds almost nothing to trust or compare: no menu, no flavours, no prices or ordering details, no reviews, no story, and no other pages for Google to rank. The single page is doing a job it was never built for, and the business looks smaller and less established than it is.

Both owners are asking the same question, and it is one of the most common questions in the landing page vs website debate: do I need a full website, or is a landing page enough? The honest answer is that they are different tools. A landing page is built to convert one kind of visitor on one offer. A website is built to be found, trusted, and explored over time. Most growing businesses eventually need both, but which one you build first depends on what your business needs right now.

The short answer

In the landing page vs website decision, choose a landing page when you want visitors to take one action right now, such as booking a consultation, signing up for a waitlist, or claiming a specific offer, and you already have a way to send people to it, such as ads, social media, or an email list. Choose a full website when you need to build trust, rank on Google for many different searches, and serve many kinds of visitors, such as new customers, returning customers, and people checking your credentials before they call.

If you are starting from nothing and you need customers this month, a strong single page with a clear offer can be the right first step. If you want customers to find you on Google month after month without paying for every click, you need a website with real pages behind it. The rest of this guide explains the difference in practical terms, so you can spend on the right thing first and add the other when it earns its place.

What is a landing page?

A landing page is a single page built around one goal and one audience. It usually has no main navigation menu, or only a minimal one, because every link that leads away from the page is a chance for the visitor to leave without taking the action you wanted. The headline matches the ad, post, or email that sent the visitor there. The copy stays focused on one problem and one offer. The page ends, and repeats along the way, with one call to action: book, call, sign up, or buy.

That focus is the whole point. A visitor who clicks an ad for snow removal in Mississauga should not land on a page about lawn care, tree trimming, and company history. They should land on a page about snow removal in Mississauga, with the service area, the response time, what is included, and a quote form. When the page matches the promise that earned the click, the visitor does not have to think. Thinking is where conversions are lost.

Landing pages are also built to be measured. Because there is only one goal, you can tell clearly whether the page works: how many visitors booked, signed up, or called. You can test one headline against another, or one offer against another, and see the result in days rather than months. That is why marketers love them for campaigns, and why a business running paid ads without a landing page is often paying for clicks and then throwing away the value of those clicks on a general homepage.

What is a website?

A website is your business's full online home: several pages connected by a menu, each answering a different question. A typical Canadian small business website has a homepage, service or product pages, an about page, a contact page, and often a blog or a reviews section. Each page can target a different search, answer a different concern, and serve a different kind of visitor.

A website does jobs a landing page cannot do well. It builds trust, because a visitor can check who you are, what you have done, where you are located, and how to reach you without taking your word for it on a single page. It ranks on Google, because each page gives Google another relevant page to show for a different search, and a blog gives you new pages over time. It serves different visitors at once: a new prospect comparing options, a returning customer looking for hours or a phone number, a job applicant, and a supplier all find what they need without being forced down one path.

The trade-off is focus. A website gives visitors choices, and choices can dilute a campaign. Someone who clicked an ad for one specific offer does not need your full menu of pages. They need the offer. That is why the answer for most established businesses is not landing page or website, but a website as the home base plus landing pages for campaigns.

When a landing page is the right choice

A landing page earns its place in five common situations. First, you are running paid ads. Google Ads, Facebook, and Instagram clicks cost money whether or not the visitor converts, so sending that traffic to a page built for one action is the simplest way to get more value from the same budget. Second, you are launching something new: a seasonal offer, a new service, a workshop, a product drop, or a waitlist. A landing page can be live in days and tested before you commit to a full site build.

Third, you have one dominant offer. A business that mainly sells one thing, such as a single consulting package, a signature course, or a home cleaning service in one city, may not need five service pages yet. One strong page that explains the offer, answers objections, and makes booking easy can carry the business for a long time. Fourth, you are promoting through a single channel with a single message, such as a podcast appearance, a trade show, or a flyer with a QR code. The page should continue the exact conversation that sent the visitor there. Fifth, you want to test demand before investing further. A landing page with a real offer and a real signup is a far better test than asking friends whether they like an idea.

In each case, the landing page works because the visitor arrives with intent and the page respects it. The visitor already knows roughly what they want. The page just has to make the next step obvious, easy, and trustworthy.

When a full website is the right choice

A full website is the right choice when customers need to research before they buy, when you offer several services, or when search traffic matters to you. Consider a renovation contractor in Toronto. A customer comparing contractors will want to see past projects, service details, service areas, reviews, and a sense of how the company works before requesting a quote. One page cannot carry all of that without becoming a long, confusing scroll. Separate pages let each service and each question have room to breathe.

A website is also the right choice when you want to be found by people who have never heard of you. Google rarely ranks a single thin page for many different searches. It ranks pages that match specific searches: a page about kitchen renovations in Etobicoke, a page about bathroom renovations, a page about the renovation process, and blog posts that answer the questions customers ask before they call. That is a website's job, and it compounds over time. Our guide to what pages a small business website should have walks through the core pages that cover most businesses.

Trust is the third reason. Larger purchases, regulated services, and anything involving someone's home, health, money, or children demand more proof than one page can provide. Credentials, testimonials, case examples, policies, and a real about page all reassure a cautious buyer. A business with only a landing page can look temporary to that buyer, even if the business itself is well established. A full website signals that the business is here to stay.

What about a one-page website?

A one-page website sits between the two, and it confuses the landing page vs website question because it looks like both. A one-page website presents the whole business on a single scrolling page: who you are, what you offer, why customers should choose you, and how to contact you, with anchor links that jump down the page instead of a menu of separate pages. For a very simple business, such as a solo consultant with one service, a food truck, or a tradesperson in one city, a well-structured one-page site can be enough to start.

The limits show up as the business grows. A one-page site has one page for Google to rank, so it can only realistically target a small number of searches. Adding a second service, a new location, or detailed content means either cramming more into the same scroll, which hurts readability, or rebuilding into a multi-page site anyway. If you expect to add services, locations, or a blog soon, starting with a proper multi-page structure saves a rebuild later. If your business is genuinely simple and likely to stay simple, a one-page site is a practical, honest starting point, and it can grow into a full website when the business does.

What a good landing page actually contains

A good landing page is not just a short page. It follows a structure that removes doubt in the order a visitor feels it. Start with a headline that matches the ad or link that brought the visitor there, in the visitor's own words, not your internal jargon. Follow it with a short subheading that states the outcome, not just the service: what changes for the customer, and by when, in plain language.

Then add the proof a stranger needs before acting: a few genuine reviews, recognizable local details such as the cities you serve, and a photo of real work, a real team, or a real location rather than generic stock imagery. Keep the form short. Every extra field costs you completions, so ask only for what you need to follow up, usually a name, a phone number or email, and one detail about the job. Make the call to action visible without scrolling on a phone, repeat it after the main sections, and make the phone number tappable on mobile, since many Canadian service customers prefer to call. Finally, add the basics that protect trust and keep you compliant: a privacy note near the form, a footer with your business name and contact details, and no claims you cannot back up. A landing page that promises a result you cannot deliver will cost more in complaints and refunds than it ever gains in leads.

The most common mistake: sending ad traffic to your homepage

If you take one practical lesson from this guide, take this one. Your homepage is built for everyone: new visitors, returning customers, job seekers, and people who typed your business name directly. An ad visitor is not everyone. They searched for one thing, clicked one promise, and arrived with a short attention span. A homepage asks them to choose from many options, and many of them choose to leave instead.

The fix is simple and does not require a rebuild. Keep your website as the home base, and build one landing page per campaign or per major offer, on your own domain, such as yourbusiness.ca/snow-removal-mississauga. The page should match the ad's wording, repeat the offer, and offer one next step. You can reuse the same page structure for each campaign and change only the headline, the details, and the form. This is also why landing pages work best on your own domain rather than on a separate tool's domain: visitors see a consistent brand, your analytics stay in one place, and any links or traffic the page earns benefit your main site rather than a third party platform.

Cost and time: what to expect in Canada

Cost follows scope, not the label on the page. A single landing page is usually faster and cheaper to build than a full website, because there is one page to design, write, and test instead of many. A full website takes longer because each page needs its own content, and content is where most projects slow down: gathering photos, writing service descriptions, and getting reviews and credentials in order. Ongoing costs apply to both, including a domain name, hosting, and occasional updates, and those costs exist whether the site is one page or twenty.

Be careful with two cost traps. The first is a cheap landing page tool that charges a monthly fee forever and makes it hard to move your page and your leads elsewhere. Owning the page on your own domain avoids that lock-in. The second is building a full website before you know what customers actually want, and then paying to rebuild it when the offer changes. A landing page first, used to test the offer, can prevent that second cost entirely. How to decide: five questions

Answer these five questions honestly, and the landing page vs website choice usually makes itself. First, how many services or products do you offer? One main offer points to a landing page. Several offers point to a website. Second, what do you want visitors to do? One clear action points to a landing page. Comparing, reading, and exploring point to a website. Third, how will people find you? Ads, social posts, and direct links point to a landing page. Google searches for many different terms point to a website.

Fourth, do you plan to grow soon? If you expect to add services, locations, or content in the next year, a website saves you from rebuilding later. Fifth, what is your budget and timeline right now? If you need something live this week and the budget is tight, a landing page is a practical first step, and a good one can be expanded into a website later rather than thrown away. If you already have steady demand and the website is how customers judge you, invest in the full site first and add landing pages for each campaign after it.

A useful rule of thumb for Canadian small businesses: if you are paying for clicks, you need a landing page for those clicks. If you want free search traffic over time, you need a website behind it. Most businesses that advertise and want to grow organically need both, and they need them connected: the website builds the trust and the search presence, and the landing pages turn campaign traffic into enquiries.

How to get more from the pages you already have

If you already have a website, you do not need to choose again from scratch. Audit the traffic you already pay for or already receive. Are ad clicks landing on your homepage? Build one landing page for your best campaign and compare the results. Are visitors reaching your service pages but not contacting you? The problem may be the offer, the form, or the trust signals on those pages, not the number of pages you have. Our guide to getting more leads from your website covers clearer offers, shorter forms, and faster follow-up, all of which apply to landing pages and websites alike.

Whatever you build, test it on a phone first. Most local search and social traffic arrives on a phone, and a page that looks fine on a desktop can hide its call to action, shrink its form, or slow to a crawl on mobile. Check the headline without scrolling, tap the phone number, submit the form yourself, and read every word out loud. If a sentence sounds like marketing filler, rewrite it in the words a customer would use. A page that is easy to read on a phone, with one obvious next step, will outperform a prettier page that makes visitors work.

Frequently asked questions

Can I start with a landing page and expand into a website later?

Yes, and for many new businesses this is the smartest path. Build the landing page on your own domain, with your real business name, contact details, and branding, so nothing is wasted when you expand. When you are ready, add service pages, an about page, and a contact page around it, and keep the landing page as the campaign page it was built to be. Starting small only becomes a problem if the page lives on a separate tool's domain or uses a throwaway design that has to be rebuilt from scratch.

Do landing pages help with SEO?

Not much on their own. A landing page targets one topic and one audience, so it can rank for a small number of closely related searches, but it cannot replace a website's many pages and content. Your service pages and blog do the heavy lifting for search. Think of landing pages as conversion tools for traffic you already have, and your website as the engine that earns new search traffic over time.

Should my landing page be on a separate domain?

Usually not. Keeping it on your main domain, such as yourbusiness.ca/offer, builds trust, because visitors see the same brand they clicked from, keeps all of your analytics in one place, and avoids paying for a separate tool. A separate domain can also confuse visitors who check whether the page belongs to a real business. The exception is a genuinely separate brand or a short-term test that you do not want connected to your main site, and even then, a subdomain or a path on your main domain is usually simpler.

Can a one-page website rank on Google?

Yes, for a small number of searches, especially searches for your business name and for one main service in one city. A one-page site struggles when you want to rank for many different services, locations, or questions, because Google has only one page to match against all of those searches. If search traffic is a priority and you offer more than one thing, a multi-page website will serve you better in the long run.
