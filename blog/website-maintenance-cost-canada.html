How much does website maintenance cost in Canada in 2026?

A website is not a one-time purchase. Here is what ongoing maintenance actually costs Canadian small businesses in CAD, what a plan should include, and what you can safely skip.

Most small business owners budget carefully for the website build and then forget about everything that comes after. Six months later the contact form stops sending emails, a plugin update breaks the mobile menu, and nobody notices for weeks. Maintenance is the part of owning a website that is invisible when it works and expensive when it does not.

This guide breaks down what website maintenance costs in Canada in 2026 in Canadian dollars: the typical plan ranges you will see, what should be included in each, and how to tell whether you are paying for something useful or for peace of mind you never actually receive.

What "website maintenance" actually includes

Maintenance is a bundle of small, recurring tasks that keep a site running, safe, and current. A complete maintenance plan usually covers most or all of these:

  • Software updates. The platform, themes, and plugins get updated regularly. Skipped updates are the single most common way small sites get hacked - automated attacks scan for known vulnerabilities.
  • Backups. Regular copies of the site, stored off the server, so the site can be restored after a hack, a bad update, or a hosting failure. A backup you have never tested is not a backup.
  • Security monitoring. Scans for malware, suspicious logins, and spam injections, plus quick cleanup when something gets through.
  • Uptime checks. Automated alerts when the site goes down, so a problem gets noticed in minutes instead of months.
  • Small fixes and content edits. Typo fixes, new photos, holiday hours, price changes. Most plans include a small amount of hands-on time per month; bigger changes are billed separately.
  • Performance checks. Periodic looks at page speed and mobile behavior, because sites get slower over time as content and plugins pile up.

If a plan only covers some of these, the rest does not go away. It lands on you - which is fine if you are technically comfortable and actually have the time.

What Canadian businesses actually pay

The ranges below are the monthly plan tiers you will commonly see quoted by Canadian freelancers and small agencies - meant to orient you, not to pin anyone down. Every quote differs based on the site's size, platform, and how much hands-on time is included.

DIY bare minimum: roughly $10 to $25 a month

This is the unavoidable cost of having a website at all, even if you do everything yourself: a domain (roughly $15 to $25 a year in CAD for a .ca or .com), shared hosting (a few dollars to about $30 a month), and an SSL certificate - which most reputable Canadian hosts now include for free. If someone is billing you separately for basic SSL on a standard site, ask why.

At this tier you are the maintenance plan: you run the updates, take the backups, and fix what breaks. It works for simple, low-traffic brochure sites when the owner is willing to learn the platform and actually do the work.

Basic maintenance plan: $50 to $150 a month

The entry-level professional tier. Typically includes software updates, backups, and basic security checks. The site stays online and current, but content edits and fixes are usually billed extra or limited to a short time allowance. This tier suits simple sites with little month-to-month change - a trades contractor, a salon, a professional practice with a brochure site.

Standard maintenance plan: $150 to $250 a month

Adds uptime monitoring, malware cleanup if something slips through, and a real allowance of hands-on time for content edits and small fixes. Most healthy small businesses land here: the site is actively used for enquiries or bookings, the owner does not want to touch it, and a few small changes are needed each month.

Advanced plan: $250 to $400+ a month

For sites with moving parts: online stores, membership areas, lots of traffic, or heavy plugin setups that need regular attention. Usually includes more dev time per month, faster response times, and deeper performance work. E-commerce sites typically sit $100 to $200 above the non-store tiers, because a broken store is a broken cash register - it gets priority and more frequent checks.

Keep this number in perspective: these are the monthly line items. Multiply any quote by 24 before comparing, because the two-year cost is what you are actually committing to.

What drives the cost up

  • Platform and plugin sprawl. A WordPress site with a dozen plugins needs more update and conflict testing than a simple hand-built site. Every premium plugin with its own annual licence adds cost too - usually $20 to $200 a year per plugin - so audit the ones you actually use.
  • E-commerce and money changing hands. Stores, bookings, and anything touching payments get faster support and stricter testing, which costs more.
  • Traffic and scale. A site serving thousands of visitors needs bigger hosting and more monitoring than a brochure site seen by a few dozen people a day.
  • Content volume. If someone needs to add blog posts, product listings, or gallery updates for you every month, that is content work, not maintenance - and it is billed accordingly.
  • Response-time promises. "We fix critical issues within four hours" costs more to deliver than "within two business days." Worth paying for if the site takes orders; overkill for a brochure site.

What you can safely skip

Not everything sold alongside maintenance is maintenance. Watch for these:

  • Paid SSL on a standard site. Reputable hosts include basic SSL certificates for free now. Specialty certificates for financial or legal sites are a real product, but a normal business site does not need one.
  • Premium plugin licences you do not use. If you are paying renewals for five plugins and using two, cancel the extras.
  • Marketing retainers disguised as maintenance. Ongoing SEO, content writing, and ad management are marketing decisions, not maintenance requirements. Your site does not stop working if you skip them - though it may stop growing. Keep the two budgets separate so you can see what each buys.

DIY vs paying someone: how to decide

Doing it yourself is a real option, and for simple sites it is reasonable. The honest requirements: you need to actually run updates within a week or two of their release, keep your own backups somewhere other than your hosting account, and have someone to call when something breaks that is beyond a settings toggle. Most owners manage the first two for a while and then quietly stop, which is when sites get hacked.

The most common middle ground: pay for the basic monitoring, backups, and updates so the technical baseline is covered, and handle your own content edits through the site's editor. You get the safety net without paying for hands-on time you will not use.

If the site brings you leads or takes orders, treat maintenance like insurance on equipment you depend on. A week of a silently broken contact form is worth more than a year of a basic plan.

Questions to ask before you sign a plan

  1. Exactly what is included? Get the list: updates, backups (how often, stored where), security scans, uptime monitoring, and how much hands-on time per month.
  2. Who owns the accounts? The domain, hosting, and platform logins should be in your name or your company's name, with the maintainer as a user - never the other way around. You should be able to leave and take everything with you.
  3. How are backups tested? A plan that has never restored a backup from its own system is a plan you have not verified.
  4. What happens when something breaks? Ask for the response time in writing, and whether cleanup after a hack costs extra.
  5. What is NOT included? Design changes, new features, and content writing are usually out of scope. Knowing the boundary avoids surprise bills.
  6. How do I cancel? Month-to-month with a clear cancellation process is the healthy standard. Be wary of long lock-ins with an early-exit fee.

Red flags

  • No mention of backups, or backups stored only on the same server as the site.
  • "You don't need updates" or a plan with no update schedule at all.
  • Separate billing for basic SSL on a standard site.
  • Hourly billing with no time estimates, so small fixes quietly become large invoices.
  • Accounts registered in the maintainer's name, so leaving means losing your domain or hosting access.
  • A contract that locks you in for a year with no clear exit.

FAQ

Can I maintain my website myself?

Yes, especially for simple sites. The unavoidable costs are the domain and hosting. You take on updates, backups, and fixes yourself. The trade-off is time and skill: most owners do fine for a while and then fall behind on updates, which is when security problems start. A common compromise is paying for just the monitoring, backups, and updates while handling your own content edits.

What happens if I skip maintenance entirely?

Nothing at first. Then, usually in this order: updates pile up, a plugin or platform update eventually conflicts with something and breaks a page, the site gets slower, contact forms or checkout silently stop working, and automated attacks exploit an unpatched vulnerability. Any one of these can sit unnoticed for weeks if nobody is watching - which is the real argument for a plan.

Is maintenance different from hosting?

Yes. Hosting is the rented server space your site lives on - the few-dollars-a-month baseline. Maintenance is the ongoing care: updates, backups, security, and fixes. Some managed hosting plans bundle basic maintenance in, so check before paying for both separately.

Do brand-new websites need maintenance right away?

Yes. A site needs its first round of updates within weeks of launch - platforms and plugins release patches constantly. The good news is that a new, well-built site is cheap to maintain: fewer plugins, clean code, and nothing broken yet. This is the cheapest moment to set up a plan.
