What is a good conversion rate for a small business website?

Your website gets 800 visitors a month and 12 enquiries. Is that good, bad, or normal? For most Canadian small businesses, a conversion rate of 2 to 5 percent is normal, but the honest answer depends on what you count as a conversion, what kind of page it is, and where the traffic came from. This guide explains what the number means, what counts as good for your type of business, and how to raise it without guessing.

The short answer

What is a good conversion rate for a small business website? For a service business, such as a contractor, clinic, accountant, or agency, 2 to 5 percent of visitors submitting a form, calling, or booking is a normal range. Below 2 percent on a key page is worth investigating. Above 5 percent on a contact or quote page is strong. For an online store, the bar is lower: 1 to 3 percent of sessions ending in a purchase is typical. For a focused landing page built for one offer, such as an ad campaign, 5 to 10 percent or higher is common.

The most useful comparison is still your own site over time. If your contact page converted at 3.1 percent last month and 1.4 percent this month, something changed, and that trend matters more than any published benchmark. Benchmarks tell you whether you are in a normal range. Your own history tells you whether something broke or improved.

What conversion rate actually measures

Conversion rate is the percentage of visits that result in the action you care about. The formula is simple:

Conversion rate = conversions divided by sessions, multiplied by 100

If 1,000 sessions produce 25 enquiries, your conversion rate is 2.5 percent. The hard part is not the math. It is deciding what counts as a conversion, and counting it consistently.

A conversion is any action that moves a visitor closer to becoming a customer. For most small businesses, the important ones are:

  • Macro conversions: a purchase, a booked appointment, a submitted quote request, or a phone call. These are the actions that directly create revenue or a sales conversation.
  • Micro conversions: adding an item to a cart, starting a form, signing up for emails, downloading a guide, or viewing a pricing page. These signal intent but are not revenue yet.

Both matter, but they should never be averaged together. A newsletter signup rate of 8 percent and a purchase rate of 1.4 percent can both be healthy on the same site, because one asks for an email address and the other asks for money. When a guide quotes a single average conversion rate without saying what was counted, treat it as a rough hint, not a target.

One more definition that causes confusion: some reports calculate conversion rate per session, others per user. A per-user rate will usually be a bit higher, because one person who visits three times and converts once counts as one user but three sessions. Either is fine, as long as you use the same one every month.

Good conversion rate benchmarks by page type

There is no single good number, because different pages ask for different levels of commitment. The ranges below come from large published studies and are best used for orientation. Each one measures something different, which is why they disagree with each other.

  • Online stores, whole site: about 1.4 to 1.6 percent median. Littledata, which tracks thousands of Shopify stores, reported a median of 1.4 percent of sessions ending in an order in late 2026, with the top fifth of stores above about 2.7 percent and the top tenth above about 3.7 percent. Statista puts the global ecommerce average near 1.6 percent. A store converting at 3 percent or higher is performing well above the median.
  • Service business contact and quote pages: 2 to 5 percent. This is the range most Canadian service businesses should expect for form submissions and consultation requests. A focused quote-request page often converts at the higher end, around 5 percent, because visitors who reach it have already decided they are interested.
  • Dedicated landing pages: about 6.6 percent median. Unbounce analysed tens of thousands of landing pages and found a median conversion rate of 6.6 percent, with the top quarter at roughly 10 percent or higher. These pages exist for one offer and one button, so they should convert better than a general website page. If your ad landing page converts at 2 percent, the page or the ad promise needs work.
  • Google Ads clicks: about 8 percent average, with wide variation. LocaliQ reported an average Google Ads conversion rate near 8 percent per click in 2026, ranging from roughly 2.5 to 14.7 percent by industry. Note that this counts whatever each advertiser chose to track as a conversion, often a lead, not a sale. It is not comparable to a whole-site purchase rate.
  • Email opt-in pages: around 10 percent or higher with warm traffic. A free, low-commitment ask converts far better than a purchase. Pages fed by your own email list or social following will run higher still. Treat any double-digit rate here as normal, not exceptional.

A practical way to use these numbers: match your page to its own benchmark. Compare a store to store data, a landing page to landing page data, and a contact page to the 2 to 5 percent service range. Comparing your whole website, including blog readers who were never going to enquire today, to a landing page median will only make a healthy site look broken.

Why benchmarks disagree, and why yours may look low

Before you redesign anything, check these five things. Each one can move your measured conversion rate by a full point or more, and none of them means your website is failing.

What you count. A purchase is rarer than a form submission, which is rarer than a newsletter signup. If your rate looks low compared to a guide, check whether that guide was counting a smaller ask. This is the single biggest reason published averages range from about 2 percent to over 5 percent for what sounds like the same thing.

Traffic source. Visitors from a Google search for your exact service convert far better than visitors from a casual social media tap. Recent analytics compilations put AI search visitors, from tools like ChatGPT and Google AI Overviews, slightly ahead of traditional organic search, around 3.5 percent versus 2.9 percent, because those visitors arrive with a specific question already answered. If you recently ran a broad social campaign, your overall rate will dip even if your best pages are unchanged.

Device. Mobile traffic is now the majority of visits for most local businesses, but it converts lower than desktop, roughly 1.8 percent versus 3.1 percent in recent cross-industry data. Small screens, shorter attention, and forms that are awkward to complete on a phone all play a part. A site with mostly mobile traffic will almost always show a lower blended rate than a desktop-heavy site. That is a reason to fix the mobile form, not to panic about the average.

Price and commitment. A 20 dollar product and a 20,000 dollar renovation do not convert at the same rate, and they should not. Higher-priced services involve comparison, a phone call, and often a second decision-maker. Judge a high-ticket service page on qualified enquiries and calls, not on whether it matches an ecommerce benchmark.

Missing tracking. This is the most common measurement problem on Canadian small business sites. If phone number clicks and form submissions are not marked as key events in Google Analytics 4, your best visitors are invisible in the conversion report. A visitor who taps your phone number on mobile and becomes a customer may never be counted at all. Our guide on how to set up Google Analytics shows how to mark those actions as key events in about five minutes. Fix measurement before you judge performance.

How to calculate your conversion rate in GA4

If you have Google Analytics 4 installed and your enquiry actions marked as key events, you can find your rate in two places.

For a site-wide number, go to Reports, then Engagement, then Conversions to see total conversions, and Reports, then Acquisition, then Traffic acquisition to see sessions. Divide conversions by sessions for the same date range. Use at least 28 days, ideally a full month, so one busy weekend does not skew the result.

For a page-level number, which is far more useful, go to Reports, then Engagement, then Pages and screens, and add the session key event rate column using the pencil icon to customise the report. This shows the percentage of sessions on each page that produced a key event. Sort by views and look at your homepage, your main service pages, and your contact or quote page. Those are the pages where the rate matters.

Then check the same report by traffic source and by device. If Google search converts at 4 percent and Instagram at 0.6 percent, the problem is not your page, it is the audience. If desktop converts at 4 percent and mobile at 1 percent, the problem is almost certainly the mobile experience, usually the form.

Record the number monthly in a simple spreadsheet: date range, sessions, conversions, and rate, for the whole site and for your top three pages. After three months you have a baseline. That baseline is worth more than any benchmark, because it is your traffic, your prices, and your customers. If you want to understand the companion metric as well, our guide on what is a good bounce rate explains how bounce and engagement fit alongside conversion rate.

What one percentage point is worth

Small changes in conversion rate are worth real money, which is why this metric deserves more attention than raw traffic. Work through a simple example in Canadian dollars.

Suppose your site gets 1,000 sessions a month and converts at 2.5 percent, producing 25 enquiries. If half of those enquiries become customers and your average job is worth 800 dollars, that is roughly 10,000 dollars in monthly revenue from the website. Raise the conversion rate to 3.5 percent, with the same traffic, and you get 35 enquiries, roughly 14,000 dollars. One percentage point, with no extra advertising spend, is worth about 4,000 dollars a month in this example.

Run the same math with your own numbers: monthly sessions, current rate, enquiry-to-customer ratio, and average job value. Most owners find that improving conversion rate by one point is cheaper and faster than doubling traffic, because the visitors are already arriving. This is also why our guide on how to get more leads from your website starts with clarity and friction before it talks about more traffic.

8 fixes that actually raise conversion rate

Work through these in order. The first three fix most low conversion rates on small business sites. The rest are refinements that compound over time.

1. Make the offer and the next step obvious in five seconds. A visitor should be able to tell what you do, who it is for, and what to click, without scrolling. A vague headline such as "innovative solutions" fails this test. A specific headline, a one-sentence subheadline, and one clear button pass it. Our guide on writing website copy that converts covers the headline formula in detail.

2. Shorten the form. Every extra field costs completions. For a first enquiry, name, contact detail, and one short message box are usually enough. Ask for budget, timeline, and project details after the first reply, not before it. If you need more information to quote, use a two-step form that asks for contact details first, so an abandoned second step still leaves you a lead.

3. Show a price, a starting price, or a range. Visitors who cannot tell whether they can afford you often leave without asking. Publishing even a starting price in CAD, with a plain sentence about what changes the final quote and whether tax is extra, filters out bad-fit enquiries and reassures good ones. Our guide on whether to put prices on your website explains how to do this for services that are quoted per job.

4. Put proof next to the ask. Real reviews with names, real photos of your work, years in business, and any licence or certification that matters in your trade. Proof placed beside the form or the call button does more work than proof buried on an about page. Never invent testimonials or results. A small number of genuine, specific reviews beats a wall of generic praise.

5. Make calling effortless on mobile. A tappable phone number, visible without scrolling, with your hours beside it. Most local searches happen on phones, often one-handed. If calling requires copying a number or opening a contact page, mobile visitors will choose the competitor whose number is one tap away.

6. Speed up the page, especially on mobile. Slow pages lose visitors before the offer is even read. Compress images, remove unused plugins and scripts, and test with Google PageSpeed Insights. Our guide on how to make your website load faster walks through the biggest wins first.

7. Match the page to the promise that sent the visitor. If an ad or a search result promises a specific service in a specific town, the landing page should say exactly that in its headline, with that service and that town named. A generic homepage receiving campaign traffic is the most common reason a landing page converts at half the expected rate.

8. Follow up fast, and say what happens next. Conversion does not end at the form. A confirmation message that says when you will reply, and a reply that actually arrives within that window, turns enquiries into customers. If you cannot reply the same day, an automatic email that sets expectations honestly is far better than silence. Visitors who hear nothing within a day usually contact a competitor as well.

When a low conversion rate is fine

Not every low rate needs fixing. Leave it alone, or judge it differently, when:

  • It is a blog post or guide. Most readers came for an answer, not to enquire today. Judge these pages on engagement time, email signups, and whether readers return, not on immediate enquiries.
  • Most traffic comes from a broad social post or a directory listing. Those visitors were browsing, and a low rate is expected.
  • Your service is high-ticket and considered. Fewer, better-qualified enquiries can be worth more than a higher rate of poor-fit ones. Track enquiry quality alongside quantity.
  • Your phone is the main conversion path and calls are not tracked yet. The measured rate will look low until click-to-call tracking is in place. Fix the tracking first.

The question to ask about any page is not "is the rate above a benchmark?" but "is this page doing the job it exists for, for the traffic it actually receives?" If the answer is yes, the benchmark is a footnote. If the answer is no, work through the eight fixes above, starting with clarity, form length, and price transparency.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good conversion rate for a small business website?

For most Canadian small business service websites, 2 to 5 percent of visitors submitting a form, calling, or booking is normal. Online stores average lower, around 1 to 3 percent of sessions ending in a purchase. Focused landing pages built for one offer convert higher, often 5 to 10 percent or more. The best benchmark is your own rate last month, measured the same way.

How do I calculate my website conversion rate?

Divide the number of conversions by the number of sessions or visitors, then multiply by 100. For example, 25 enquiries from 1,000 sessions is a 2.5 percent conversion rate. In Google Analytics 4, mark your enquiry actions as key events, then check Reports, Engagement, Conversions, or add the session key event rate column to your Pages and screens report.

Why is my conversion rate lower than the benchmarks?

Benchmarks measure different things. A whole-store purchase rate will always be lower than a landing page lead rate. Traffic source, device, price, and how much commitment the conversion asks for all move the number. Most small business sites also undercount conversions because phone clicks and form submissions are not tracked as key events, which makes the real rate look worse than it is.

How do I improve my conversion rate?

Start with clarity and friction: make the offer and next step obvious above the fold, shorten forms to the fewest fields possible, show prices or starting prices where you can, add real reviews and photos, and speed up mobile loading. Then fix measurement first, so you can tell whether a change actually helped.
