Why is my website so slow? 9 real causes and how to fix them

Your site takes five seconds to load, customers bail, and nobody can tell you why. Here are the nine most common causes of slow websites, ranked by impact, and what to actually do about each one.

You tap your own website link and wait. And wait. The logo appears, then nothing, then the page stumbles into place piece by piece. Meanwhile your competitor's site snaps open instantly. If you have ever wondered "why is my website so slow?", you are not alone, and it is rarely one dramatic thing. It is usually a handful of small, fixable problems stacked on top of each other.

Speed is not a vanity metric. Google's own published research found that 53% of mobile visitors leave a page that takes longer than three seconds to load. Over half. Your customers browse on phones, often on cell data in a parking lot or a job site, and they will not wait. Speed is also a confirmed Google ranking factor, which means a slow site gets less traffic on top of converting less of it.

The good news: most slowness comes from a short list of usual suspects. This article walks through all nine, in rough order of how often they are the real culprit, with plain-English explanations of what to do about each.

Step 0: measure it before you fix it

Do not start changing things on gut feel. Run your homepage through Google's free PageSpeed Insights tool (pagespeed.web.dev) on both mobile and desktop. It gives you a score out of 100 and, more usefully, a short list of what is dragging your site down with the biggest offenders first.

Pay attention to the metric called Largest Contentful Paint (LCP). That is the time it takes for the main content of the page to become visible. Google's public Core Web Vitals guidance says under 2.5 seconds is good. If yours is 4 seconds or more, you have real work to do. If it is already under 2.5, your speed is fine and your problem is probably somewhere else, like design or messaging.

One more tip: test on a real phone on cell data, not just your office Wi-Fi. Your office connection is fast and your browser has your site cached. Your customers have neither.

Cause 1: giant, uncompressed images

This is the number one cause of slow small-business websites, and it is not close. Someone uploads a 6 MB photo straight from a phone to the homepage banner. A page with four or five of those is asking a visitor's phone to download 30 MB just to read your address.

The fix is simple and free:

  • Resize images to the size they actually display at. A banner that shows at 1,200 pixels wide does not need a 5,000-pixel source file.
  • Compress every image before uploading. Free tools like TinyPNG or Squoosh routinely shrink file sizes by 70 to 80 percent with no visible quality loss.
  • Use modern formats. WebP images are much smaller than JPEGs and PNGs and work in every current browser. Ask whoever manages your site to serve WebP where possible.
  • Lazy-load images below the fold. Images further down the page should only load when the visitor scrolls near them, not all at once on page load.

If you do nothing else in this article, fix your images. On most sites we see, this single change produces the biggest visible improvement.

Cause 2: too many plugins doing too little

If your site runs on WordPress, every active plugin loads code on your pages, and a lot of that code loads whether the page needs it or not. The contact form plugin, the slider plugin, the popup plugin, the social feed plugin, the SEO plugin, the analytics plugin, plus the three plugins you installed in 2023 and forgot about. Twenty or thirty plugins is not unusual on a small business site, and every one of them adds weight.

Go through your plugin list with a ruthless eye and ask of each one: what does this do for my customers, and is there a lighter way to do it? Delete what you do not use (not just deactivate, actually delete it). If two plugins overlap, keep the better one. Sliders, popup builders, and heavy animation packs are the most common offenders: they cost you seconds of load time for visual effects that rarely earn their keep.

Cause 3: cheap, overloaded hosting

Hosting is rented server space, and very cheap shared hosting packs hundreds of websites onto one machine. If a couple of your "neighbours" get busy, your site slows down too. Signs your hosting is the bottleneck: the site is slow even though the design is simple, your admin dashboard feels sluggish, or things get worse at busy times of day.

Decent shared hosting for a small business site in Canada typically runs in the low tens of dollars per month in CAD. Moving from a bargain-bin host to a reputable one, and making sure the server runs a current PHP version with server-level caching, is one of the fastest upgrades available. If your site is already on good hosting, this is probably not your problem, and the causes below matter more.

Cause 4: no caching

Without caching, your server rebuilds every page from scratch for every single visitor: queries the database, runs the code, assembles the page. With caching, the server builds the page once and hands out ready-made copies to everyone after. For repeat visitors, the browser can also keep a local copy instead of re-downloading everything.

A caching plugin (or server-level caching from your host) can cut load times dramatically on a WordPress site, and it takes minutes to set up. If your PageSpeed report mentions "serve static assets with an efficient cache policy", this is what it is asking for. It is also one of those things that should have been set up on day one, so if it is missing, check what else was skipped. Cause 5: too many third-party scripts

Chat widgets, Facebook and TikTok pixels, review popups, booking widgets, analytics tags, email popups. Each one is a small piece of someone else's code that your page has to download and run, often from a server you do not control. Add five or six and you can lose a second or more before your own content even appears.

The fix is an audit, not a shopping trip:

  • List every script on your site and ask what business purpose it serves. A chat widget nobody answers and a pixel for an ad account you stopped using are dead weight.
  • Delay what you keep. Scripts like chat widgets and tracking pixels should load after the page is usable, not before. Any competent developer can defer them.
  • Host what you can locally. Fonts and common libraries served from your own domain skip a round trip to an outside server.

Cause 6: a heavy theme or page builder

Many WordPress sites are built on multipurpose themes or drag-and-drop page builders that load every feature on every page, even the features that page does not use. The page builder that makes editing easy can easily add a megabyte or two of CSS and JavaScript to every single page load.

This one is hard to fix with a plugin. If the theme itself is the weight, the honest options are: switch to a lightweight theme, rebuild the pages without the builder, or accept the speed ceiling the theme imposes. Cause 7: videos embedded the expensive way

A standard YouTube embed does not just load a video. It loads the entire YouTube player, its scripts, and its tracking the moment your page opens, even if the visitor never presses play. Two or three embeds can add seconds to your load time.

The fix: show a thumbnail image with a play button, and only load the actual video player when someone clicks it. This is called a video facade, and most developers can add it in under an hour. Your visitors see the same video; the ones who never press play cost you nothing.

Cause 8: too many web fonts

Custom fonts are small individually, but each weight and style (bold, italic, light, and so on) is a separate file the browser must download before it can draw your text. A site loading six font weights across two font families is spending real time just on typography.

Limit yourself to two font families at most, load only the weights you actually use, and consider system fonts for body text. Most visitors cannot tell the difference between a system font and a custom one in a paragraph, but they can tell when the page takes three seconds to show any text at all.

Cause 9: redirects, outdated everything, and accumulated junk

The last bucket is everything that accrues when a site sits untouched for years: chains of redirects (page A points to page B points to page C, each one adding a round trip), an outdated CMS and theme, a database full of old revisions and spam comments, and expired certificates. None of these is dramatic on its own. Together they add up.

This is the "maintenance debt" category, and it is why ongoing maintenance exists. A site that gets regular updates, cleanups, and checkups does not accumulate this kind of drag. A site nobody has touched since 2022 almost certainly has.

What to fix first: the priority order

Do not try to fix all nine at once. Work in this order, because it is roughly the order of return on effort:

  1. Images. Compress and resize everything on your homepage and top service pages. Biggest win, costs nothing, takes an afternoon.
  2. Hosting. If your host is the bottleneck, no amount of tuning on top will fix it. Move to decent hosting first.
  3. Caching. Turn it on at the server level or with a plugin. Minutes of work, immediate payoff.
  4. Plugin and script audit. Delete what you do not need, defer what you keep.
  5. Video embeds and fonts. Cheap wins once the big three are handled.
  6. Theme weight and maintenance debt. These are the deep fixes. If the site needs them, it may be time to talk rebuild instead of another round of patches.

Most small business sites that work through the first four steps go from painfully slow to comfortably fast. If you run PageSpeed Insights again after each step, you will see exactly which change earned its keep.

When fixing is not enough: the rebuild question

There is a point where patching a slow site stops making sense. If your site runs on an outdated theme nobody supports, if the page builder is the foundation of every page, or if every fix uncovers two more problems, you are paying to maintain weight instead of removing it.

The test is simple: if causes 1 through 5 are fixed and the site is still slow, the problem is structural, and structural problems get fixed by rebuilding, not tuning. A lean modern build has no plugin bloat to audit and no heavy theme to fight, which is why it stays fast without constant babysitting. FAQ

How fast should a website load?

Aim for your main content to be visible in under 2.5 seconds. That is the threshold Google publishes in its Core Web Vitals guidance, and it is a sensible target for any business site. As a hard rule, keep it under three seconds: Google's research found that more than half of mobile visitors abandon pages that take longer than that. Under two seconds is excellent and absolutely achievable for a small business site built well.

Will a slow website hurt my Google rankings?

Yes. Page speed is a confirmed Google ranking factor, and the Core Web Vitals (loading speed, interactivity, and visual stability) are part of how Google evaluates page experience. A slow site also gets fewer of the signals Google likes, because visitors who bounce in two seconds do not stick around, click through, or convert. Speed work is SEO work.

Is WordPress always slow?

No. WordPress itself is not slow; badly built WordPress sites are. A lean WordPress site on good hosting with a lightweight theme, a handful of plugins, compressed images, and caching can load in under two seconds. The reputation comes from the other kind: page-builder themes, thirty plugins, and 6 MB images. The platform is rarely the problem; the build is.

Most of the high-impact fixes are free if you or your developer do them: compressing images, deleting unused plugins, turning on caching, and deferring scripts cost nothing but time. Moving to better hosting usually means paying a fair monthly rate instead of a bargain one, in the low tens of dollars per month in CAD.
