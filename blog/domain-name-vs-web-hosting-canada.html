Domain name vs web hosting: what is the difference?

Every website needs both a domain name and web hosting, and they are two different things you usually buy separately. Here is what each one does, how they connect, what they cost in Canada, and the ownership mistakes that can take your site offline.

If you are getting your first business website, you will hear two terms in the first five minutes: domain name and web hosting. They get used almost interchangeably in sales pages and casual conversation, which is exactly why they confuse people. They are not the same thing, they are not interchangeable, and understanding the difference saves you money and prevents one of the most common (and most expensive) mistakes a small business can make online.

The short version: your domain name is your website's address, and web hosting is the place where your website actually lives. You need both for a working website, they do completely different jobs, and they are billed separately. The rest of this guide explains each one properly, shows how they connect, covers Canadian costs, and walks through the decisions that matter when you buy them.

The short answer: an address and a home

Think of your website as a house. Your domain name is the street address: it is what people type to find you, like yourbusiness.ca. Web hosting is the house itself: the physical space where everything inside it lives, your pages, photos, text, and files. An address with no house behind it is an empty lot. A house with no address is somewhere nobody can find. You need both, and they are separate purchases.

That analogy holds up surprisingly well for the details too. You rent your address (a domain is registered for a set period, usually one to ten years, and you renew it). You rent your house (hosting is a recurring subscription, monthly or yearly). And just like in real life, you can move house without changing your address: if you switch hosting companies, your domain name stays the same and customers never notice. What changes is where the domain points, which is a behind-the-scenes setting you or your developer updates.

What is a domain name?

A domain name is the human-readable address people type into a browser to reach your website, for example yourbusiness.ca. Behind the scenes, computers find websites using long strings of numbers called IP addresses. A domain name is the friendly, memorable label that stands in for one of those numbers.

A domain name has two parts. The part before the dot is the name you choose, usually your business name. The part after the dot is the extension, also called the top-level domain: .ca, .com, .net, .org, and hundreds of others. For a Canadian small business, the two extensions that matter most are .ca (Canada's country domain) and .com (the worldwide default). Our guide on how to choose a domain name in Canada covers the .ca vs .com decision, CIRA's Canadian Presence Requirements, and the naming rules that actually matter.

Three facts about domains that surprise first-time buyers:

  • You do not buy a domain outright, you register it. Registration gives you the exclusive right to use that name for a set period, usually one to ten years. When the period ends, you renew it or lose it. Anyone who tells you they "own" a domain forever is simplifying.
  • Every domain name in the world is unique. Only one business can hold yourbusiness.ca at a time. That is why good names get taken, and why registering early matters even if your website is months away.
  • A domain stores nothing. It holds no pages, no photos, no files. It is only a pointer. That is why buying a domain alone does not give you a website, and why a domain by itself costs so little compared with everything else a website needs.

Domains are sold by companies called registrars, which are accredited to register names on your behalf. In Canada, popular options include Canadian registrars and the big international ones. What matters is not the brand name on the registrar, it is that the domain is registered in your name, on an account you control, with auto-renew turned on and a current payment method. More on that below, because it is the single most important ownership decision in this whole guide.

What is web hosting?

Web hosting is the service that stores your website's files and delivers them to visitors. When you buy a hosting plan, you are renting space on a powerful computer called a server, kept in a data centre with fast internet, backup power, and cooling. Your website's pages, images, videos, and databases live on that server. When someone visits your domain, their browser asks the server for your files, and the server sends them back. That round trip is what "loading a website" actually is.

Hosting is what makes your site available around the clock. Without it, there is nothing for a browser to load. It is also what determines a large part of how fast your site feels, how much traffic it can handle, and how well it is protected. If your site is slow, the cause is often the hosting plan rather than the design, which is why we wrote a separate guide on why websites are slow and one on what web hosting costs in Canada.

For a typical Canadian small business, the hosting types you will actually encounter are:

  • Shared hosting. Your site shares one server with many other sites. It is the cheapest option and plenty for a small business website, a brochure site, or a simple online store. The trade-off is that a traffic spike on a neighbouring site can slow yours down.
  • Managed WordPress hosting. Shared hosting tuned specifically for WordPress, with updates, backups, and caching handled for you. A good fit if your site runs on WordPress and you would rather not manage the technical side yourself.
  • VPS hosting. A virtual private server: a slice of a server reserved just for you, with guaranteed resources. Costs more, performs better, and suits busier sites or online stores with real traffic.
  • Cloud and dedicated hosting. Larger, more expensive options for high-traffic sites and web applications. Most small businesses will never need these, and anyone selling them to a five-page brochure site is overselling.

A hosting plan usually includes more than just storage: an SSL certificate (the padlock in the browser, now expected by visitors and Google), email accounts on your domain, backups, and a control panel for managing it all. Some plans bundle a free domain for the first year. That is convenient, but read the ownership section below before accepting a "free" domain from a host, because the fine print sometimes matters.

Domain name vs web hosting: the key differences

Side by side, the two services are easy to tell apart:

What it is Your website's address (yourbusiness.ca) The server space where your website's files live
What it does Helps people find and remember your site Stores your site and serves it to visitors
Without the other An address with nothing behind it; nothing loads A site nobody can reach, except by a raw server address
Typical cost in Canada Roughly CAD $15 to $25 per year for a standard .ca or .com Roughly a few dollars to about CAD $20 per month for shared hosting
How you pay Registered for 1 to 10 years, then renewed Recurring subscription, monthly or yearly
Who sells it A domain registrar A web hosting company
Uniqueness Globally unique; only one owner at a time Not unique; many sites can share one server
Security concerns Renewal lapses, hijacking, privacy of your contact details SSL, malware, backups, server uptime

The practical takeaway from that table is simple: the domain is cheap and rarely changes, while hosting is the ongoing cost and the thing you will eventually upgrade or switch. Budget accordingly. A domain is a once-a-year line item you can almost forget about; hosting is a monthly service you should review once a year.

How a domain and hosting work together

Because the domain and the hosting are separate services, they need a translator to talk to each other. That translator is the Domain Name System, or DNS. Here is what happens, step by step, when a customer visits your site:

  1. The customer types yourbusiness.ca into their browser.
  2. The browser asks the DNS system where that name points. DNS looks up the records attached to your domain and returns the IP address of your hosting server.
  3. The browser connects to that server and asks for your homepage.
  4. The server sends back your files, and the browser displays your website.

The whole exchange usually takes a fraction of a second. Two DNS settings do most of the work: nameservers, which say which company manages your domain's DNS records, and A records, which point your domain at a specific server IP address. When you move to a new host, your developer updates the A record (or the nameservers) so the same domain name now points at the new server. Your customers keep typing the same address, and they land on the new host without noticing anything changed.

One timing detail worth knowing: DNS changes take time to spread around the world, usually minutes to a few hours, occasionally up to 24 hours. That is normal. If you switch hosts and the old site still shows for a while, it is usually DNS propagation, not a mistake. Do not panic and do not change the settings again in a hurry; that only restarts the clock.

DNS also does more than point your website at a server. The same system routes your email: MX records tell the internet where mail for yourbusiness.ca should be delivered. That is why your domain and your email are connected, and why losing control of a domain can also break your business email. Our guide on getting your business on Google touches on the same point from the local-search side: consistent, working contact details across Google, your website, and your email all reinforce each other.

Do you need both?

For a real business website, yes: you need both a domain name and hosting. There are only a few situations where one alone is enough, and they are worth naming so you can recognize them:

  • Domain only: you register the name now to stop someone else taking it, and build the website later. You can park the domain or forward it to your Instagram or Facebook page in the meantime. This is a sensible move when you have settled on a business name but the website is a few months away.
  • Hosting only: you are building a site on a staging or test address (a temporary subdomain your host provides) before the real domain is ready. Developers do this all the time. The site exists, but customers cannot find it at your real address until the domain is connected.
  • Neither, temporarily: you use a free website builder subdomain, like yourbusiness.wixsite.com or yourbusiness.wordpress.com. That works for a hobby project, but for a business it looks unprofessional, you do not own the address, and you cannot take it with you if you leave the platform. If you are serious about the business, register your own domain.

One more common question: can you have a website with a domain name but no hosting if the site is tiny? No. Even a one-page site is a file somewhere, and that file has to live on a server. The smallest website still needs hosting; it just needs very little of it.

Should you buy your domain and hosting from the same company?

Many hosts sell domains, and many registrars sell hosting, so you will be offered the bundle either way. Both approaches work. Here is how to choose:

  • Same company: simpler to start. The connection is set up for you, there is one bill, and one support team to call when something breaks. For a first website, that simplicity is genuinely valuable.
  • Separate companies: more freedom later. If your host raises prices, performs badly, or goes out of business, you can move the site without touching the domain. You point the domain at the new host and you are done. When the domain and hosting live with one company, leaving is still possible, but it involves transferring the domain too, which adds a step and a delay.
  • The deciding factor is ownership, not convenience. Whichever route you choose, the domain must be registered in your name, on an account you control. If a host offers a "free domain for the first year," check the terms: who is listed as the registrant, can you transfer the domain away later, and what does renewal cost after year one? A free first year followed by an inflated renewal is a common pricing trick.

Our recommendation for most Canadian small businesses: register the domain with a reputable registrar in your own account, and choose hosting separately based on the site you actually need. If you would rather have one bill and one support contact, that is fine too, as long as you can confirm in writing that the domain is registered to your business and transferable.

What a domain and hosting cost in Canada

Costs are the part everyone wants a straight answer on, so here are the honest ranges for a typical small business site. Prices vary by provider and by whether you pay monthly or annually, so treat these as planning numbers and check current pricing before you buy.

  • Domain name: a standard .ca or .com usually costs in the low tens of Canadian dollars per year, roughly CAD $15 to $25 per year. Premium or short names that someone already owns can cost far more on the aftermarket. Renewal usually costs about the same as the first year, except at registrars that advertise a cheap first year and a higher renewal, so check the renewal price, not just the intro price.
  • Web hosting: shared hosting for a small business site typically runs from a few dollars a month to roughly CAD $20 per month, depending on the plan, the provider, and whether you pay annually. Managed WordPress hosting and VPS plans cost more, starting roughly in the CAD $20 to $50 per month range. Our detailed breakdown in how much web hosting costs in Canada covers plan types and the add-ons that quietly inflate the bill.
  • Together: a domain plus shared hosting usually comes to well under CAD $300 per year for a typical small business website. That is the baseline. Email hosting, an SSL certificate (often included now), backups, and security add-ons can push it higher, and a busy online store on a VPS plan costs more again.

Two cost traps to watch for. First, the intro-price trap: a host advertises CAD $3 per month, but that price applies only to a three-year prepayment, and the renewal jumps to CAD $12 per month. Always look at the renewal price. Second, the add-on creep: a cheap hosting plan with paid extras for backups, malware scanning, and email can end up costing more than a slightly pricier plan that includes them. Compare total cost, not headline price.

The ownership mistakes that take businesses offline

The domain-vs-hosting confusion causes real damage, and it almost always comes down to ownership. These are the mistakes we see most often, and all of them are avoidable:

  1. Letting someone else register your domain. A developer, agency, or "marketing friend" registers yourbusiness.ca in their account to be helpful. Years later they disappear, stop answering, or fall out with you, and you cannot renew, transfer, or point your own domain anywhere. Recovery through the registry is slow and sometimes impossible. The rule is absolute: the domain is registered in your name, on your account. If a developer needs access, they can be added as a technical contact, but the registrant is you.
  2. Missing a renewal. Letting a domain expire is the most common way businesses lose one. Registrars send reminders, but they go to the email on file, which is exactly the email people let die. Turn on auto-renew, keep a valid payment method on the account, and keep the contact email current. A lapsed domain can be grabbed by someone else, and buying it back is expensive if it is possible at all.
  3. Forgetting the domain and hosting renew on different schedules. Your domain renews once a year; your hosting renews monthly or yearly on its own cycle. It is easy to remember one and forget the other. A lapsed hosting plan takes the site down even though the domain is fine, and a lapsed domain takes the site down even though the hosting is paid up. Put both renewal dates in your calendar.
  4. Not knowing where anything is. Businesses change developers, and the new one asks a simple question: where is the domain registered, and where is the site hosted? If nobody can answer, every fix starts with detective work. Keep a one-page record: registrar name and account email, hosting company and plan, renewal dates, and who has access. Store it somewhere the business owner can reach, not only in a developer's inbox.
  5. Confusing a website builder with hosting. Platforms like Wix, Squarespace, and Shopify bundle hosting into their monthly fee, which is fine, but the site lives on their platform. If you cancel, the site does not come with you. That is not a scam, it is the model, but it is a decision you should make knowingly. If you ever want to move, you will be rebuilding, not transferring.

A simple buying checklist

If you are about to buy your first domain and hosting, run through this list in order:

  1. Pick the domain. Match your business name, keep it short and easy to spell, prefer .ca if you serve Canadian customers (with the .com as a redirect if you can). Our domain name guide has the full checklist.
  2. Register it yourself, in your name. Use an account you control, with your business email on file. Turn on auto-renew and two-factor authentication, and enable the registrar lock if one is offered.
  3. Choose hosting for the site you actually have. A brochure site needs shared hosting. A WordPress site is happiest on managed WordPress hosting. A busy store needs a VPS. Do not buy a plan sized for a site you might have in three years.
  4. Check the renewal price, not the intro price. Compare what you will pay in year two, including any add-ons you need.
  5. Connect them and test. Point the domain at the host (your host's setup guide or your developer handles this), then visit the site from your phone and a computer, check that the padlock shows, and confirm your email still works.
  6. Write it all down. Registrar, host, renewal dates, account emails. One page, stored where you can find it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I buy a domain name without web hosting?

Yes. You can register a domain name on its own and park it, forward it to another page, or use it for email only. But a domain on its own shows no website: until it points at hosting that holds your site's files, visitors who type it in will see nothing (or an error page). Most businesses register the domain first to lock in the name, then add hosting when the site is ready.

Do I need web hosting if I already have a domain name?

Yes, if you want a website at that address. The domain is only the address; hosting is the server space where your pages, images, and files actually live. Without hosting, there is nothing for the browser to load when someone visits your domain. The one exception is if you forward the domain to a page hosted somewhere else, like a social profile or a third-party landing page builder.

Should I buy my domain and hosting from the same company?

Buying both from one company is simpler at the start because the connection is set up for you. Buying them separately gives you more freedom: if the host raises prices or performs badly, you can move the site without touching the domain. Either way works. The important rule is the same: the domain must be registered in your name, on an account you control, so you can point it anywhere you choose.

How much do a domain name and web hosting cost in Canada?

A standard .ca or .com domain usually costs in the low tens of Canadian dollars per year, roughly CAD $15 to $25 per year. Shared web hosting for a small business site typically runs from a few dollars a month up to roughly CAD $20 per month, depending on the plan and whether you pay monthly or annually. Together, the pair usually costs well under CAD $300 per year for a typical small business website, before email or any extras.
