How much does web hosting cost in Canada in 2026?

The short answer: most Canadian small businesses pay between $4 and $30 a month for hosting, with $10 to $18 a month being the realistic range once introductory pricing wears off. Managed WordPress hosting runs $25 to $35 a month, a virtual private server $20 to $100, and dedicated hardware $100 and up. The number on the pricing page is rarely the number you end up paying - here is what hosting actually costs, what moves the price, and the renewal trap that doubles most people's bills.

Web hosting is the rented server space your website lives on. Every time someone opens your site, a computer somewhere serves up its files, and you pay rent for that computer. It is the smallest line item in most businesses' web budgets - often under $200 a year - yet it is the one people overpay for most often, because the pricing is designed to look cheaper than it is.

That is the real subject of this guide. Not the headline prices - you can read those yourself - but the price you actually pay in year two, the extras that appear at checkout, and which type of hosting a small business genuinely needs. All prices below are in CAD, based on 2026 Canadian hosting guides and provider pricing.

The four types of hosting and what each costs

Hosting comes in four broad tiers. Almost every small business lives on the first two.

Shared hosting: $3 to $18 a month (realistically)

Shared hosting puts your site on a server with dozens or hundreds of others, splitting the cost. It is the cheapest way to keep a website online, and for a brochure-style site with a few hundred visitors a day, it is genuinely enough. Canadian guides put the headline range at roughly $3 to $18 a month, but here is the part that matters: that range spans introductory and renewal pricing, which are two different products wearing the same name.

The advertised $3 or $4 a month is a first-term rate. When the term ends, the same plan typically renews between $10 and $18 a month. So a small-business site on budget shared hosting realistically costs $120 to $220 a year after the first year, not the $36 the pricing page suggests. Shared hosting is the right call when your site is small, your traffic is modest, and you (or someone you pay) can handle routine WordPress updates and backups. It is the wrong call when your site is slow because of server response time - one overloaded neighbour on the same server can drag your pages down with it.

Managed WordPress hosting: $25 to $35 a month

Managed WordPress hosting is shared or cloud infrastructure with the maintenance handled for you: automatic updates, daily backups, staging sites, WordPress-specific security and caching, and support staff who actually know WordPress. Canadian guides put it at roughly $25 to $35 a month, and - unlike budget shared plans - the price generally holds at renewal rather than tripling.

This is the sweet spot for most businesses running WordPress. You are paying for someone else to worry about updates, malware cleanup, and the 2 a.m. "my site is down" moments. If your current host sends you a renewal at $15 a month but charges extra for backups, security scanning, and staging - which many do - the managed plan at $30 that includes all of it is often cheaper in practice. The flat, predictable price is the point.

VPS hosting: $20 to $100 a month

A virtual private server gives your site its own guaranteed slice of a server - dedicated CPU, RAM, and bandwidth that neighbours cannot touch. Canadian guides place entry-level VPS plans around $20 a month, scaling to $100 for more resources. A VPS makes sense when you have outgrown shared hosting: thousands of daily visitors, an online store with real traffic, or custom software that shared hosts will not run.

The cost decision on a VPS is not really the hardware - it is management. An unmanaged VPS at $20 a month assumes you can SSH into a server, install updates, and fix things when they break. A managed VPS with the same specs runs two to six times more, because you are paying for someone to do that work. For a business owner with no server skills, an unmanaged VPS is the most expensive option in the list, because the first emergency will cost more than the savings.

Dedicated and cloud hosting: $100 a month and up

Dedicated hosting rents you an entire physical server. Cloud hosting spreads your site across flexible infrastructure that scales with demand. Both start around $100 a month and climb from there. Almost no small business needs either. They exist for high-traffic stores, SaaS products, and sites where a minute of downtime costs real money. If a host is upselling you dedicated hardware for a five-page services site, find a different host.

The renewal trap: the price you see is not the price you pay

This is the single most important thing in this guide, so here it is plainly: most budget hosting is sold on introductory pricing that lasts one term - usually one to three years - and renews at two to four times the intro rate. In extreme cases the increase exceeds 500 percent. The $2.95-a-month plan that costs $106 for three years renews at $10 to $15 a month, or $360-plus for the next three years.

This is not a scam - the renewal price is in the fine print, and regulators have pushed hosts to disclose it more clearly. But it works because almost nobody reads the fine print, and because the pain arrives years later, long after you have forgotten the intro deal. Here is how to buy hosting like someone who has seen the trap:

  • Calculate the two-term cost, not the headline price. Add the intro term plus one renewal term. A $4/month intro that renews at $14 is $216 a year in reality, not $48. Compare hosts on that number.
  • Check what the extras cost after the intro. Free domain for year one, free SSL forever, paid backups from year two - write down what each add-on costs at renewal, because a $3 plan with $8 a month of extras is an $11 plan.
  • Prefer hosts with flat renewal pricing. Some providers - generally the managed WordPress hosts and the month-to-month VPS providers - charge the same rate every month, forever. That predictability is worth paying a little more for.
  • Set a calendar reminder before renewal. Most hosts auto-renew at the higher rate. A reminder a month before gives you the option to switch, renegotiate, or at least budget for it. Switching hosts takes a few hours for a standard site, and many hosts migrate you for free.

What actually drives the price (it is not storage)

Hosting plans love to advertise disk space and bandwidth, and for a small-business site neither matters much. Your five-page site with photos is a few hundred megabytes; the "100 GB" on the plan is marketing. What actually separates a $4 plan from a $30 plan:

  • Support that solves things. On a budget plan, support resets passwords. On a good plan, support fixes the broken site. When something breaks at 9 p.m. before a big day, this is the entire product.
  • Backups you can actually use. Daily automatic backups stored off the server, kept for 30 days, restorable in one click. A host that keeps seven days of backups on the same server as your site is no help when you discover a hack three weeks later.
  • Server response time. Time to first byte - how fast the server starts answering - is the one hosting metric that affects your Google ranking and your visitors' patience. Good shared and managed hosts answer in a few hundred milliseconds; overloaded budget servers can take over a second, and no amount of page optimization fixes that. Our guide to why websites get slow explains how to tell whether the server or the site is the bottleneck.
  • Security and updates. Malware scanning, web application firewalls, and (on managed plans) automatic core updates. Shared hosting is only as safe as its worst neighbour, so the host's security posture matters more than the brochure suggests.
  • Staging and developer tools. A one-click staging copy lets changes be tested before they go live. Nice to have for a brochure site; essential if the site earns money.

The Canadian specifics: currency, data centres, and privacy

Hosting in Canada has three wrinkles that American pricing guides never mention.

Currency. Several big-name hosts bill in USD, which means your $9.99 USD plan is really about $14 CAD today and a different number next quarter. Exchange swings quietly move your hosting bill. Canadian hosts that bill in CAD - Web Hosting Canada, CanSpace, HostPapa, and others - remove the guessing. When comparing, convert everything to CAD before deciding anything is cheaper.

Data centres and latency. Servers in Toronto, Montreal, or Vancouver respond faster to Canadian visitors than servers in Texas. The difference is measured in tens of milliseconds - not dramatic, but real, and free when you pick a host with Canadian data centres. It also matters for search: speed is a ranking factor, and server location is the one speed input you choose once and never touch again.

Data residency and privacy. Canadian privacy law (PIPEDA federally, plus Quebec's Law 25) does not strictly require your website data to live in Canada, but it does require you to protect personal information wherever it goes and to disclose cross-border transfers in your privacy policy. Hosting on Canadian soil removes the question entirely - no disclosure to write, no foreign-access rules to explain. If your site collects quote requests, appointment bookings, or customer details, Canadian data residency is a genuine compliance simplification. Our Canadian privacy policy guide covers what your site needs to disclose either way.

The hidden extras that appear at checkout

The plan price is the beginning of the invoice, not the end. Before you compare two hosts, check these:

  • Domain renewal. The "free domain" is free for year one. Renewal runs $15 to $45 a year for a .ca or .com, and some hosts charge more than a dedicated registrar would. Our domain name guide breaks down Canadian pricing.
  • Email hosting. Some plans include email; many now push you toward a separate paid mailbox. If you need info@yourbusiness.ca addresses, confirm what is included.
  • Backups. Included on good plans, a paid add-on on cheap ones - sometimes $2 to $5 a month. A site without backups is a site you are willing to rebuild from scratch.
  • SSL certificates. The padlock in the browser. Most reputable Canadian hosts include this free now via Let's Encrypt; if a host charges for basic SSL in 2026, that tells you something about the host.
  • Site migration. Many hosts move your existing site for free to win your business. Some charge $100-plus. Ask before you assume.
  • Control panel fees. A few hosts add a monthly cPanel licensing fee on VPS plans. It is legitimate, but it should be disclosed, not discovered.

What a Canadian small business should actually pay

Enough theory. Here is the decision, by situation:

  • New brochure site, tight budget: budget shared hosting at a Canadian host, one to three years prepaid on the intro rate. Real cost: $40 to $150 for the first term, then $120 to $220 a year at renewal. Handle updates yourself or budget a maintenance plan. Our maintenance cost guide covers what ongoing care runs.
  • Established business, WordPress site, no in-house tech skills: managed WordPress hosting at $25 to $35 a month. The flat price, automatic backups, and WordPress-literate support are worth it the first time something breaks - which, on WordPress, is a matter of when.
  • Growing store or high-traffic site: managed VPS at $40 to $80 a month. Do not buy an unmanaged VPS to save $30 a month unless someone on your team can run a Linux server.
  • Just need email and a landing page: the cheapest shared plan you can find from a reputable host, or even a website builder's included hosting. Do not overthink a site that gets fifty visitors a month.

And the two things to never do: do not pick hosting on the intro price alone, and do not buy more server than your traffic justifies. A $30 managed plan for a site that gets 200 visits a month is money burned; a $4 shared plan for a store doing $50,000 a month in sales is risk taken. Match the hosting to the business, and recheck once a year - hosting needs change as the business grows.

Frequently asked questions

Can I switch web hosts later without rebuilding my website?

Yes. Your website's files, database, and domain are portable. Moving means copying the site to the new host, testing it, and pointing your domain to the new server - usually a few hours of work for a standard small-business site. Many hosts offer free migration for new customers. The catch is proprietary site builders: a site built in a host's locked builder cannot move, which is one reason to avoid building a business site on a builder you cannot leave.

Does web hosting affect my Google ranking?

Indirectly, yes. Google weighs page speed and uptime, and both depend partly on hosting. A host with slow server response times (time to first byte consistently above about 600 milliseconds) drags down every page. Frequent downtime hurts too, because Google cannot rank pages it cannot reach. But hosting is rarely the main reason a site ranks badly - thin content, no local signals, and slow pages usually matter more. Rule of thumb: measure your server response time first; only upgrade hosting if the server is actually the bottleneck.

Do I need a Canadian web host for PIPEDA compliance?

PIPEDA does not require your data to be stored in Canada, but it does require you to protect personal information wherever it goes and to tell people where it is stored. Keeping data on Canadian servers removes the cross-border transfer question entirely, which simplifies your privacy policy and your Law 25 obligations in Quebec. If your site collects customer details, quotes, or appointment information, Canadian data residency is a genuine plus, not just marketing.

Is free web hosting worth it for a business?

No. Free hosting is fine for a personal experiment, but it pays for itself by showing ads on your site, using a subdomain instead of your domain, throttling your speed, and offering no real support or backup. For a business that depends on its website to generate calls and customers, the reputational cost of a slow, ad-covered, unreliable site dwarfs the $4 to $15 a month that basic hosting costs. There is no business case for free hosting.
