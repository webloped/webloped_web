How to add a contact form to your website

A visitor who is ready to enquire will not always phone you. Many would rather type a short message and wait for a reply, and if your website gives them no easy way to do that, they go back to Google and contact the next business on the list. A contact form is the front door for those people, and adding one takes about thirty minutes.

Think about the last time you needed a service and found a business online. You liked the site, the reviews were good, and you had a question before you were ready to commit. Calling felt like too much. Opening your email, finding their address, and writing a formal message felt like work. A short form on their contact page would have taken thirty seconds. If the site had one, you probably used it. If it did not, you probably moved on.

That is the job of a contact form. It is not a fancy feature, and it is not a sales tool. It is the lowest-effort way for a visitor to reach you, and the lowest-effort way for you to receive an enquiry with enough detail to answer it properly. A phone number serves the people who want to talk now. A form serves everyone else: the person browsing at 11 pm, the person at work who cannot take a call, the person who wants a quote in writing before they decide.

Adding a contact form to your website is a small job, but it has more ways to go wrong than most people expect. Forms that send messages nowhere, forms that ask for so much information that nobody finishes them, forms that drown in spam, forms that never tell the visitor their message arrived. This guide covers the whole job in order: what to ask, how to add the form on each platform, where the messages go, how to stop the spam, and how to test it like a customer would. It is written for Canadian small businesses, with Canadian privacy notes and CAD costs throughout.

Why a contact form earns its place on your site

A contact form does three things that a bare email address and a phone number do not. First, it lowers the effort of getting in touch. The visitor does not have to open a mail app, copy an address, or decide what to write. The form tells them exactly what you need, in the order you need it, and they fill in the blanks. Second, it gives you structured enquiries. Instead of a one-line email that says "call me," you get a name, a way to reply, and a message that says what the person actually wants, which means your first reply can be useful instead of a request for more information. Third, it protects your email address. An address printed on a page gets harvested by spam bots within weeks. A form keeps the address hidden while still letting people write to you.

The form also works while you are closed. A visitor who finds your site at 10 pm on a Sunday can send a message and go to bed knowing you will see it in the morning. Without a form, that same visitor has to remember to come back during business hours, and most will not. For a service business that gets enquiries outside office hours, this alone is worth the thirty minutes it takes to set up.

None of this replaces a phone number. Some visitors will always prefer to call, and your number should still be easy to find on every page. The form is the second door, for the people who will not use the first one. Step 1: Decide what your form should ask

Before you touch any tool, decide what the form asks for, because this decision shapes everything else. The rule is simple: ask for the least you need to reply helpfully, and nothing more. Every extra field gives a busy visitor one more reason to give up before pressing Send.

For most Canadian small businesses, four fields are enough: name, email address, an optional phone number, and one open box for the message. Name tells you who you are talking to. Email gives you a way to reply that works at any hour. Phone is optional because some visitors will not give it until they know you are real, and making it required loses them. The message box is where the visitor says what they need, in their own words, and that is the part you actually need to answer.

Some businesses need one or two more fields, and that is fine when the field saves a round of back and forth. A contractor might ask for the type of project and the city or neighbourhood, because the answer changes whether the job is in their service area. A clinic might ask whether the person is a new or returning patient. A business that takes bookings might ask for a preferred date. The test for any extra field is whether it changes your first reply. If the answer would be the same with or without it, leave it out.

Keep the labels plain. "Name," "Email," "Phone (optional)," and "How can we help?" are clearer than anything clever. Mark the required fields with a small asterisk and a short note that says which fields are required. Do not make the phone number required. Do not ask for a home address, a date of birth, or a budget unless your business genuinely needs it to reply, because each of those makes a visitor pause and wonder why you want it. And do not ask for a credit card number, a password, or a government ID in a form, ever. If a process needs that kind of detail, move it to a phone call or a secure channel, and say so on the form.

Step 2: Add the form to your website

The way you add a contact form depends on what your site is built with, and the good news is that every common platform has a simple path. You do not need to write code for any of these, and none of them should take more than half an hour.

WordPress. Install a form plugin from the official WordPress plugin directory. WPForms Lite is the easiest starting point: install it, activate it, open its form builder, and start from the simple contact form template it offers. Contact Form 7 is the other well-known free option, and it is powerful, but its interface is less friendly for a first-time user. Once the form is built, place it on your contact page using the block the plugin adds to the editor. Both plugins let you choose which fields are required, set the email address that receives the messages, and write the confirmation message the visitor sees after submitting. If your site is on WordPress, this is the path to take, and it is free.

Wix and Squarespace. Neither needs a plugin. Both have a form block built into the editor. Open your contact page in the editor, add a form block from the list of elements, and edit the fields to match the four-field list above. Set the destination email in the form settings, which is the address the submissions are sent to. Publish the page and the form is live. The built-in forms on these platforms also save submissions inside your site dashboard, so you have a backup copy even if an email goes missing.

Shopify. Shopify includes a contact form template, and most themes have a contact page with a form already on it. If your theme does not, add a form app from the Shopify App Store, or use the contact form section in the theme editor. Shopify stores form submissions in your store admin as well as emailing them to you, which is a useful safety net.

A site you edit yourself, or a custom-built site. A plain HTML form needs something on the other end to receive the message, because a static page cannot send email by itself. The simplest option is a form-handling service such as Formspree or Basin. You point your form at their endpoint, they receive the submission and email it to you, and most have a free plan that covers a small number of submissions a month, which is enough for a small business starting out. If your site has a developer, they can connect the form to your own email or to a simple backend instead, which gives you more control and no monthly fee. Either way, the form on the page looks the same to the visitor.

Wherever the form lives, put it where people expect it. The contact page is the obvious home, and the form should sit below your phone number and business hours, not instead of them. A visitor who prefers to call should not have to scroll past a form to find the number. Some businesses also add a short version of the form, just name, email, and message, at the bottom of a service page or a quote page, because a visitor who has just read about a service is often ready to enquire without navigating to another page. That is a good idea when the form is short. It is a bad idea when it duplicates a long form, because a visitor who sees two different forms on the same site starts to wonder which one is the real one.

Step 3: Make sure the messages actually reach you

This is the step most people skip, and it is the one that matters most. A form that looks perfect on the page but sends its messages nowhere is worse than no form at all, because visitors believe they have contacted you and you never know they tried.

Open the form's notification settings and set the destination to an email address you check every day. Ideally this is a professional address at your own domain, such as hello@yourbusiness.ca, rather than a personal Gmail address. A domain address looks more trustworthy to customers, and you control it even if you change email providers. Our guide on setting up a business email address in Canada walks through the options and the costs, which are usually a few dollars a month per mailbox.

Send a test message the same day you publish the form, and do it the way a customer would: open your contact page in a private or incognito browser window, fill in the form with a different email address than the one it sends to, and press Send. Then check three places. First, your inbox, where the message should arrive within a minute or two. Second, your spam or junk folder, because a form notification that lands in spam is a form that is silently failing. Third, the submissions area inside your website dashboard, if your form tool saves copies there, which most WordPress plugins and builders do. If the message appears in any of those places, you know the chain works. If it appears in none of them, the problem is usually one of three things: the destination email is typed wrong, the form is connected to an old address nobody checks, or the site's email sending is not configured, which is common on WordPress sites that have never sent mail before and usually needs a small SMTP plugin to fix.

Repeat the test once a month, and again after any change to your site, your email, or your hosting. Forms break quietly. A hosting change, a plugin update, or a new spam filter can stop delivery without any visible error on the page, and the only way you find out is when a customer phones to ask why you never replied to the message they sent last week. A monthly test takes two minutes and catches that before it costs you a job.

One more detail that saves real enquiries: set up a backup destination. If your form tool supports it, send a copy of every submission to a second address, such as a second staff member or a shared inbox. If it does not, make sure the tool saves submissions inside the dashboard, and check that list once a week. An enquiry that arrives by email and is also saved on your site is an enquiry you cannot lose to a spam filter.

Step 4: Stop the spam without stopping the customers

Within days of going live, a contact form starts attracting spam. Bots crawl the web looking for forms and fill them with links, nonsense, and the occasional phishing attempt. A form with no protection will fill your inbox with junk, and the real enquiries will get buried. The fix is to add protection that bots notice and people do not.

Start with a honeypot field. Most form tools include one for free, and you usually just need to turn it on in the settings. A honeypot is an extra field that is invisible to people but visible to bots. A real visitor never sees it and never fills it in. A bot fills in every field it finds, including the invisible one, and the form throws that submission away silently. It costs the visitor nothing, it blocks a large share of automated spam, and it is the first thing to enable.

If spam still gets through, add Google reCAPTCHA. The modern version, reCAPTCHA v3, runs in the background and scores each submission without making the visitor click on pictures of traffic lights. You need a free key from Google, and most form plugins walk you through adding it. Older checkbox versions work too, but they add friction, and every bit of friction loses a few real visitors, so use the invisible version first and only switch to a visible check if the spam is severe.

A third layer is a simple human question on the form itself, such as asking the visitor to type the name of your city, or to answer a one-word question only a person in your area would know. Use this sparingly, because it is also friction, but for a local business it is effective: a bot in another country rarely knows that your town is called Oakville. And keep your email address off the page itself. Publishing an address in plain text invites spam from bots that harvest addresses, which is one more reason the form is better than an exposed address in the first place.

Finally, check your spam folder for the first week after you add protection, and check it again after any change. Spam filters are blunt instruments. A genuine enquiry from a new customer can look like spam to an aggressive filter, especially if the message contains a link or a word the filter dislikes. Marking a real enquiry as not spam teaches the filter, and catching one lost message in the first week is worth more than a month of perfect filtering that quietly eats a real lead.

Step 5: Tell the visitor what happens next

A form that swallows a message and shows nothing in return leaves the visitor wondering whether it worked. Every form needs a confirmation, and the confirmation needs to say two things: that the message arrived, and when the visitor will hear back.

The simplest version is a short message on the same page: "Thanks for writing. We have received your message and we will reply within one business day." That is enough, and it is far better than a bare "Message sent." If your form tool supports it, send the same confirmation by email to the visitor, so they have a record and a copy of what they wrote. That email should come from your business address, not a no-reply address, so the visitor can reply to it directly if they remember something else.

A thank-you page is a step up from an on-page message, and it is worth doing if your form tool supports it. Instead of showing a message on the contact page, the form sends the visitor to a simple page that says the message arrived, repeats the reply promise, and offers a next step, such as your phone number for anything urgent, or a link to your services page. A thank-you page also makes it easy to see in your analytics how many forms were submitted, because each visit to that page is one completed enquiry. Our guide on setting up Google Analytics explains how to track that properly.

Whatever you choose, keep the promise you make in the confirmation. If you say one business day, reply within one business day. If you cannot, change the wording to two business days rather than breaking the promise. A visitor who waits three days for a reply that was promised in one will assume you do not care, and they will contact the next business while they wait. The reply time you advertise is a commitment, and the form is where you make it.

Privacy in Canada: what to get right before you go live

A contact form collects personal information, and Canadian privacy law treats that seriously. A form submission can contain a name, an email address, a phone number, and details about a person's home, health, or finances, depending on your business. That is personal information under PIPEDA, the federal privacy law that applies to most Canadian businesses, and under provincial laws in Quebec, British Columbia, and Alberta where they apply.

Three things cover most of what a small business needs to do. First, say so in your privacy policy: that your site has a contact form, what information it collects, why you collect it, and how long you keep it. If you do not have a privacy policy yet, adding a form is a good reason to write one. Second, add a short line near the form's submit button, such as "We use your details only to reply to your message. See our privacy policy." That is the consent a visitor needs to give, and it is also reassurance: people are more willing to share details when they can see what happens to them. Third, keep submissions only as long as you need them. A form message from two years ago about a quote that never turned into a job does not need to sit in your inbox or your dashboard forever. Delete old submissions on a schedule, for example once a year, and say so in your privacy policy.

One more caution: check where your form data is stored. Many popular form tools and form-handling services store submissions on servers outside Canada, usually in the United States. That is not forbidden, but your privacy policy should say so, and you should know it before a customer asks. If your business handles sensitive information, such as health or financial details, this matters more, and it is worth choosing a tool that stores data in Canada or lets you choose the region.

Common mistakes that cost enquiries

Most contact forms fail in the same few ways, and all of them are easy to avoid once you know to look for them.

Too many fields. The most common mistake. A form that asks for a full address, a budget, a timeline, a referral source, and a file upload before the visitor has even said hello will lose most of the people who start it. Ask for the four fields above, and collect the rest in your reply. You can always ask for more detail once the visitor has decided they want to talk to you.

No reply promise, or a broken one. A form with no confirmation message leaves the visitor guessing. A form that promises a reply within one business day and delivers it in four leaves the visitor feeling ignored. Say when you will reply, and keep it.

A form nobody tests. Forms break quietly after hosting changes, plugin updates, and email provider changes. A form that has not been tested in months may have been failing for weeks. Test it monthly, and after every change to the site.

A form that is painful on a phone. More than half of your visitors are on a phone, and a form that is hard to use on a small screen loses them. Open your contact page on a real phone after publishing the form. Check that the fields are large enough to tap, that the email field brings up an email keyboard, that the phone field brings up a number keyboard, and that the submit button is easy to reach without zooming. Our guide on making your website mobile friendly covers the full mobile checklist.

No spam protection, or protection that blocks real people. A form with no protection drowns in spam. A form with an aggressive captcha that makes visitors solve three puzzles loses real enquiries. Start with a honeypot, add invisible reCAPTCHA only if you need it, and check your spam folder for the first week.

Hiding the form. A form buried at the bottom of a long contact page, or only reachable from a tiny link in the footer, will not get used. Put the form on the contact page where it is visible without scrolling far, and link to the contact page from your main navigation and from the bottom of your service pages.

Contact form vs live chat vs phone: which one do you need?

A contact form is not the only way for visitors to reach you, and it is not always the best one. The three channels serve different visitors, and most small businesses need at least two of them.

The form is for the visitor who is not in a hurry and wants to write a proper message. The phone is for the visitor who wants an answer now and is ready to talk. Live chat sits between the two: it is for the visitor who has one quick question and wants an answer in minutes without a phone call. A chatbot, which our guide on whether to add a chatbot covers, is for the questions that have the same answer every time, such as your hours or your starting price.

If you can only set up one channel, the form is the right first choice for most businesses, because it works at every hour, it costs nothing, and it does not need anyone watching it. Add a phone number alongside it, because some visitors will always call. Add live chat later, when you have someone who can answer within a few minutes during business hours. The combination covers almost every visitor: the patient one writes, the impatient one calls, and the curious one chats.

What to do in the first 30 days

The first month with a new form is a trial, and you should treat it like one. Each week, look at three things: how many messages arrived, how many of those turned into a booking, a quote, or a phone call, and how long you took to reply to each one. Your email and your form dashboard give you all three numbers without any extra tools.

If messages are arriving but not turning into work, read them again. The usual causes are a slow first reply, a reply that asks for information the form should have collected, or a reply that stops short of offering a next step. Fix the reply, not the form. If almost no messages arrive, check the obvious first: is the form visible on the contact page, is the submit button working on a phone, and is the contact page linked from your navigation. Then check the delivery chain with a test message, because a form that looks fine but sends nothing is the quietest failure there is.

After a month you will know whether the form is earning its place. A healthy sign is a steady trickle of messages that turn into real enquiries, even if the total is small. Three messages a week that produce one or two jobs is a good result for a small business. A hundred spam messages a week and no real ones is a sign the spam protection needs work, not a sign the form is useless. Either way, the messages tell you what your visitors want, in their own words, and that is market research you did not have to pay for. If the same question appears in three different form messages, the answer belongs on your website, on the relevant service page or in a new blog post, so the next visitor does not have to ask.

Frequently asked questions

How do I add a contact form to my website for free?

On WordPress, install a free form plugin such as WPForms Lite or Contact Form 7, build a short form, and place it on your contact page. On Wix, Squarespace, or Shopify, use the form block or form app that is already built into the platform. On a custom-built site, connect the form to a form-handling service such as Formspree or Basin, which have free plans for low volumes. In every case, point the form at an email address you check every day and send yourself a test message before you call it done.

Where do contact form messages go?

They go to the email address you set in the form's notification settings, ideally a professional address at your own domain such as hello@yourbusiness.ca. Most WordPress form plugins also save a copy of every submission inside your website dashboard, which is a useful backup if an email is lost or lands in spam. Check both places for the first few weeks until you trust the delivery.

How do I stop spam from my contact form?

Start with a honeypot field, which most form tools include for free: it is an invisible field that only bots fill in, so any submission that fills it is thrown away silently. Add Google reCAPTCHA or a similar check if spam still gets through, and add a confirmation step such as a simple question only a person in your area would know. Keep your email address off the page itself, because published email addresses attract spam, and check your spam folder for the first week so a real enquiry is never lost while you tune the filters.

How quickly should I reply to a contact form message?

Within one business day at the latest, and faster is better. A visitor who fills in a form is usually comparing two or three businesses at once, and the first useful reply often wins the job. Tell visitors on the form's confirmation message when they will hear from you, for example within one business day, and then keep that promise. If you cannot reply that fast, shorten the promise instead of breaking it.
