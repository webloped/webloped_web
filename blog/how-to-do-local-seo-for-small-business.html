How to do local SEO for small business: a practical guide for Canadian businesses

Local SEO is how your business shows up when someone nearby searches for what you do. Here is the full process, in the order that actually moves results, with nothing you need to buy first.

Picture a homeowner in Kitchener with a burst pipe. She does not search for "plumbing theory" or "best plumbers in Canada." She searches for "emergency plumber near me" on her phone, looks at the three businesses in the map results at the top, checks their ratings and hours, and calls one of them. The whole decision takes a few minutes, and the plumbers who were not visible in that moment never got a chance to compete.

That is the entire game of local SEO. It is not about ranking for the whole internet. It is about showing up, looking trustworthy, and being easy to contact at the exact moment someone in your area needs what you sell. A bakery in Saskatoon does not need to outrank bakeries in Halifax. It needs to be the obvious choice for someone searching for a birthday cake in Saskatoon this week.

The good news is that most of local SEO is unglamorous, free, and fully in your control. It is a profile you complete properly, details you keep consistent everywhere, pages on your website that match real local searches, reviews you earn steadily, and a simple monthly habit of checking what is working. This guide walks through each step in the order that matters most, so you can do it yourself or judge fairly whether outside help is worth paying for.

The short answer

Learning how to do local SEO for small business comes down to five jobs, done in this order. First, claim and fully complete your Google Business Profile, because it feeds the map results most local customers see first. Second, make your business name, address, and phone number identical everywhere they appear online. Third, build website pages that match the service-and-city searches your customers actually type. Fourth, earn genuine reviews steadily and reply to all of them. Fifth, track calls, direction requests, and website visits once a month and fix whatever is weakest.

That is the whole system. Everything else, including directory listings, local links, photos, and posts, supports those five jobs. If a task does not help a nearby customer find you, trust you, or contact you more easily, it is probably not worth your time yet. Start with the profile and the consistency work, because those two steps alone fix the most common reason small businesses are invisible in local search: Google is not sure the business is real, open, and located where it claims to be.

How local search actually works

When someone searches with local intent, Google shows two kinds of results. The first is the map pack: the small set of businesses with a map, usually three, shown near the top of the page. The second is the regular organic results below it, where website pages rank. A strong local presence aims at both, because some customers tap a map listing and call immediately, while others scroll past the map and click a website to compare options first.

Google has described local ranking as resting on three ideas: relevance, distance, and prominence. Relevance means how well your profile and website match what the person searched for. Distance means how close your business, or your service area, is to the searcher. Prominence means how well known and trusted your business appears, based on reviews, links, mentions, and general web presence. You cannot change distance, but you control relevance and prominence almost completely, and that is where your effort should go.

One practical consequence follows from this. A business that serves customers at their homes or workplaces, such as a landscaper, a cleaning company, or a mobile mechanic, is judged differently from a shop with a storefront. Google lets service-area businesses hide their home address and list the areas they serve instead, which protects privacy while still allowing them to appear in local results. If you work from home, do not publish your home address just because a form asks for one. Set up the service area properly, and put your phone number and service area clearly on your website instead.

Step 1: claim and complete your Google Business Profile

Your Google Business Profile is the single highest-impact local SEO task, and it is free. Search for your business name on Google and Google Maps. If a profile already exists but you have never claimed it, claim it and verify ownership, usually by a mailed postcard, a phone or email code, or a video verification, depending on what Google offers your business type. If no profile exists, create one. Until you own the profile, anyone can suggest edits to it, and you cannot reply to reviews, post updates, or correct wrong hours.

Once you own it, complete every field honestly. Use your real business name exactly as it appears on your storefront, your website, and your signage. Do not add extra keywords or city names to the business name field, such as "Best Plumber Toronto Cheap." Google treats that as spam, competitors can report it, and it can get the profile suspended. Choose the most specific primary category available for what you actually do, then add secondary categories only if they describe real services you offer.

Write the description in plain language: what you do, who you serve, and where you operate, in the words a customer would use. Add your correct hours, including holiday hours when they differ, and keep them updated when they change, because wrong hours are one of the fastest ways to lose a customer who drove to a closed shop. Add your phone number, your website address, and the services you offer with short descriptions. Then add real photos: the outside of your premises so people recognize it, the inside, your team at work, and examples of finished work. Stock photos are better than nothing, but real photos build far more trust, and customers notice the difference immediately.

Finally, use the features most owners ignore. Post short updates about offers, new services, seasonal hours, or events, roughly once a week if you can manage it. Add common questions and answers to the questions section before customers ask them, such as whether you offer free quotes, whether parking is available, or whether appointments are required. Turn on messaging only if someone will actually answer it promptly, because an unanswered message is worse than no message button at all. Step 2: keep your name, address, and phone number identical everywhere

Search engines compare your business details across the web to confirm you are one real business. When your name, address, and phone number, often shortened to NAP, appear in slightly different forms on different sites, Google becomes less confident, and your rankings suffer. This is one of the most common and most fixable local SEO problems for Canadian small businesses, because details change over time: a phone number changes, a business moves, a suite number gets added, or a partner creates a listing with a nickname version of the name.

Pick one canonical version of each detail and use it everywhere. That means the same business name, the same street address format, and the same phone number on your website, your Google Business Profile, Bing Places, Apple Maps, Yelp, Yellow Pages, your social media profiles, and any industry directories for your trade. If you moved, update the old listings rather than creating new ones, because duplicate listings split your reviews and confuse both customers and search engines. If you find a duplicate or an incorrect listing, claim it and correct it, or request its removal through that directory.

Do not stop at the big directories. Check the places customers in your industry actually look: trade associations, local chambers of commerce, supplier "find a dealer" pages, and community business listings in your city or town. You do not need hundreds of listings. A few dozen accurate, consistent listings beat hundreds of sloppy ones, and listings you never update do more harm than listings you never create. Set aside an afternoon to audit the main ones, keep a simple document with the login for each listing, and re-check them whenever your details change.

One Canadian detail is worth noting. If you serve customers in both English and French, or you operate in Quebec, make sure your business name and key details are consistent in both languages rather than creating two conflicting versions of the business. A bilingual description on your profile is helpful, but the business name itself should stay the same everywhere so search engines and customers recognize it as one business.

Step 3: build website pages that match local searches

Your Google Business Profile gets you into the map results, but your website is what wins the organic results below the map and convinces a comparison shopper to choose you. The core mistake most small business websites make is having one page that tries to cover everything: one "Services" page listing ten services in ten cities. Google cannot tell which search that page is for, so it ranks it for none of them well.

The fix is simple in structure, even though it takes writing effort. Create one page per main service, and where you genuinely serve multiple cities, consider one page per service-and-city combination that you actually serve. A page titled "Furnace Repair in Cambridge" with real details about the service, the process, the service area, and how to book will outperform a generic services page for someone searching exactly that phrase. But do not create a page for a city you do not serve, and do not copy the same page ten times with only the city name swapped. Thin, duplicated city pages are easy for Google to spot, and they make your business look less trustworthy to the humans who land on them.

Each local page should answer the questions a customer in that city would ask before calling. What exactly is included in the service? How long does it take? What does the first visit or consultation look like? Which neighbourhoods or nearby towns do you serve? How can someone book, and how quickly will you respond? Include your phone number, your service area, and a short contact form on the page itself, so the visitor never has to hunt for the next step. Our guide to what pages a small business website should have covers the core structure that supports these local pages.

Two technical details are worth getting right. First, put the service and the city in the page title and the main heading, in natural language, such as "Snow Removal in Mississauga" rather than stuffing five keyword variations into the title. Second, add your business name, address or service area, phone number, and hours to your website in a consistent place, usually the footer and the contact page, so both visitors and search engines can verify them. If your developer handles the technical side, ask them to add LocalBusiness structured data to your homepage with those same details, which is a small piece of code that helps search engines understand exactly what your business is and where it operates.

Local content also earns its place here. A short blog post answering a question your local customers ask, such as when to winterize pipes in Ontario, which permits a deck needs in your municipality, or what to check before hiring a contractor in your province, gives Google a relevant page to show and gives a visitor a reason to trust you before they call. Write about your actual area and your actual work, not generic advice copied from elsewhere, and one useful local post a month beats ten generic posts nobody in your city would search for.

Step 4: earn reviews steadily, and reply to all of them

Reviews do three jobs at once in local SEO. They influence whether Google shows you prominently, they influence whether a customer who finds you chooses you, and they tell you what customers actually value about your work. A business with a steady stream of recent, specific reviews looks alive. A business with five reviews from three years ago looks uncertain, even if the work is excellent.

Build review requests into the end of every job, not into a once-a-year campaign. The simplest system works best: after a job is finished and the customer is happy, send a short message with a direct link to your Google review page and one sentence asking them to share their experience. Do it the same day, while the experience is fresh. Ask every happy customer, not just the ones you are sure will leave five stars, because cherry-picking only your happiest customers is both less honest and less useful than a genuine mix that shows how you handle real work.

Never buy reviews, never offer a discount or a gift in exchange for a positive review, and never post reviews for your own business or ask friends and family to do so. Google removes fake reviews, competitors can report them, and in Canada, misleading reviews can also raise issues under the Competition Act rules against deceptive marketing. The same honesty applies in the other direction: never post fake negative reviews about a competitor. It is the kind of shortcut that costs far more than it gains.

Reply to every review, good and bad, within a few days. Thank positive reviewers by name and mention something specific about their job, which shows future readers that real people had real experiences. For negative reviews, stay calm and professional in public, acknowledge the concern without arguing, and move the detailed conversation offline by inviting the reviewer to call or email you directly. Our guides to getting more Google reviews and responding to a negative Google review cover the scripts and the privacy points in detail.

Step 5: earn local links and mentions

Links from other websites to yours act as votes of confidence, and for local SEO, links from local and relevant sources count the most. One link from your city's chamber of commerce, a local newspaper story about your business, a supplier's dealer page, or a community organization's sponsor page is worth more than dozens of links from unrelated directories you have never heard of.

Earn these links by doing things worth mentioning. Sponsor a youth sports team or a community event and make sure the organizer links to your website from their sponsor page. Partner with a complementary local business, such as a realtor recommending a home inspector, and link to each other where it genuinely helps customers. Offer a local publication or community site something useful: expert comments for a story about your trade, a short guide for homeowners in your city, or photos and details of an interesting local project, with permission from the customer. Join your local business association and make sure your member listing links to your site.

What not to do matters as much. Do not buy links in bulk, do not join link schemes that promise hundreds of links for a monthly fee, and do not submit your site to every directory that emails you. Low-quality links can hurt more than they help, and cleaning them up is slow work. If an offer sounds like a shortcut around earning trust, it almost always is, and the money is better spent on real work that a local site would want to mention.

Step 6: track results once a month, and fix the weakest link

Local SEO fails quietly when nobody checks it. Set a monthly reminder, thirty minutes is enough, and look at a small set of numbers that reflect real business, not vanity metrics. From your Google Business Profile, check how many people called, requested directions, visited your website, or messaged you, and which searches showed your profile. From your website, check which pages brought in visitors and which pages led to contact form submissions or calls. Our guide to setting up Google Search Console shows how to see the actual searches that brought people to your site, which is free and more useful than guessing.

Then fix the weakest link, not everything at once. If your profile gets seen but nobody calls, the problem is usually trust or clarity: photos, reviews, hours, or the description. If people call but your website gets almost no local visits, the problem is usually your service pages or your page titles. If you get website visits but few enquiries, the problem is usually the offer, the form, or the next step on the page. One fix a month, measured the following month, compounds into a strong local presence within a year.

Keep a simple one-page record of what you changed and what happened. Note the date you updated your profile, the date you published a new service page, and the date you started asking for reviews, alongside the monthly calls and enquiries. Without that record, you will not know which change helped, and you will be tempted to pay for a new tactic every time someone sells you one.

What not to do: the shortcuts that backfire

Local SEO has a long list of tempting shortcuts, and most of them backfire in ways that take months to repair. Stuffing keywords into your business name can get your profile suspended. Fake reviews get removed and can damage your reputation when customers notice the pattern. Creating listings at addresses where you do not operate, such as a rented mailbox or a virtual office you never use, violates Google's rules for service-area businesses and risks suspension of the whole profile. Promising a customer a discount for a positive review, rather than for an honest review, crosses into misleading marketing territory.

Be equally careful with anyone who guarantees you the number one spot on Google, promises results in thirty days, or refuses to explain what they will actually do for your money. No honest provider can guarantee a specific ranking, because rankings depend on your competitors, on the searcher's location, and on Google's own changes. A legitimate helper will explain their plan in the same plain steps this guide uses, report on calls and enquiries rather than only on rankings, and let you keep ownership of your profile, your website, and your listings. If a provider wants to own your Google Business Profile or register your domain in their name, that is a reason to walk away, not a convenience to accept.

How long does local SEO take, and what does it cost?

Local SEO is a slow build with a long payoff, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. Completing your profile, fixing your listings, and publishing your first service pages can be done in a few focused weeks. Visible improvement in map and organic results usually takes a few months, because Google needs time to re-check your updated details, accumulate new reviews, and compare your pages against competitors who have been building for years. Businesses in competitive cities and trades, such as plumbing in Toronto or dentistry in Vancouver, should expect the longer end of that range, while businesses in smaller towns with fewer competitors often see movement sooner.

Cost follows the same honest pattern. Doing it yourself costs mainly your time: the profile, the listings, and the review system are free, and the main tools, including Google Search Console and your profile's own insights, are free as well. Paid tools for tracking rankings or auditing listings exist, but you do not need them to start, and you should not buy one until you know which question it answers for you. Hiring help costs whatever the provider charges in CAD for the scope agreed, so compare proposals on the same basis: which steps they will do, how they will report results, and whether you keep ownership of everything they build. A proposal that lists the steps in this guide, with reporting on calls and enquiries, is a far better sign than a proposal that promises a ranking and explains nothing.

If budget is tight, spend it in the order of this guide. A complete profile and consistent listings come before paid ads, because ads sent to a business with wrong hours, no reviews, and no clear contact step waste the ad budget. A website with proper local pages comes before a fancy redesign, because a beautiful site that Google cannot match to local searches will not be found. A simple monthly routine to keep it working

Local SEO is mostly maintenance once the setup is done. Here is a routine that takes under an hour a month and keeps the whole system healthy. First, check your profile: are the hours right, are there new reviews to reply to, and is there a new photo or post worth adding? Second, check your website's local pages: is the phone number still correct, is the service area still accurate, and is there a question customers keep asking that deserves a paragraph or a new post? Third, ask for reviews from the past month's happy customers if you have not already, and follow up once with anyone who said they would leave one.

Fourth, look at the month's numbers: profile calls and direction requests, website visits from local searches, and enquiries. Fifth, do one improvement: fix the weakest page, add one local post, correct one listing, or earn one local link. That is it. Businesses that follow this routine for a year almost always outperform businesses that pay for a burst of activity and then ignore local search for eleven months, because Google rewards businesses that look consistently open, active, and trusted.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a website for local SEO, or is a Google Business Profile enough?

A profile alone can win you map visibility, and some businesses do get calls from the profile alone. But a website wins the organic results below the map, answers the questions a comparison shopper asks before calling, and gives you a place to publish service pages, local content, and booking steps that a profile cannot hold. For most businesses, the profile and the website work as a pair: the profile gets you found, and the website gets you chosen.

How long does local SEO take to work?

Expect a few months before you see meaningful movement, with the exact timing depending on your city, your trade, and how strong your competitors already are. Profile fixes and listing corrections can improve how you appear within weeks, but rankings, reviews, and local links accumulate over months. Treat anyone who promises a top ranking in thirty days as a warning sign, not a selling point.

Should I do local SEO or Google Ads?

They do different jobs, and many businesses eventually use both. Ads buy visibility immediately and stop the day you stop paying, which makes them useful for a launch, a seasonal push, or a competitive market. Local SEO builds visibility that keeps working without paying for every click, but it takes months to build. If you need customers this week, ads plus a strong profile and a focused landing page are the faster path. If you want customers finding you month after month without an ad budget, local SEO is the investment that pays for itself over time.

Can I do local SEO myself, or should I hire someone?

Most owners can do the core steps themselves: claiming and completing the profile, keeping details consistent, asking for reviews, and publishing one good service page at a time. Hire help when the work stalls because of time, technical details, or competition: a developer for structured data and site speed, a writer for service pages and local content, or an SEO provider for a competitive market. Whatever you hire out, keep ownership of your profile, your domain, and your website, and ask for reporting on calls and enquiries, not just rankings.
