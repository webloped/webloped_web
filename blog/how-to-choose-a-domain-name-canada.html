How to choose a domain name for your small business in Canada

Your domain is the one address customers will remember, type, and say out loud. Here is how to pick one that is memorable, trustworthy, and Canadian-appropriate - plus what to do the moment you register it.

A domain name is a small decision with a long half-life. You will print it on your truck, put it on invoices, say it on the phone hundreds of times, and - if it is any good - hear customers repeat it back to you. A bad one is annoying forever; a good one you never think about again.

The good news: choosing a domain name is not a creative-writing contest. There is a short list of practical rules, and the Canadian angle (the .ca question, CIRA's rules, and a few local pitfalls) is easy to get right once you know it. This guide covers the naming rules that actually matter, the .ca vs .com decision, the legal check people skip, and the five minutes of admin that protect your name after you register it.

First: what a domain does and does not do for you

Let's set expectations. A domain name:

  • Is your address, not your advertising. Customers find you through Google, your Google Business Profile, referrals, and signage - not by guessing your URL. The domain's job is to be easy to remember and hard to get wrong when they hear it.
  • Carries trust signals. A domain that matches your business name, uses a sensible extension, and powers your email address (you@yourbusiness.ca instead of yourbusiness123@gmail.com) makes you look established. Our guide on getting your business on Google explains the other half of that first-impression equation.
  • Is not an SEO cheat code. Years ago, stuffing keywords into a domain helped rankings. Today Google treats an exact-match-keyword domain as only a tiny signal at best. A domain like toronto-best-cheap-plumber.ca will not outrank a real business with real reviews. Pick for humans, not for algorithms.
  • Outlives everything else. You will redesign the website (see what a redesign costs), change hosting companies, maybe even rebrand the logo. The domain stays. Treat it like a lease you plan to keep for decades: register it in your own name, at an account you control.

.ca or .com? The Canadian decision

This is the question every Canadian owner asks, so let's settle it:

  • .ca is Canada's country domain, run by the Canadian Internet Registration Authority (CIRA). It tells visitors and search engines you are Canadian, which builds trust with Canadian customers and gives your site a small local-relevance signal in Canadian search results. Research on Canadian shopping behaviour has consistently shown Canadians prefer buying from Canadian sites - a .ca address reinforces that.
  • .com is universally recognized and the default assumption worldwide. If you sell or plan to sell outside Canada, .com is the safer primary choice.

For a typical Canadian small business that serves Canadian customers, the default answer is .ca as your main address, plus the matching .com registered and redirected to it. Both usually cost in the low tens of CAD per year, so owning both is cheap insurance against competitors, imitators, or simple customer confusion.

One Canadian-specific catch: you cannot always register a .ca domain. CIRA requires registrants to meet Canadian Presence Requirements - roughly, you must be a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, a business or organization registered in Canada, or hold a Canadian trademark, among other qualifying categories. Most Canadian small businesses qualify without thinking about it. But two practical consequences follow:

  • Do not let a developer or agency overseas register your .ca domain in their name to be "helpful." If they do not meet the requirements, or if you later lose access to their account, recovering the domain is painful. The registrant on a .ca domain must be you or your business.
  • When you register, CIRA requires accurate registrant contact details. Keep them current - if your email dies, so does your ability to prove ownership when it matters.

The 9 rules for picking the name itself

With the extension settled, here is how to choose the part before the dot:

  1. Match your business name, as closely as possible. If your business is Maple Leaf Plumbing, mapleleafplumbing.ca is the obvious choice and mapleleaf.ca is the dream. Customers search your name; make the domain the same thing. Every gap between your name and your domain is a customer who ends up on the wrong site.
  2. Shorter beats clever. Aim for under 15 characters before the dot. Long domains get mistyped, misheard, and truncated on business cards and truck decals. If your legal name is long, use the short version customers actually say.
  3. Easy to spell beats cute. Avoid creative spellings (kwik, kutz, fyne). If a customer has to ask "with a K or a C?" the name has failed its one job. This is also why hyphens and numbers are trouble: "is that a dash or an underscore?" and "the number 4 or the word four?" are conversations you will have forever. Skip them if you can.
  4. Do the radio test. Say the domain out loud, the way you would say it on a phone call. Then imagine a customer typing what they heard. If it survives that trip - no confusion, no "can you spell that?" - it passes.
  5. Check the mashup. Read the domain as one unbroken string and look for accidental words. Removing spaces can create surprises (the classic cautionary tales are real), so stare at the joined letters before you buy.
  6. Keywords are fine in moderation. Adding a word that says what you do (mapleleafplumbing.ca instead of mapleleaf.ca) helps a stranger understand the business at a glance. Just do not turn it into a keyword salad - one clarifying word is plenty.
  7. Future-proof it. Naming your domain after one service ("oakville-gutter-cleaning.ca") boxes you in when you add services or cities. Prefer the business name over the current offering. Expanding later is much cheaper than rebranding a domain.
  8. Check trademark conflicts before you fall in love. Search the Canadian Trademarks Database (free, run by the Canadian Intellectual Property Office) for your proposed name. A domain that infringes someone else's registered trademark can be taken from you through dispute proceedings - and reprinting everything with a new name costs far more than a five-minute search. Also search Google and your provincial business registry to make sure you are not colliding with an existing local business.
  9. Check the social handles too. You do not need identical handles everywhere, but before committing, check that a reasonable version of the name is free on the platforms your customers use. Discovering your name is taken on Instagram after you have printed 5,000 business cards is an avoidable annoyance.

What if the name you want is taken?

Good .ca and .com names for real businesses get taken. Do not panic, and do not immediately buy a weird alternative. Work through this order:

  • Try the other extension. If mapleleafplumbing.ca is taken but the .com is free (or vice versa), grab the free one. Remember the plan above: own both when possible.
  • Add a small qualifier. A city or service word usually solves it: mapleleafplumbingontario.ca, mapleleafplumbingco.ca. Keep it short and sayable.
  • Check who owns it. A WHOIS lookup (CIRA's lookup for .ca) tells you whether the name is actively used or just parked. A parked domain is sometimes for sale - but be careful: never offer more than the name is worth to you, and buy through a proper escrow or transfer process, not a wire to a stranger's email.
  • Do not buy a hyphenated version as your main address. maple-leaf-plumbing.ca as a backup redirect is fine. As the name on your truck, it is a lifetime of spelling it out loud. Only go here if nothing else works.
  • Never squat on someone else's brand. Registering a domain to sell it to the trademark owner, or registering a confusingly similar version of a competitor's name, can be taken from you and can create legal exposure. Pick a name that is yours.

After you register: the 5-minute setup that protects you

Registration takes minutes; the admin around it is what most owners skip and later regret. Do these before you do anything else:

  1. Turn on auto-renew and keep a valid payment method. Letting a domain expire is the most common way businesses lose one. Registrars send renewal reminders, but they go to the email on file - which is exactly the email people let die. Auto-renew plus a current card removes the risk.
  2. Register it in your name, on your account. If a developer, agency, or "marketing friend" offers to register it for you, say thanks and do it yourself. Domain disputes almost always trace back to someone else holding the keys. Our guide on what to ask a web designer before hiring includes ownership questions worth asking on day one.
  3. Use registrar locking and, where available, two-factor authentication. Domain hijacking is rare but devastating: with your registrar password, someone can point your domain anywhere. A registrar lock plus 2FA makes that dramatically harder.
  4. Set up professional email on the domain. You can usually add email hosting at your registrar or through Google Workspace or Microsoft 365. info@mapleleafplumbing.ca on an invoice reads very differently from a free email address, and it costs a few dollars a month.
  5. Register for multiple years if the price is right. Multi-year registration (up to 10 years for .ca) locks in today's price and removes annual renewal risk. It is not an SEO advantage despite old myths - it is just convenient.

Watch out for domain scams

Once you own a domain, your contact details exist in public records, and scammers know it. The classics targeting Canadian businesses:

  • Fake renewal notices. Official-looking letters or emails warning your domain "expires soon" and demanding payment - from a company that is not your registrar, at an inflated price. This is called domain slamming. Real renewals happen in your registrar account, not via a stranger's invoice. When in doubt, log in to your registrar directly instead of clicking anything in the message.
  • "Someone is trying to register your name" emails. A supposed Chinese or overseas registrar claims a third party is applying for your brand name as a domain and offers to sell you "priority registration" first. It is a pressure tactic. Ignore it.
  • Trademark upsell calls. Callers claiming to be from a trademark office offering to "protect" your domain name for a fee. Canada's trademark registration goes through CIPO, not cold callers.

The rule of thumb: your domain is managed at your registrar, period. Anything that asks you to pay or act somewhere else is suspect until proven otherwise.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a .ca domain or is .com fine for a Canadian business?

Either can work. A .ca domain signals to visitors and search engines that you are Canadian, which helps trust and local relevance with Canadian customers. A .com is universally recognized and better if you sell outside Canada. The safest move is to register both and point the .com at your .ca site (or vice versa) - each usually costs in the low tens of CAD per year, so it is cheap insurance against competitors or lookalikes grabbing the other one.

Can anyone register a .ca domain?

Not quite anyone. CIRA, Canada's domain registry, requires registrants to meet Canadian Presence Requirements - for example, being a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, a corporation or organization registered in Canada, or holding a Canadian trademark. Most Canadian small business owners qualify easily. But it means you should not have a developer or agency outside Canada register your .ca domain in their name - the registrant should be you or your business.

How much does a domain name cost in Canada?

A standard .ca or .com domain usually costs in the low tens of Canadian dollars per year at most registrars - roughly CAD $15 to $25 per year. Premium or already-taken names can cost much more on the aftermarket. What really adds up is everything around the domain: hosting, email, and especially failing to renew, which can cost you the name itself. Multi-year registration locks in the price and removes the annual risk.

Should I buy all the versions of my domain (net, org, misspellings)?

You do not need to buy every extension ever created. A sensible minimum is your .ca and your .com, plus any obvious misspelling of your business name if it is easy to get wrong. Beyond that, save your money - what matters far more is renewing what you own, keeping your contact details current, and using the domain consistently so customers learn one address.
