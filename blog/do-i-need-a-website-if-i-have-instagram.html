Do I need a website if I have Instagram?

Short answer: keep posting on Instagram, but give it somewhere to send people. Here is the honest breakdown of when Instagram is enough, when it quietly costs you customers, and the right order to do both.

You have 2,000 followers, you post Reels twice a week, and the DMs keep coming. Why would you pay for a website on top of that? It is one of the most common questions Canadian small business owners ask, and it is completely fair. A website costs money, takes time, and Instagram feels free.

But this is the wrong comparison. Instagram and a website do different jobs. Thinking of it as either/or is what costs people money: they keep the Instagram that gives them reach and skip the website that would have closed the sale.

The customer journey is not one platform

Nobody discovers you, checks your reviews, compares your prices, and buys in a single Instagram tap. Research from GWI, the audience insights firm (summarized by Squarespace), found that the typical adult internet user now discovers brands and products through an average of 5.8 different channels. A customer might see your Reel, search your business name on Google, check your reviews, and then look for your website before taking any action.

Every time one of those steps finds nothing, you lose people. And the numbers are consistent: BrightLocal's 2026 consumer survey, reported by RedFork Marketing, found that 54% of consumers visit a business's website after reading positive reviews. Your reviews live on Google; the moment those reviews win someone over, more than half of them go looking for a website. If there is none, the journey stalls.

The useful way to frame it: social media is where people find you. Your website is where they decide. Instagram is genuinely good at reach. It is just not good at closing, because you cannot build anything on it.

Instagram is rented ground

Your Instagram profile lives inside Meta's walls, and Meta sets the rules:

  • The algorithm decides who sees you. A small percentage of your own followers see any given post, and Meta changes the rules regularly. Your best-performing content today can be buried by tomorrow's update.
  • Competitors sit on your page. Sponsored posts and suggested accounts run right next to your profile. Someone who tapped on your Reel can be one swipe away from your competitor.
  • Your account can vanish. Suspended, hacked, or restricted accounts are not rare. One bad-flag incident and years of audience building are gone. You cannot appeal to a website the way you can appeal to Meta.
  • The format is fixed. Bio, grid, Reels, stories. That is it. You cannot lay out your pricing, service areas, booking flow, policies, and proof in the order that convinces a customer. A website has no such limits.

None of this means you should leave Instagram. It means Instagram should not be the only thing you own online. A website is the one place on the internet that is fully yours: nobody can suspend it, the algorithm cannot bury it, and the layout serves your customers instead of Meta's advertisers.

Google finds websites, not Instagram grids

Here is the discovery problem most Instagram-only businesses do not see. When someone in your city needs what you sell, they Google it: "lash tech scarborough", "fence repair brampton", "custom cakes near me". Google returns websites and map listings, not Instagram grids.

You can be the best in your trade with 10,000 followers and still be invisible to every customer who never heard of you. Those are the expensive customers to lose, because people who search Google are not browsing for fun - they are comparing options and picking one. If your business has no website, you are not in the comparison at all. Your competitors with a simple, clear site are.

There is also a credibility gap. Roughly eight in ten people check a business online before they buy, book, or even pick up the phone (a figure widely reported across consumer surveys). Most expect a real website. A social profile that might not be updated, might be run by anyone, and shows you at your most casual does not carry the same weight. A tidy site with your services, prices, real photos, and a contact method says you are a proper business.

What you actually need is smaller than you think

The reason many business owners stall on this decision is that they imagine a website as a fifty-page corporate monster. It is not. The website that pairs with your Instagram can be small. It needs to answer the questions people ask before they pick up the phone:

  • What you do - one page per service, written the way customers search for it.
  • Where you do it - your city or service area, stated plainly so Google and customers know.
  • How pricing works - a price range, starting prices, or how you quote. "DM for prices" on Instagram is where inquiries die.
  • Proof - real photos of your work, your team, your space. The good photos can be the same ones you already post on Instagram.
  • How to reach you - a phone number with tap-to-call, a simple contact or booking form.

That is it. A focused one-to-five-page site that loads fast and reads clearly on a phone beats both no website and an overbuilt one. The right order: do both, in this sequence

If you are starting from Instagram-only, do not tear anything down. Add layers in the order that pays off fastest:

  1. Claim and complete your Google Business Profile. It is free, and it is often the highest-return hour you will spend this quarter. It is what puts you on the map when someone searches nearby.
  2. Get a real website, even a small one. What you do, where you do it, how pricing works, how to book. The cost breakdown is in our Canadian website pricing guide - for a small credibility site, custom builds commonly start in the low four figures in CAD, and DIY builders run $20 to $60 a month if you are comfortable building it yourself.
  3. Link Instagram to the website, both ways. Put the website link in your bio (and your link-in-bio tool if you use one), and embed or link your Instagram feed on the site so visitors see you are active. Your best Instagram content - finished projects, behind-the-scenes, reviews screenshotted from DMs - gets a second life on the site, where the platform gets no credit and your customers stay in your space.
  4. Keep posting on Instagram. Nothing changes there. Now, though, the link in your bio goes somewhere that answers questions, takes bookings, and does not compete with suggested posts for your visitor's attention. The window now has a shop behind it.

This order matters because each step makes the next one stronger. The Google profile sends people to the website; the website captures the people Instagram and Google send it; Instagram keeps the top of the funnel full. None of them does all three jobs.

When you genuinely do not need a website yet

Honest answer: there are businesses that can wait, and telling you otherwise would be a sales pitch, not advice.

  • You are fully booked on word of mouth and turning work away. A website will not help you turn away more work. Spend the effort on serving the customers you have.
  • You sell entirely through a marketplace - Etsy, Amazon, a delivery app - that already handles discovery, payment, and reviews, and you are happy with those terms.
  • You are testing an idea and do not yet know if it is a business. Prove demand first; a website does not validate anything.

Even in all three cases, do one thing: register the domain name. Roughly $15 to $25 a year for a .ca or .com, it stops someone else from taking your business name, and it is sitting there ready the day you decide you want a site. Register it yourself so you own it. That is the cheapest decision in this entire article.

FAQ

Is Instagram enough to get found on Google?

No. Google indexes Instagram profiles, but when people search for a service in their city, Google returns websites and map listings - not social grids. An Instagram profile also gives Google almost nothing to work with: no service pages, no structured details, no site content. A website gives you pages that can rank for the exact searches your customers type, and it strengthens the Google Business Profile that puts you on the map.

Can I just put all my prices and info in my Instagram bio?

A bio holds a few links and 150 characters. It cannot carry service descriptions, price lists, policies, booking options, and proof in a layout that makes sense. Every business that tries the bio-as-website ends up with a link-in-bio tool stacked with dead-end links and customers asking "how much?" in the DMs. A website is where all of that lives in one place, and your bio just points there.

What is the cheapest way to look legitimate online?

In order of return per dollar: a complete, free Google Business Profile with reviews; a domain name registered in your own name (about $15 to $25 a year); and a small, fast website with your services, prices, and contact details. That combination makes you findable, credible, and reachable for a fraction of a month's ad spend. Our pricing page explains how small projects like this are structured, with fixed quotes in CAD.

Do I need a website if I only sell locally, like in one neighbourhood?

Local is the strongest case for a website, not the weakest. Local customers are the ones Googling "near me" searches, and local search results are where a small site wins fastest against competitors who only have social profiles. A single clear page about your service in your area, plus a claimed Google Business Profile, is the entire local playbook.
