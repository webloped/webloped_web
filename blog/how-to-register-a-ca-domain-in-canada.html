How to register a .ca domain in Canada

A .ca domain tells visitors you are Canadian before they read a single word on your site. But you cannot buy one the same way you buy a .com. Here is who qualifies, what it costs in CAD, and the step-by-step process to register a .ca domain in Canada.

The short answer: to register a .ca domain in Canada, you need a real connection to Canada, a name that is available, and a CIRA-certified registrar to sell it to you. The registry that runs .ca, the Canadian Internet Registration Authority (CIRA), only allows people and organizations that meet its Canadian Presence Requirements to hold one. Once you know which category you fall into, the actual purchase takes a few minutes and usually costs somewhere around $10 to $20 CAD per year, depending on the registrar. The details you enter at checkout decide who legally holds the name for years, so they deserve more care than the checkout form suggests.

More than 3.7 million .ca domains are registered, and for a Canadian small business the ending does real work: it signals to visitors that you are local, it is often easier to get a short, clean name on .ca than on .com, and it pairs naturally with a Canadian business address and phone number. This guide walks through the rules, the costs, and the steps in plain language.

What a .ca domain is, and who runs it

The .ca ending is Canada's country-code domain, the same way .uk belongs to the United Kingdom and .au belongs to Australia. It is run by CIRA, a not-for-profit organization that has managed the .ca namespace since 2000. CIRA sets the rules for who can hold a .ca, keeps the master list of every registered .ca name, and can review a registration and cancel it if the holder does not qualify.

One thing that surprises first-time buyers: you do not buy a .ca from CIRA directly. CIRA does not sell names to the public. You register through a CIRA-certified registrar, a company CIRA has approved to sell .ca domains, and that registrar passes your details to the registry. Most of the companies that sell .com names are also certified for .ca, and CIRA publishes a list of certified registrars on its own website.

The main difference from .com is eligibility. Anyone in the world can register a .com. A .ca is reserved for people and organizations with a genuine connection to Canada. That is the whole reason the ending carries trust: a visitor who sees .ca knows the business behind it passed a Canadian test.

Who can register a .ca domain: the Canadian Presence Requirements

CIRA's Canadian Presence Requirements, usually shortened to CPR, list eighteen categories of people and organizations that may register and hold a .ca. When you register, you pick the category that fits you. Most Canadian small businesses fall into one of these:

  • Canadian citizens, including citizens living abroad. You can register using your current overseas address.
  • Permanent residents of Canada who are ordinarily resident here. In CIRA's policy, that means living in Canada for more than 183 days in the twelve months before you apply, and in each twelve-month period after that for as long as you hold the domain.
  • Corporations incorporated under federal law or the law of any province or territory. A numbered company or a named corporation registers under its exact legal corporate name.
  • Partnerships registered under the laws of a province or territory, where more than two-thirds of the partners qualify under the requirements.
  • Trademark owners who hold a trademark registered in Canada. This category only qualifies you for a .ca that contains the exact word part of that trademark.
  • Other Canadian bodies, including trusts, associations, trade unions, political parties, schools, libraries, museums, hospitals, governments, and Indigenous peoples and bands, each with its own conditions.

A sole proprietor usually registers as an individual, under the citizen or permanent resident category. A foreign company with no Canadian entity and no registered Canadian trademark does not qualify, and neither does a Canadian mailing address on its own. You are not audited at the moment of purchase, but CIRA runs a validation process and can ask for documents such as a Canadian passport or birth certificate, a permanent resident card, proof of corporate status, or a trademark registration. If the details do not hold up, CIRA can cancel the domain. So pick the category that is genuinely true, and enter the registrant details exactly as they appear on your ID or incorporation papers.

Step 1: choose the name

A .ca name can use letters, numbers, and hyphens, but it cannot start or end with a hyphen. It can also include French accented characters, such as e with an accent, c with a cedilla, and similar letters. In practice, most businesses stick to plain unaccented letters because they are easier to type, say out loud, and print on a sign or a truck.

Shorter is almost always better. Your business name plus .ca is the obvious first choice, and because .ca has far fewer registrations than .com, a short name that is taken on .com is often still free on .ca. If your exact business name is taken, try a version with your city or your trade in it, such as yournameplumbing.ca or yourname-toronto.ca, rather than a long string of hyphens. Our guide on how to choose a domain name in Canada covers the naming trade-offs in more detail.

Before you fall in love with a name, check the trademark angle. If another Canadian business already owns a registered trademark on that name, registering the matching .ca can create a dispute you do not want. A quick search of the Canadian trademarks database and a Google search of the name plus your city takes five minutes and can save a real headache.

Step 2: check availability

Every registrar has a search box where you type the name and see instantly whether the .ca is free. You can also check with CIRA's own WHOIS lookup, which shows whether a name is registered and, for business registrants, who holds it. For individual registrants, CIRA does not publish personal contact details in the public WHOIS at all, so you will not find someone's home address there.

If the name you want is taken, check whether it is actually in use. Many .ca names are registered and parked, doing nothing. If the owner is a business in a different industry, a polite email asking whether they would sell it sometimes works, though prices for a name someone else already owns are entirely up to them. If the name is taken by a direct competitor, move on to a variation instead of fighting for it.

Step 3: pick a CIRA-certified registrar

Only a certified registrar can sell you a .ca, so your first real choice is where to buy. Compare registrars on the things that cost you later, not just the first-year price:

  • The renewal price. First-year prices are often discounted, and the renewal price is what you will actually pay year after year. A registrar that sells the first year for $5 and renews at $25 costs more over three years than one that charges $15 every year.
  • Auto-renew and expiry policy. A good registrar reminds you before expiry, keeps auto-renew on by default, and gives you a grace period to recover the name if a payment fails. A .ca does not simply vanish the moment it expires; it passes through defined stages that can give you more than two months to recover it, but only if you notice in time.
  • Transfer-out policy. Moving a .ca between registrars uses an authorization code, and a 60-day lock applies after a fresh registration or a previous transfer. A registrar should not make the transfer process hard or charge a fee for it.
  • Support and DNS tools. You will need to point the domain at your hosting, so the registrar's DNS settings should be easy to reach and edit. Canadian support hours help when something goes wrong on a weekday afternoon.

Well-known options that sell .ca domains include Canadian registrars such as Webnames.ca and WHC, and international ones such as Namecheap and GoDaddy. Prices change, so check the current renewal price at two or three registrars before you commit. The difference between the cheapest and the most expensive is usually only a few dollars a year, and the registrar with the clearest renewal and expiry policy is usually the better buy.

Step 4: register, and get the details right

At checkout you will enter the registrant details and select your CPR category. This is the step that matters most, because the registrant is the legal holder of the domain. For a corporation, enter the exact legal name of the company, not the trade name or the brand name, and select the corporation category. For an individual, enter your full legal name and select the citizen or permanent resident category that applies.

Also pay attention to the email address you register with. It should be an address you check regularly and control for the long term, not a personal inbox you might abandon or an employee's address. Password resets, renewal reminders, transfer authorization codes, and CIRA validation notices all go to that address. Losing access to it is one of the most common ways a small business loses a domain.

The registration term can be one to ten years. One year is fine if you are testing an idea, but registering for two or three years is often cheaper per year and removes the annual risk of forgetting to renew. Use a payment method that will still be valid at renewal time, and turn auto-renew on unless you have a deliberate reason not to.

Step 5: connect the domain to your website and email

Registering the name is only half the job. A domain on its own shows nothing; it has to point somewhere. If you already have hosting, your host will give you nameserver addresses or an IP address to enter in the registrar's DNS settings, and the change usually takes effect within a few hours, sometimes faster. If you are starting from scratch, the order is domain first, then hosting, then the website itself. Our guide on domain names versus web hosting explains the difference between the two, and how much web hosting costs in Canada covers what to budget for the hosting side.

Two things are worth setting up in the same session:

  • A business email address on the domain, such as hello@yourbusiness.ca. It looks far more professional than a free Gmail address, and it is the address customers will reply to. See our guide on how to set up a business email address in Canada.
  • An SSL certificate, so the site loads over HTTPS and browsers do not show a "not secure" warning. Most hosts include a free certificate, and our guide on whether you need an SSL certificate explains what it does.

Once the DNS is pointed and the site is live, type the domain into a browser and check that it loads with the lock icon showing. Also check the version without "www" and the version with it, so visitors reach the site either way.

What a .ca domain costs in Canada

A .ca registration usually costs somewhere around $10 to $20 CAD per year at most registrars, with first-year promotions sometimes lower. Renewals are typically in the same range, but they are often higher than the first-year price, which is why the renewal price matters more than the headline price. Registering for multiple years sometimes brings the yearly cost down a little.

The domain itself is a small part of the total cost of having a website. Hosting usually runs a few dollars to a few tens of dollars a month, and the website build is the larger one-time cost. If you are budgeting the whole project, our guide on how much a website costs in Canada puts the pieces together in CAD.

One cost that is easy to forget: privacy protection. For .ca, you usually do not need to pay for it. CIRA automatically withholds the personal contact details of individual registrants from the public WHOIS, at no extra cost. Business registrants' details are published, which is normal for a company. Some registrars still try to sell WHOIS privacy as an add-on for .ca; for an individual registrant it adds nothing.

.ca versus .com: which should a Canadian business choose?

For a business whose customers are mostly in Canada, .ca is usually the better primary choice. It tells a visitor immediately that you are Canadian, it is easier to get a short name on, and it can help slightly with Canadian search visibility because Google understands the domain is aimed at Canada. It also avoids the awkward situation of a Canadian customer wondering whether a .com business ships to Canada or serves their province.

That said, .com still has one advantage: it is the default people type from habit. If your exact name is available on both endings, registering the .ca as your main domain and also holding the .com as a redirect is the safest setup, if the budget allows. Visitors who type the .com land on your .ca site either way. If you can only afford one, and your customers are Canadian, choose .ca.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Registering under the wrong name. If your web designer or a friend registers the domain in their own name, they legally own it, not you. Always register in the business's legal name, with an email address the business controls.
  • Letting auto-renew lapse. A domain that expires can be recovered during the grace and redemption stages, but recovery gets more expensive and less certain the longer you wait, and eventually the name goes back on the market for anyone to register. Put the renewal date in your calendar as well as relying on auto-renew.
  • Ignoring CIRA emails. CIRA occasionally sends validation notices to registrants. If one arrives and you ignore it, the domain can be at risk. Open mail from CIRA and your registrar, and keep the registrant email address current.
  • Buying the domain and forgetting the website. A domain with no site is just a name on a list. If you are not ready to build the site yet, that is fine, but do not let the name sit unpointed for a year while competitors build their presence. A simple one-page site with your services, phone number, and contact form is a better use of the domain than a blank page.

Frequently asked questions

Can anyone register a .ca domain?

No. Only people and organizations that meet CIRA's Canadian Presence Requirements can register and hold a .ca. That includes Canadian citizens, permanent residents, Canadian corporations, and owners of a Canadian-registered trademark, along with other categories such as Indigenous peoples, governments, schools, and hospitals. A foreign business with no Canadian entity or trademark does not qualify.

How much does a .ca domain cost?

Usually somewhere around $10 to $20 CAD per year, depending on the registrar. First-year prices are often discounted, so compare the renewal price, not just the first-year price, before you choose a registrar. You can register for one to ten years, and registering for several years at once is sometimes cheaper per year.

Do I need a .ca domain, or is .com fine?

A .com works fine technically, and your site will load the same either way. But if your customers are mostly Canadian, a .ca signals that you are local, is easier to get as a short name, and can help slightly with Canadian search visibility. If you can hold both, use .ca as your main domain and point the .com at it as a redirect.

What happens if my .ca domain expires?

It does not disappear immediately. A .ca passes through defined expiry stages that can give you more than two months to recover it, usually by renewing through your registrar. Recovery can cost extra during the later stages, and once the process ends the name returns to the public pool, where anyone can register it. Turn auto-renew on and keep your registrant email address current so you get the reminders.
