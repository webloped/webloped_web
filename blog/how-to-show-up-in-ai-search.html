How to show up in AI search: getting your Canadian small business recommended by ChatGPT, Gemini, and Google AI Overviews

More customers now ask an AI assistant for a shortlist instead of scrolling through ten blue links. Here is how to show up in AI search by making your business easy to find, easy to understand, and easy to verify.

A customer in Cambridge needs a plumber, a photographer, or a bookkeeper. A year or two ago, that customer would have typed a phrase into Google and scanned the results. Today, that customer is just as likely to ask ChatGPT, Gemini, or Google AI Mode a plain question: "Who is a reliable plumber near me?" or "Find a wedding photographer in Kitchener-Waterloo who does outdoor photos." The answer comes back as a short list of three or four names, often with a sentence about each one. If your business is not in that answer, you were never in the conversation.

That shift worries a lot of small business owners, and it should not. Learning how to show up in AI search does not require a new trick, a special file, or a separate kind of marketing. It mostly requires doing the clear, verifiable basics well: a website that states plain facts, listings that agree with each other, genuine reviews, and pages that answer real customer questions in plain language. Small businesses can compete here, especially for local questions, because AI answers to local questions draw heavily on local listings, reviews, and service pages, where a well-described local business can stand out against a national brand that says very little about that city.

The short answer

To show up in AI search, make sure AI tools can read your website, state clearly what you do, who you serve, and where you work, keep your Google Business Profile and other listings accurate and consistent, add structured data that labels those facts, answer customer questions directly on your own pages, and earn mentions and reviews on sites other than your own. Google has said there are no extra requirements for appearing in AI Overviews or AI Mode beyond normal search eligibility, and the same SEO fundamentals still apply. That is good news. It means the work below is not a gamble on a new platform. It is the same foundation that helps you rank on Google, presented in a way AI tools can also use.

There is no switch to flip and no guarantee to buy. Pages need to be indexed before they can be cited, and the trust that makes an AI tool recommend you again and again builds over weeks and months of consistent, accurate information. The steps in this guide are in order of impact. Start at the top, and do not skip ahead to tactics that sound advanced while the basics are missing.

How AI search picks a business

Traditional Google search ranks pages and shows links. AI search reads many sources and writes an answer. That difference changes what matters most.

An AI assistant is trying to answer a question, not to fill a results page. When someone asks for the best accountant in Mississauga or a roofer who handles emergency repairs, the tool looks for businesses it can describe with confidence: what the business does, where it works, how to contact it, what it charges or how pricing works, and what other people say about it. It then checks whether different sources agree. Your website says one thing, your Google Business Profile says the same thing, a directory listing matches, and recent reviews describe the same service in the same city. Agreement builds confidence. Contradictions destroy it. A phone number that differs between your site and your profile, a service area that is vague, or hours that have not been updated since a holiday can be enough for an AI tool to pick a competitor whose facts are cleaner.

Clarity beats cleverness. AI tools favour pages that state facts plainly and in full sentences a person could quote. A page that says "We are a family dental clinic in Guelph offering checkups, cleanings, and emergency appointments, open Monday to Saturday" is far more useful to an AI answer than a page full of slogans, stock photos, and a "Learn more" button. Specifics help too: real service names, real neighbourhoods and cities you actually serve, real hours, and real starting prices or a clear explanation of how pricing works. Vague claims such as "quality service at great prices" give an AI tool nothing to quote and a customer nothing to act on.

Freshness matters as well. An AI answer that recommends a business with last year's hours or a closed location is a bad answer, so tools look for signs that information is current: recently updated pages, a Google Business Profile with recent photos and posts, and a steady flow of recent reviews. You do not need to publish constantly. You need to avoid looking abandoned.

Step 1: Let the AI crawlers in

Before anything else, check that AI search tools are allowed to read your website at all. Your robots.txt file tells crawlers which parts of your site they may visit. If it blocks everything, or blocks AI crawlers by name, some assistants will not show your site in their answers. OpenAI has said that sites which opt out of its search crawler, OAI-SearchBot, will not appear in ChatGPT search answers. Google AI Overviews draw on Google's regular search index, so normal Google indexing matters there too.

Ask your web developer or host to check three things. First, that robots.txt does not block OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, Googlebot, and Bingbot. Second, that your hosting setup or firewall is not quietly blocking those crawlers even though robots.txt allows them. Third, that your key pages are actually reachable and readable without logging in. If your developer is unsure, a quick check in Google Search Console will confirm whether Google can crawl and index your pages. Bing Webmaster Tools does the same for Bing, which feeds some AI assistants.

One distinction is worth knowing. OpenAI separates GPTBot, which collects content for training AI models, from OAI-SearchBot, which surfaces websites in ChatGPT search. Blocking GPTBot does not have to remove you from ChatGPT search, as long as OAI-SearchBot is still allowed. If privacy or training concerns matter to you, discuss that split with your developer rather than blocking every OpenAI crawler at once and disappearing from search answers by accident.

Do not hide your key facts behind obstacles. If your services, prices, hours, and service area only appear inside images, downloadable PDFs, or popups that load late, crawlers and AI tools may miss them. Put the important facts in ordinary text on the page itself. Images and PDFs can stay as extras. They should not be the only place a fact lives.

Step 2: State the facts plainly on your own website

Your website is the source AI tools should trust first, because it is the one source you control. Treat your homepage and each service page as a fact sheet. A visitor, and an AI reader, should be able to answer four questions within seconds: What do you do? Where do you do it? Who is it for? How do they contact you or book?

Write those answers in plain text. Name each service the way customers search for it, not only by an internal or branded name. List the cities and neighbourhoods you genuinely serve, and do not claim a city you will not travel to. AI tools and customers both notice when a business claims twenty cities and has no evidence of working in any of them. Put your business name, address or service area, phone number, and hours in text on your contact page and, where it fits, in the footer. If you have a physical location, show the full address. If you are a service-area business without a storefront, say so clearly and name the area you cover.

Pricing deserves the same honesty. You do not have to publish a full price list if your work is quoted per job, but do explain how pricing works: whether quotes are free, whether there is a callout fee, what a typical job costs in CAD, or what factors change the price. AI tools quote specifics far more often than slogans, and customers trust a page that answers the money question instead of dodging it. Our guide to writing website copy that converts covers the same habit from the customer side: specific, customer-first language wins over generic claims.

Give each main service its own page where you can. One long page that lists everything your business does forces an AI tool to guess which service matches which question. A dedicated page for each service, with its own clear heading, description, service area, and call to action, gives the tool a clean page to cite for that exact question. If you only have a few services, a well-organized page with clear sections for each one works too, as long as each section states the service, the area, and the next step in plain text.

Step 3: Answer real customer questions, answer first

AI search is, at its core, a question-answering system. The pages it cites most are the ones that answer a question directly and then explain. Write the way your customers ask. A roofer's customers ask "How much does roof repair cost in Ottawa?" and "How long does a roof replacement take?" A bookkeeper's customers ask "What does a bookkeeper charge for a small business in Canada?" and "Do I need a bookkeeper or an accountant?" Each of those questions deserves a page or a clear section that opens with the direct answer in the first sentence, followed by the detail, the exceptions, and the next step.

Keep the direct answer short and specific. "A small roof repair in Ottawa typically costs a few hundred dollars, while a full replacement depends on roof size, material, and access" is a start, but a stronger answer adds the range or the factors in the same paragraph so the reader does not have to hunt for it. Then add the useful context: what changes the price, how long the work takes, what is included, and what happens next if the customer contacts you. Avoid padding the answer with a long introduction about your company history before the question is answered. The history can come later, after the reader has what they came for.

Add a short FAQ section to your main service pages, using the exact questions customers ask on the phone, in email, and in your contact form. Three to five questions per page is plenty. Write each answer in two to four sentences, in plain language, with a specific fact where one exists. This is the same structure AI tools are built to read, and it is also the structure that helps a busy human scan a page and decide to call. Our post on whether blogging helps a business website makes the same point for blog content: each post should answer one real customer question, completely, rather than covering a topic in general terms.

Be careful with claims. Only state facts you can stand behind: your real hours, your real service area, your real process, and prices or ranges you actually charge. AI tools repeat what they read, and a confident-sounding claim on your site can end up quoted in an answer to a customer who then arrives expecting it. If a number is an estimate, say so and say what it depends on. If you are unsure about a legal or regulatory point, such as a Quebec language requirement or a privacy rule, describe it in general terms and point readers to professional advice instead of stating it as a settled fact. Accuracy is a ranking asset here, not just good manners.

Step 4: Add structured data so machines do not have to guess

Structured data, often called schema markup, is a small block of code that labels the facts already on your page in a format machines can read reliably. For a local business, the two most useful types are LocalBusiness schema, which labels your business name, address or service area, phone number, hours, services, and service area, and FAQPage schema, which labels your questions and answers. Adding schema does not guarantee a citation or a top ranking. It removes ambiguity. It tells an AI tool and a search engine, in plain machine-readable terms, "this phone number belongs to this business, these are its hours, this is the area it serves."

Ask your developer to add LocalBusiness schema to your homepage and contact page, and FAQPage schema wherever you publish a FAQ section. The schema must match what visitors see on the page. If the schema says you are open Saturdays but the page says you are closed, that mismatch hurts trust rather than helping it. Keep the schema updated whenever hours, services, or prices change. If you use WordPress or another platform with a schema plugin, check that the plugin is actually outputting correct information rather than filling in defaults you never reviewed.

Test the result. Google offers a free Rich Results Test that shows whether it can read your structured data and whether anything is invalid. Fix errors rather than leaving broken markup in place. Broken or misleading schema is worse than no schema, because it teaches search engines that your code cannot be trusted.

Step 5: Keep your Google Business Profile and listings in agreement

For local questions, AI tools lean heavily on your Google Business Profile and on directory listings, because those sources exist to answer exactly the questions customers ask: where are you, when are you open, what do you do, and what do other customers think? If your profile is incomplete or contradicts your website, AI tools have to choose which source to believe, and they may simply choose a competitor.

Complete your profile fully: the correct business name, the right primary category and any relevant secondary categories, a complete description in your own words, your services, your service area, your hours including holiday hours, your phone number, your website link, and recent photos of your real work, premises, or team. Keep your name, address, and phone number identical everywhere they appear: your website, your Google Business Profile, Apple Business Connect, Bing Places, Yelp, and any industry directories that matter in your field. Even small differences, such as a shortened street name in one place and the full name in another, add doubt. Our guide to getting your business on Google walks through the profile setup step by step, and our Google Maps ranking guide covers the same three local ranking factors, relevance, distance, and prominence, that shape AI answers to local questions too.

Listings on other sites matter because AI tools treat agreement across independent sources as evidence. Claim and correct the listings that customers and AI tools actually check in your industry, whether that is a trade directory, a professional association, a review platform, or a local chamber listing. You do not need to be listed on hundreds of obscure directories. A handful of accurate, complete, well-maintained listings beats a hundred half-finished ones. Check them once a quarter, and correct them the same week anything changes.

Step 6: Earn reviews and mentions on other sites

AI tools trust what others say about you more than what you say about yourself. That is why reviews and third-party mentions carry so much weight in AI answers. A business with recent, detailed Google reviews that name specific services and neighbourhoods gives an AI tool quotable evidence from real customers. A business with no reviews, or with only old ones, gives it nothing to work with.

Build a simple review habit. Ask every satisfied customer, in person or in a short follow-up message, and make it easy with a direct review link or QR code. Respond to every review, positive and negative, in a calm and specific way. Our guides to getting more Google reviews and responding to a negative Google review cover the rules, the ask scripts, and the reply formula in detail. Never buy fake reviews, offer incentives for positive reviews in a way that breaks platform rules, or ask staff or family to pose as customers. Fake reviews are easy to spot, they put your profile at risk, and they mislead the very customers you want to keep.

Mentions beyond reviews help too. A mention in a local news story, a chamber of commerce member listing, an industry association directory, a supplier's partner page, or a local "best of" guide written by a real publication all tell AI tools that your business is known and active in its community. You do not need press coverage to benefit. Sponsoring a local team, speaking at a community event, joining a local business group, or publishing a genuinely useful local guide can all earn honest mentions over time. Focus on mentions that a customer would find believable and useful, not on low-quality link schemes that exist only to manipulate search results.

Step 7: Keep the information current

AI search rewards businesses that look alive. Put a visible "last updated" note on pages where dates matter, such as pricing and FAQ pages, and update it when the facts change. Refresh your Google Business Profile when hours, services, or photos change, and add a post or a new photo every few weeks if you can. Reply to new reviews promptly. Remove services you no longer offer and pages for offers that have ended. A site that still advertises a 2024 promotion in late 2026 signals neglect to customers and to AI tools alike.

Canadian context helps here too. If you serve customers in more than one province, state your service areas plainly and note any differences, such as travel fees or French-language service, rather than leaving customers to guess. If you serve Quebec customers, consider French content for the pages those customers will read, and get advice on the current language rules for your situation rather than assuming one English page covers the whole country. Prices should be shown in CAD with taxes and shipping or travel costs explained clearly, so neither a customer nor an AI tool has to convert or guess. And if your business is seasonal, say so: state your busy season, your booking lead time, and when you are closed, so AI answers do not send customers to you in a month you are not working.

What not to do

A few common mistakes waste time or actively hurt your chances. The first is chasing a magic file. An llms.txt file, a plain-text summary of your site meant for AI tools, is a newer informal convention, not an official standard, and Google has said its search ignores llms.txt and similar special AI files. Such a file does not hurt, but it is no substitute for allowing the crawlers in, writing clear pages, and keeping listings consistent. Do not pay anyone who promises that one file will get you cited.

The second is believing you can pay for an organic citation. You cannot pay to be recommended inside an AI answer itself. Some platforms show separate, labelled ads alongside AI answers, but the recommendation in the answer depends on being indexed, relevant, and verifiable. Anyone selling a guaranteed spot in ChatGPT answers is selling something they cannot deliver.

The third is flooding your site with thin, AI-generated pages. Publishing dozens of near-identical pages for every city and service combination, each with the same generic text and a swapped city name, is the kind of content both search engines and AI tools have learned to ignore. It also reads badly to humans. One strong, specific page per real service and real area, kept accurate, outperforms fifty thin ones. The same applies to keyword stuffing. Repeating a phrase such as "plumber Toronto" twenty times on a page does not make an AI tool more confident. It makes the page harder to read and less quotable.

Finally, do not promise yourself a number one spot. No honest provider can guarantee that ChatGPT, Gemini, or Google will recommend your business for a given question, because the answer depends on the question, the customer's location, the sources available that day, and your competitors. What you can control is the foundation: clear facts, consistent listings, genuine reviews, and current pages. That foundation also helps your normal Google rankings, which is why this work pays off even if AI search changes again next year.

How to check whether it is working

Start by asking the tools the way your customers would. Once a month, open ChatGPT, Gemini, and Google AI Mode or AI Overviews and ask the exact questions your customers ask, including the city or neighbourhood: "Who is a good electrician in Barrie for panel upgrades?" or "Best family photographer in Halifax for fall photos?" Write down which businesses are named, what each one is described as doing, and whether your business appears. Note the wording. If a competitor is described with a specific service and price range while you are described vaguely, or not at all, that tells you which facts are missing or unclear on your site.

Then check the signals behind the answers. In Google Search Console, look for impressions and clicks on the pages you improved, and confirm the pages are indexed. In Bing Webmaster Tools, check for indexing and any AI-related reporting available there. In Google Analytics, look for visits referred from chatgpt.com, perplexity.ai, and similar domains, and check whether those visitors contact you. Our guide to setting up Google Analytics covers the setup if you have not installed it yet. In your Google Business Profile, watch views, calls, and direction requests, and in your own records, ask new enquiries how they found you. A customer who says "ChatGPT recommended you" is the most direct evidence you will get.

Give it time. New pages and listings can take weeks to be indexed and picked up, and the reviews, mentions, and consistency that make recommendations stick usually take a few months of steady work. Check monthly, fix one or two gaps each time, and judge the trend over a quarter rather than the result of a single question on a single day. AI answers vary by phrasing, location, and timing, so one absence is not a verdict. A pattern of absence across many phrasings, after the foundation is in place, is a signal to revisit your service pages, your profile, and your reviews.

If your site is not showing up on Google at all, fix that first, because AI Overviews draw on the same index. A starter plan you can finish this week

If the full guide feels like a lot, start with the highest-impact work in one focused session:

  • Allow the crawlers. Confirm robots.txt and your host allow OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, Googlebot, and Bingbot, and that key pages are indexed in Google Search Console.
  • Fix your facts. Rewrite your homepage and top two service pages so each states what you do, where you work, who it is for, and how to contact you, in plain text, with CAD pricing explained.
  • Answer three questions. Add a short FAQ to each main service page, opening each answer with the direct answer in the first sentence.
  • Complete your profile. Fill in your Google Business Profile completely, add recent photos, and make your name, address, and phone number match your website exactly.
  • Ask for reviews. Ask your three most recent satisfied customers for a Google review, and reply to any reviews you have not answered yet.
  • Add schema. Ask your developer to add LocalBusiness and FAQPage schema that matches what visitors see, and test it with Google Rich Results Test.

That is a realistic afternoon of work, and it covers the foundation every later tactic depends on. Repeat the review and listing checks monthly, keep pages current when facts change, and add one genuinely useful answer page per month for the questions customers keep asking.

Frequently asked questions

Can I pay to show up in ChatGPT or Google AI Overviews?

No, not inside the organic answer itself. You cannot pay to be recommended in a ChatGPT answer or an AI Overview. Some platforms show separate, labelled ads next to AI answers, but the recommendation in the answer depends on being indexed, relevant to the question, and verifiable across trusted sources. Treat any offer of a guaranteed paid spot in AI answers as a warning sign.

Do I need an llms.txt file to show up in AI search?

No. An llms.txt file is a newer informal convention, not an official standard, and Google has said its search ignores llms.txt and similar special AI files. Adding one does not hurt, but it does not replace the work that matters: allowing AI crawlers, writing clear pages, adding structured data, keeping listings consistent, and earning reviews and mentions. Do the foundation first, and treat llms.txt as an optional extra at most.

How long does it take to show up in AI search?

It varies. A clear, indexed page can be cited fairly quickly once a crawler has read it, sometimes within weeks. Building the reviews, listings, and mentions that make AI tools recommend you consistently usually takes a few months of steady work. There is no instant switch, and answers vary by question, location, and timing, so check monthly and judge the trend over a quarter rather than a single day.

Is showing up in AI search different from SEO, and do I still need to rank on Google?

Mostly no, and yes. Google has said the same SEO fundamentals apply to AI Overviews and AI Mode, with no special optimizations required. The difference is emphasis: AI answers lean more heavily on clear, direct answers and on what third-party sources say about you. You still need to rank and be indexed on Google, because AI Overviews draw on that index, and because many customers still search the traditional way. The foundation in this guide serves both.
