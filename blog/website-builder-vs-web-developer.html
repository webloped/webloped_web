Should you hire a web developer or use a website builder?

The website builder vs web developer decision comes up in almost every project conversation we have. Here is the honest breakdown - when a builder is the smart choice, when it will cost you more in the long run, and how to decide in ten minutes.

We are a web development company, so we have every incentive to tell you that you need a developer. We are not going to do that. A website builder - Wix, Squarespace, Shopify, GoDaddy, or one of the newer AI builders - is genuinely the right call for a large share of small businesses. Paying a developer when a builder would do is just as wasteful as building everything by hand when you need a developer.

The honest answer to website builder vs web developer is that they are not really competitors. They are different tools for different jobs. The trick is knowing which job you have. This guide lays out what each option actually gives you, what each costs over time, and the situations where each one wins - so you can pick with your eyes open instead of guessing.

What a website builder actually is

A website builder is a subscription service: you pick a template, drag and drop your content into it, and the platform handles hosting, security updates, and the technical plumbing. Typical plans run in the tens of CAD per month, billed by the platform - and note that several builders bill in USD, so the number on your credit card statement will be higher than the advertised price after the exchange rate.

What you get: a live website within days (sometimes hours), no technical skills required, and predictable monthly pricing. What you give up: design freedom (your site will look like thousands of other sites on the same template), fine control over performance and SEO, and ownership of the underlying code. You are renting a space inside someone else's system. That is fine for a lot of businesses - but it is a constraint you should understand before you commit.

The quality gap has narrowed. AI-assisted builders that generate a full site from a few prompts are now genuinely usable for simple sites. But "simple" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The moment your site needs custom functionality - a booking flow that matches your real process, a quote calculator, a connection to your CRM or inventory system - builders start fighting you, and the workarounds get ugly fast.

What hiring a developer actually gets you

Hiring a developer means paying for strategy, design, and code built specifically for your business. The site is designed around your customers and your goals instead of being fitted into a template. If you have read our breakdown of how much a website costs in Canada, you know the realistic ranges - this is not the cheap option, and anyone selling it as cheap is cutting corners you will pay for later.

What you are really buying is not code, it is decisions you do not have to make: what the visitor should see first, what makes them trust you, what to say on each page, how the site should behave on a phone, and how Google should read it. A good developer also builds the site to be fast - page speed is both a ranking factor and a conversion factor, and builder sites consistently score worse here because they carry the weight of the platform's generic code.

You also own the result. The code, the design, and the site itself are yours to keep, move to any host, and have any developer work on. With a builder, if the platform raises prices, changes features, or shuts down, your only move is to rebuild somewhere else.

The real cost comparison

Builders win the first-year math easily: a year of subscriptions is a few hundred CAD, while a professional build is a serious one-time investment. But do the comparison over three years, because websites live for years, and two things change the picture:

  • Builder costs never stop. The subscription is forever. Miss a payment and the site goes down. Over three to five years, the gap between "cheap builder" and "expensive developer" shrinks substantially - and the developer-built site is an asset you own, while the builder site is a rental.
  • Your time is a cost too. A first-time builder site usually takes a business owner 20 to 40 hours to build and polish. At the value of your own time, that is not free. And most DIY sites underperform at the thing that matters: turning visitors into enquiries and sales. One extra customer a month from a better-converting site can pay for the difference.
  • E-commerce has extra costs on both sides. Some builders charge transaction fees on your sales on top of payment processing fees, and those percentages compound as you grow. A developer-built store avoids the platform cut but adds payment processing and maintenance to your plate. Run your actual sales numbers through both options.

The fairest way to think about it: a builder is cheaper when your website is a supporting tool, and a developer pays for itself when your website is a growth asset - when more and better leads translate directly into revenue.

Website builder vs web developer: the side-by-side

  • Time to launch: builder wins - days. A developer build typically takes two to six weeks, because it includes design, copy, and testing stages a builder skips.
  • Upfront cost: builder wins - a subscription vs a project quote.
  • Design uniqueness: developer wins. Builders offer thousands of templates, but so does everyone else using them. A developer-built site looks like your business, not like a template.
  • Speed and performance: developer wins. Builder sites carry platform overhead that you cannot strip out; a developer can optimize every kilobyte.
  • SEO: builder is adequate for basics, developer wins for competitive markets. Builders cover titles, headings, and sitemaps; developers add schema markup, clean URL structures, redirect planning, and the technical tuning that decides close rankings.
  • Custom features: developer wins, and it is not close. If you need it and the builder does not offer it, you are stuck.
  • Maintenance: builder wins for simplicity - updates are automatic. A developer-built site needs someone handling updates and backups, though a care plan covers this.
  • Ownership and lock-in: developer wins. With a builder you can leave, but you leave your site behind and rebuild from scratch. Your domain, however, should always be registered in your own name regardless of the path you choose.

When a website builder is the right call

Use a builder, confidently, when:

  • You need an online presence this week - a landing page for a launch, a simple portfolio, a brochure site for a new business.
  • Your budget is genuinely near zero and your needs are genuinely simple.
  • The site is informational, not transactional - hours, location, services, contact form, done.
  • You are testing an idea and want to validate it before investing real money.
  • You enjoy tinkering and plan to update the site yourself regularly.
  • You are a freelancer or sole operator whose clients come mostly from referrals, and the site just needs to confirm you are legitimate.

There is no shame in any of these. A clean builder site beats a half-finished custom project every time - and it beats the most common option of all, which is no website while you "think about it."

When you should hire a developer

Hire a developer when:

  • Your website is how you get customers - if leads come through the site, the site is a sales tool and should be built like one.
  • You need custom functionality: booking systems, quote calculators, customer portals, integrations with your CRM, inventory, or payment systems.
  • You sell online at any real volume - the conversion rate, checkout experience, and fee structure of your store directly affect revenue.
  • You compete in a crowded market where standing out matters. If every competitor uses the same templates, a distinctive site is a genuine advantage.
  • Speed and SEO are business-critical - competitive local searches, paid traffic landing pages, or any site where a one-second delay costs money.
  • You have been burned by lock-in before, or you plan to own and grow this asset for years.

A useful rule of thumb: if the website needs to do something specific to your business, hire a developer. If it needs to say something about your business, a builder will do.

The middle paths most people miss

It is not a binary choice. Two hybrid approaches work well in practice:

  • Start on a builder, rebuild professionally when it pays. Launch fast and cheap, learn what your customers actually respond to, then invest in a proper build once the business justifies it. Just go in knowing the rebuild is a fresh build, not a transfer - budget for it.
  • A developer build on an open platform. A developer does not have to mean fully custom code. A professional build on an open system gives you custom design and proper SEO without full lock-in, at a lower price point than a from-scratch build. Ask any developer you talk to which platforms they build on and what happens if you ever want to leave them.

Whatever you choose, register your domain yourself, in your own name, with your own account - never let it live inside someone else's account. Your domain is the one asset that must survive any platform change. If you are hiring, our guide on what to look for in a web partner covers the questions worth asking before you sign.

How to decide in ten minutes

Answer these five questions honestly:

  • 1. How do customers find and choose you? If the website is central to winning business, invest in it. If it is a credibility check after a referral, keep it simple.
  • 2. What must the site do? List the actual functions - booking, selling, quoting, integrating. If the list goes beyond "show information and take messages," you probably need a developer.
  • 3. What is your real budget? Include your own time. A builder is cheap in dollars and expensive in hours; a developer is the reverse.
  • 4. What happens in two years? If you expect the business to grow, pick the option that grows with it. Migrating off a builder mid-growth is disruptive and means rebuilding.
  • 5. Who maintains it? A builder needs your time but no skills. A developer-built site needs a care plan or an in-house person. Both need a named owner - the sites that rot are the ones nobody owns.

If three or more answers point toward "the site matters and needs to do real work," talk to a developer. If most answers are "simple and soon," build it yourself on a builder and spend your energy on the business instead.

Frequently asked questions

Is a website built with Wix or Squarespace bad for SEO?

Not automatically. Website builders have improved their SEO basics - you can set page titles, meta descriptions, and headings, and Google does index builder sites. Where they tend to fall behind is page speed, technical flexibility (schema markup, redirects, URL structure), and the custom optimization a developer would handle. For a local business with modest competition, builder SEO is often enough. In a competitive market, the technical edge of a professionally built site matters more.

Can I move my website from a builder to a custom site later?

Your content - text, images, product data - can be moved, but the site itself cannot be exported as code on most builders. Moving platforms means recreating the design and re-setting up SEO, redirects, and integrations. Plan for a full rebuild rather than a simple transfer, and keep your domain registered separately in your own name so switching platforms never risks losing your web address.

Is it really cheaper to build my own website with a builder?

The subscription is cheap - typically in the tens of CAD per month - but the real cost is your time. A business owner building a builder site for the first time usually spends 20 to 40 hours on it, and the result is often weaker at converting visitors into customers. Compare the builder route against both the cost of a developer and the value of those hours spent on your actual business before deciding.

Will AI website builders replace web developers?

AI builders have made simple sites faster and easier to create, and they are genuinely good for straightforward brochure sites. They have not replaced developers for sites that need custom functionality, integrations, performance tuning, or a distinctive design. The pattern so far: AI handles the easier half of the job, and the harder half - strategy, customization, and quality control - still needs a human.
