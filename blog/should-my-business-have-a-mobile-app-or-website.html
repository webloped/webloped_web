Should my business have a mobile app or a website?

"We need an app" is something small business owners say a lot. Sometimes it is exactly right. But often, a mobile-friendly website would have done the job for a fraction of the cost. Here is the honest breakdown.

The short answer to "should my business have a mobile app or a website?" is this: most small businesses should build the website first. A website is cheaper, faster to launch, works on every phone and computer without anyone downloading anything, and it can be found on Google - which is where most new customers come from. Build the app later, when your business proves it needs one.

That is not anti-app bias. There are businesses where an app is clearly the right call, and we will name them. But the gap between what people imagine an app costs and what it actually costs is enormous, and the gap between "our customers will download our app" and reality is even bigger. This guide walks through both sides with real numbers so you can make the decision with your eyes open.

What do people actually mean by "an app"?

It helps to separate three different things people lump together under "app":

  • A native mobile app is downloaded from the App Store or Google Play and installed on the phone. It can send push notifications, work offline, and use the camera, GPS, and other hardware directly. It has to be built twice - once for iPhone, once for Android - or built with a cross-platform framework that still needs testing and maintenance on both.
  • A web app runs in a browser on any device. Think of your online banking portal, Google Docs, or a booking system you open from a link. No download, no app store, works on every phone out of the box.
  • A progressive web app (PWA) sits in the middle. It is a website built so it can be installed to a phone's home screen, open quickly, and in some cases work offline and send push notifications - all without going through an app store.

When a small business owner says "we need an app," what they usually want is a web app experience: online booking, ordering, or a customer portal. Most of that can be delivered inside a good website or a PWA, without the cost and complexity of a native app.

The cost reality check

Let us put the numbers side by side in CAD terms, because this is where most decisions should start:

  • A professional small business website typically starts in the low four figures in CAD and goes up from there, depending on pages, features, and design quality. Yearly running costs are modest: a domain, hosting, and maintenance.
  • A custom native app for both iPhone and Android usually lands in the five figures or more - several times the cost of the website - because you are effectively building two products that have to look identical, stay in sync, and pass app store review. And the launch cost is not the total cost: every year brings operating system updates, bug fixes, and store compliance changes that someone has to handle.
  • App store fees are a small but recurring line item: Apple charges 99 USD per year for a developer account, Google Play charges a one-time 25 USD fee. Both are billed in USD, so budget with the exchange rate in mind.

So the app is not just a bigger cheque once. It is a bigger cheque twice (two platforms), plus an ongoing maintenance bill that never goes to zero, plus store policies you have to live with. Apple's review guidelines, for example, reject apps that are "just a website wrapped in an app" - your app must do something meaningfully beyond what a website could do, or it can be rejected after you have paid to build it.

A useful mental model: a website is a project; an app is a commitment. Budget for the app's second year, not just its launch. If that maintenance number makes you uncomfortable, that discomfort is information.

The download problem nobody budgets for

Here is the part that surprises most business owners: building the app is only half the battle. Getting people to download and keep it is the other half, and it costs real money.

A website gets visitors for free from Google. Someone searches "plumber near me" or "book haircut Cambridge," clicks your site, and books - no download, no friction, no commitment. An app starts at zero users and stays there until you market it. Ask yourself honestly: how many apps do you have on your phone from businesses you use once or twice a year? Now ask whether your customers will treat your business differently.

The average phone owner uses a small handful of apps regularly. Breaking into that rotation requires either a daily habit (ordering lunch, tracking workouts) or a strong incentive (a loyalty program worth real money). A restaurant a customer visits twice a year, a contractor they hire once, a clinic they visit annually - these businesses rarely clear the download bar. The app sits unopened, and every dollar spent marketing it competes with the website that would have converted that same customer from a search click.

This is why the smartest first move for most businesses is a mobile-first website with a proper booking or ordering flow. If customers use that flow constantly and start asking "do you have an app?" - that is your demand signal. You will be building the app with real evidence instead of hope.

When a mobile app is the right answer

Apps earn their cost when several of these are true:

  • Customers come back constantly. Ordering coffee every morning, checking loyalty points weekly, tracking deliveries daily - a habit that lives in a pocket.
  • Push notifications are the feature, not a nice-to-have. Appointment reminders, order status updates, and urgent alerts reach people reliably through an app in ways email cannot.
  • Offline access matters. Field staff filling in inspection forms in basements and rural job sites, technicians with no signal - work has to continue without a connection.
  • The phone's hardware is central. Sustained camera work, GPS tracking, Bluetooth peripherals, or biometric login that a browser handles poorly.
  • It is a tool for your own team. A route planner, a stock counter, a shift checklist - internal apps skip the whole download-marketing problem because the users are already yours.
  • You already have an audience ready to install it. An engaged customer base that will choose to download on day one changes the economics completely.

If two or three of those describe your situation, price the app seriously. If none do, hold off.

When a website is the right answer

A mobile-friendly website wins when:

  • You need new customers. Websites are discoverable; apps are not. Nobody searches an app store for a service they do not know exists. They search Google, and a good website meets them there.
  • Customers find you once and act. Book the appointment, request the quote, buy the product - most small business transactions are one visit, one action.
  • Your budget is finite. A website delivers the core business outcomes - credibility, leads, sales - at a fraction of the app cost.
  • Content changes often. Menus, prices, hours, service lists - updating a website takes minutes and reaches everyone instantly. Updating an app means new builds, store review, and waiting for users to update.
  • You are validating an idea. Launch the booking flow or store on the web first, watch real usage for a month, and let the data tell you whether an app is worth it.

And note: "mobile-friendly website" is not a compromise position. With a fast responsive build, thumb-friendly navigation, click-to-call buttons, and a smooth checkout or booking flow, the customer experience on a phone is genuinely excellent - no download required. A well-structured small business site handles the vast majority of customer journeys end to end.

The middle path: start web, add app later

This is the route most small businesses should take, and it is not settling - it is sequencing. The order looks like this:

  1. Build a fast, mobile-first website. One clear action per page, a booking or checkout flow that works with a thumb, pages that load in under a few seconds. This is your foundation, and it pays for itself from search traffic alone.
  2. Add the "app-like" features to the website first. Want to test whether customers would reorder online? Add reordering to the site. Want reminders? Email and SMS reminders reach everyone with no install. A PWA layer can add a home-screen icon and offline basics when it starts making sense. Real usage data beats opinions every time.
  3. Build the native app when the signal is loud. Customers asking for it unprompted, a loyalty program with real traction, a workflow that genuinely needs the phone's hardware. Now the app has a proven job to do, and you already have the website's customer base to launch it to.

The businesses that skip straight to step three usually end up with an expensive app that duplicates the website - services, hours, gallery, contact form - while the website itself is slow and outdated. That is the worst of both worlds: two things to maintain, neither of them great. Do step one properly first, and everything downstream gets easier.

A quick decision framework

If you want a five-minute answer, score yourself on these questions:

  • How do customers first find you? If the answer is "Google," you need a website. Full stop. Apps do not do discovery.
  • How often does the same customer interact with you? Weekly or more, with repeat transactions: app territory. Monthly or less: the website is enough.
  • What can your budget sustain? Be honest about year two, not just launch. An app you cannot afford to maintain becomes a liability - outdated, buggy, and one bad review away from hurting you.
  • Is there a feature only an app can do? Name it specifically. "It would be nice to have an app" is not a feature. Offline field work, daily push reminders, loyalty at the counter - those are features.
  • What would you cut to pay for the app? Opportunity cost is real. The money for an app is money not spent on the website, on marketing, or on the business itself. Make the trade explicit.

If most answers point to "customers find me on Google and act once," invest in a great website and skip the app conversation entirely. Frequently asked questions

Do small businesses need a mobile app?

Most do not. A well-built, mobile-friendly website covers what most small businesses need: being found on Google, showing services and prices, taking enquiries and bookings, and selling online. An app earns its cost when customers use your business repeatedly, when you need features a browser cannot do well like offline access or constant push notifications, or when the app serves as a tool for your own staff.

How much does a mobile app cost for a small business in Canada?

A custom app for both iPhone and Android is a significantly larger investment than a website. A professionally built small business website typically starts in the low four figures in CAD, while a custom two-platform app usually lands in the five figures or more, plus ongoing maintenance for both platforms, OS updates, and app store compliance. Store developer fees are billed in USD: 99 USD per year for Apple and a one-time 25 USD fee for Google Play.

Can a website replace a mobile app?

For many small businesses, yes. A fast, mobile-friendly website handles enquiries, bookings, e-commerce, and repeat ordering without anyone downloading anything. A progressive web app (PWA) sits in the middle: a website that can be installed to a phone's home screen and in some cases sends push notifications, without going through an app store. The app advantage shows up for daily-use habits, offline work, and features that need the phone's hardware directly.

Should I build a website first or an app first?

Build the website first. It costs less, launches faster, works on every device, and gets found on Google, which sends you customers you did not pay to acquire. Add an app later once the business proves that customers want one - real demand, not a guess. The exception is if your whole business concept depends on app-only features like constant push alerts or offline field work; in that case the app is the product, not a marketing add-on.
