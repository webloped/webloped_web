How to get backlinks for a small business website: a practical guide for Canadian businesses

Backlinks are one of the oldest signals Google uses to judge whether your website is worth trusting. Here is how a small Canadian business earns them honestly, without buying links or paying for a scheme.

Picture two electricians in Kitchener. Both have clean websites, fair prices, and good reviews. One has been mentioned on the city chamber of commerce site, a local news story about a community project, and a supplier's dealer page. The other has no links from anywhere except its own social media profiles. When someone searches for an electrician in Kitchener, Google has to decide which of the two to show first, and the one other websites have vouched for has a real advantage.

That is the entire idea behind backlinks. A backlink is simply a link from another website to yours, and search engines treat it as a small vote of confidence. A link from a real, relevant site tells Google that someone other than you thought your page was worth pointing people to. One good link will not transform your rankings overnight, and a hundred bad ones can actively hurt you, but a steady collection of honest links from relevant sites is one of the most durable advantages a small business website can build.

The hard part is that backlinks are also one of the most misunderstood parts of SEO, and the industry around them is full of people selling shortcuts. This guide explains what backlinks actually do in 2026, what separates a good link from a useless one, and nine practical ways a Canadian small business can earn them, mostly for free, in the order you should try them.

The short answer

To get backlinks for a small business website, start with the free links you already qualify for: your Google Business Profile, the main Canadian business directories, your chamber of commerce, and your industry association. Then turn the relationships you already have into links: suppliers, partners, customers, and community organizations you work with. Then create at least one page on your site that is genuinely worth linking to, such as a useful local guide or a clear price guide, and tell the right people it exists. Finally, earn editorial links by helping local journalists and by writing for publications your customers actually read. That sequence, done steadily over a few months, beats any bulk link package you can buy.

The one rule that governs everything below: earn links from sites your customers would recognize and trust. If a link would look strange to a real customer who clicked it, it probably will not help you with Google either.

What a backlink is, and why it still matters

A backlink is a link on someone else's website that points to a page on yours. It is different from an internal link, which is a link between two pages on your own site. Both matter, but only a backlink is a third party saying something about you. Search engines have used links this way since the beginning: Google's original PageRank system judged a page partly by how many other pages linked to it, and Google still lists PageRank among its core ranking systems today.

Links help in three ways. First, they help Google discover your pages. When a search engine crawler follows a link from an established site to a page on yours, it finds that page faster than it would by waiting to stumble across it. Second, they pass along a measure of trust. A link from a respected local newspaper or an industry association carries more weight than a link from a brand new blog nobody reads. Third, they send real visitors. A link on a supplier's dealer page or a community sponsor page can bring people who are already looking for what you sell, and those visitors matter whether or not the link helps your rankings at all.

It is worth being honest about how much links matter now, because the honest answer is less than it used to be. At a conference in 2023, Google's Gary Illyes said links are not in the top three ranking signals and have not been for some time. In March 2024, Google quietly removed the word "important" from the link section of its spam policy documentation. And an Ahrefs study published in May 2025 found that only about 1.7 percent of new pages reached the top 10 of Google within a year, which shows how slowly new pages climb even with links behind them. None of that means links are dead. It means they are one signal among many, and they work best as part of a site that is already useful, fast, and relevant. If your pages are weak, links will not rescue them. If your pages are good, links are often the difference between page two and page one.

What makes a backlink good or bad

Not all links are equal, and the difference matters more than the total count. A small business with ten strong links will usually outrank a competitor with two hundred weak ones. Here is what to look for when you judge a link opportunity.

Relevance beats authority. A link from a local supplier's website, your city's business association, or a trade publication in your industry is worth more to you than a link from a high-traffic blog about an unrelated topic. Google reads the linking page for context, so a link surrounded by words about your trade tells Google more about what you do than a link on a generic page that links to everyone.

Editorial links beat self-made links. A link someone chose to place because your page was useful is worth far more than a link you placed yourself in a comment, a forum signature, or a low-quality directory. The whole point of a backlink is that someone else decided your page was worth recommending. Links you can create for yourself in bulk are the ones Google discounts most heavily.

Real traffic beats a high score. SEO tools assign authority scores to websites, and it is tempting to chase the highest number. But a link from a site your customers actually visit, such as a local news site, a community page, or a supplier directory, is more valuable than a link from a high-scoring site nobody reads. If the linking site gets no real visitors, the link is unlikely to carry much weight.

Anchor text should sound natural. Anchor text is the clickable wording of a link. When many links to your site all use the exact same keyword phrase, such as "best plumber Toronto," it looks manufactured. Natural links use a mix: your business name, your web address, a generic phrase like "their website," and occasionally a descriptive phrase. You cannot control what other people write, and you should not try to. Just do not ask everyone to use the same keyword.

Followed and nofollow links both have a place. A "nofollow" link carries a tag telling search engines not to pass ranking credit, though Google now treats the tag as a hint rather than a strict rule. Most links from social media profiles, directories, and many news sites are nofollow, and they are still worth having: they send visitors, they help people find you, and they make your link profile look natural. A healthy site has a mix of both. Do not turn down a good link just because it is nofollow.

One more distinction matters. A link from a new website domain, called a referring domain, counts more than a second link from a site that already links to you. Ten links from ten different local sites are worth far more than ten links from the same site. When you track progress, count referring domains, not total links.

Before you build any links: get the foundation right

Links to a site with nothing worth reading are wasted. Before you chase a single backlink, make sure there is something worth linking to. This is the step most businesses skip, and it is why so much link building fails.

First, make sure your main pages exist and are genuinely useful: a homepage that says clearly what you do and where you operate, one page per main service, an about page with real names and a real location, and a contact page with a phone number and a form. A journalist or a partner who is deciding whether to link to you will check your site first, and a thin or half-finished site is an easy reason to say no. Our guide to what pages a small business website should have covers this structure in detail.

Second, set up Google Search Console and submit your sitemap, if you have not already. It is free, and its Links report is where you will watch new backlinks appear and see which of your pages other sites link to most. Our guide to setting up Google Search Console walks through it step by step.

Third, write down one consistent version of your business name, address, and phone number, and use it everywhere. Search engines compare these details across the web to confirm you are one real business, and inconsistent details weaken that confidence. This is especially important for the directory listings in the first method below.

Finally, pick three target pages for your link building: usually your homepage, your main money page, and your best guide or resource. Most of your links should point to those pages. Spreading links thinly across twenty pages helps nobody, and it makes your progress harder to measure.

Method 1: claim the free listings you already qualify for

This is the fastest starting point, and it costs nothing but a few hours. There are listings your business already qualifies for, and many of them link to your website. Claim them, complete them properly, and make sure the details match the consistent version you wrote down.

Start with your Google Business Profile and Bing Places, which are free and feed the map results most local customers see first. Then add Apple Maps, Yelp, Yellow Pages, and 411.ca. Then look at the listings specific to your trade: your provincial or national industry association, any licensing body or trade directory for your profession, and the Canadian Federation of Independent Business member directory if you belong. Then add your local chamber of commerce and your Business Improvement Area, if your street or neighbourhood has one. Most chambers list members with a link to the member's website, and that link comes from a trusted local site, which is exactly the kind of link that helps.

A few honest warnings about directories. Most directory links carry little ranking weight on their own, and Google's spam policies list low-quality directory links among the examples of link spam. So do not pay for a service that submits you to hundreds of directories. A few dozen accurate, relevant listings beat hundreds of sloppy ones. And if a directory charges a large annual fee and cannot show you that real customers use it, skip it. Our guide to getting your business on Google covers the profile setup in more detail.

Method 2: turn relationships you already have into links

The easiest backlinks often come from people you already do business with. Suppliers, manufacturers, partners, and happy customers all have websites, and many of them have pages that exist precisely to list the businesses they work with.

Ask your suppliers whether they have a "find a dealer," "authorized installer," or "where to buy" page, and whether your business can be listed on it. Ask manufacturers you are certified for whether they list certified partners. Ask complementary local businesses whether a genuine recommendation makes sense: a realtor recommending a home inspector, a wedding venue recommending a photographer, a contractor recommending a supplier. A link that genuinely helps their customers is easy for them to say yes to, and a link swap that exists only to trade links is not. Google's spam policies list excessive link exchanges as link spam, so keep these honest: link to each other only where it helps a real visitor.

Do the same with your customers, where it fits. If you did notable work for a business that has a website, ask whether they would mention you on a project page or a testimonial page with a link. If you sponsor a local team, event, or charity, make sure the organizer links to your website from their sponsor page. And if you belong to any professional association, alumni group, or community organization, check that your member listing links to your site, and ask if it does not.

This method costs no money and no new content. It just requires asking, which is why it is the method most small businesses never get around to. Spend one afternoon making a list of ten relationships and sending ten short emails, and you will usually come away with two or three links that would have taken months to earn any other way.

Method 3: turn unlinked mentions into links

Some websites already mention your business by name but do not link to you. Those are the easiest links you will ever earn, because the hard part, getting noticed, is already done.

Search Google for your business name in quotation marks, then add a minus sign and your own domain, so your own pages drop out of the results. For example, search for "Webloped" -site:webloped.ca, replacing the name and domain with your own. Look through the results for pages that mention your business without linking: local news stories, event pages, supplier lists, community pages, and roundups of local businesses. Then send a short, polite email to the site owner or the author, thank them for the mention, and ask whether they would add a link so readers can find you. Most will say yes, because it makes their page more useful too.

Set a reminder to repeat this search every few months. Mentions accumulate slowly, and each one is a link waiting for a single email. While you are at it, check whether any old links to your site are broken, pointing to a page you deleted or renamed. If you find one, email the site owner with the correct address. Fixing a broken link is a favour to them as well as to you.

Method 4: earn links through local sponsorships and community work

Local links are the most valuable kind for a Canadian small business, and the most reliable way to earn them is to do things in your community that are worth mentioning. Sponsor a youth sports team and make sure the league links to your website from its sponsor page. Sponsor a local event, a school fundraiser, or a community festival, and check that the event page lists sponsors with links. Join your chamber of commerce or your Business Improvement Area and take part in something visible enough to be written about.

The key is that the activity has to be real. A sponsorship that exists only for the link is easy to spot, and it will not be mentioned anywhere worth linking from. A sponsorship of a team your own kids play on, an event in the neighbourhood you serve, or a cause your customers care about will naturally produce mentions, photos, and links from local sites, because local organizations link to their real supporters.

This method also has a side benefit that no other method offers: the links come with local reputation. A customer who sees your name on a community sponsor page already trusts you a little more before they ever visit your website. That trust is the same thing Google is trying to measure when it counts your links.

Method 5: create one page that is worth linking to

People link to pages that help their readers. Most small business websites have no page like that, only service pages and a contact form. If you create just one genuinely useful resource, it becomes the page other sites link to, and it keeps earning links long after you publish it.

Good options for a small business include a clear local guide, a price guide for your trade, a checklist, or a small piece of original data. A roofing company in Hamilton could publish a guide to what a roof replacement costs in Ontario and what affects the price. A bookkeeper could publish a checklist for new Ontario businesses registering for HST. A landscaper could publish a month-by-month guide to lawn care in southern Ontario. The topic should be something your customers actually search for, something you know better than a general writer, and something with a local angle that a national site cannot easily copy.

Original data is especially link-worthy, even on a small scale. A survey of your own customers, a summary of prices you have actually quoted, or a count of the most common questions your customers ask can all become a page that local journalists and other businesses cite. You do not need a thousand responses. Fifty real answers from your own customers is more interesting than a generic article, because it is the only place on the internet that data exists.

Keep the page honest and practical. A page that is mostly a sales pitch will not earn links, and a page that copies content from elsewhere will not rank. Write it in your own words, from your own experience, and update it once a year so it stays accurate. Our guide to writing website copy that converts covers the writing side, and the blog posts on this site are an example of the approach: one useful answer per page, written for a real reader.

Method 6: write for publications your customers read

A guest article on a site your buyers read puts your name in front of them and usually includes a link back to your website. This works when the publication is genuinely relevant and the article is genuinely useful. A financial advisor writing for a local business magazine, a contractor writing for a home renovation blog, or a physiotherapist writing for a community health site are all examples of a good fit. A generic article on an unrelated site with hundreds of guest posts is not.

Start with publications you already read and trust. Look for a "write for us" page, a contributors page, or an editor's email address, and pitch one specific idea, not a general offer to write something. Your pitch should say what the article would cover, why their readers would care, and why you are qualified to write it. Include a link to the resource page you created in method 5, so the editor can see the quality of your work before they reply.

Keep the article free of sales language. The point is to be helpful enough that readers trust you, and the link in your author bio does the rest. One well-placed guest article a quarter is far more useful than ten thin posts on sites nobody reads. And be careful with any site that charges you to publish a guest post without disclosing it: if you pay for a placement, Google's guidance is that the link should carry the sponsored or nofollow attribute, so it should not be sold to you as a ranking shortcut.

Method 7: broken link building

Broken link building is simple, polite, and underused. Websites link to pages that no longer exist, and site owners usually appreciate being told. You find those broken links, then offer your own page as a replacement.

Start with resource pages, local guides, and blog posts in your field: pages that list useful links for their readers. Use a free broken link checker, such as the Check My Links browser extension, to scan a page for links that return an error. When you find a broken link pointing to a page on your topic, email the site owner, point out the broken link, and suggest your relevant page as a replacement. You are helping them fix their page, which is why this works better than a cold request for a link out of nowhere.

This method works best when you already have the resource page from method 5, because you need something worth recommending as a replacement. It is slow, and most emails will go unanswered, but the links it produces are editorial, relevant, and exactly the kind Google values. Five polite emails a month is a realistic pace for a small business owner.

Method 8: help local journalists and answer expert requests

Journalists and local reporters need expert quotes, and a quote from a real local business owner is exactly what they want. A single quote in a local news story can bring a link from a trusted news site, plus real visitors who read the story.

The best-known service for this was HARO, Help a Reporter Out, which shut down in December 2024. The services that have taken its place include Qwoted and Featured, where journalists post requests for expert sources and you can answer the ones in your field. Sign up, set alerts for your trade and your city, and answer only the requests you are genuinely qualified for, with a short, specific answer and your real name, business, and website. Journalists work on deadlines, so a fast, useful reply beats a long one that arrives the next day.

Do not ignore local reporters either. A short email to a reporter at your city paper or a local online publication, introducing yourself as a local expert in your trade and offering to comment on stories in your field, can lead to quotes whenever your topic comes up in the news. A plumber who can explain a burst pipe story, an accountant who can explain a tax change, and a contractor who can explain a building code change are all useful sources, and local news sites link to their sources more often than people expect.

Method 9: find the links your competitors have that you do not

If a website links to your competitors, it might link to you too. This is the one method that usually needs a paid tool, such as Ahrefs or Semrush, which both start at roughly $100 USD a month or more. If you are not ready to pay for a tool, skip this method for now and come back to it later. The free methods above will keep you busy for months.

With a tool, enter a competitor's domain and look at their backlinks. Filter for the ones that look like real opportunities: local directories, industry lists, supplier pages, community sites, and publications. Ignore the links that come from sites you could never realistically get onto, and ignore the ones that look like paid placements or link schemes. What is left is a shortlist of sites that already link to businesses like yours, which makes them the warmest outreach targets you have.

Then approach each one with a reason, not just a request. If it is a directory, ask to be listed. If it is a list of local businesses, ask to be added. If it is a resource page, offer your resource page from method 5. If it is a publication, pitch a guest article. A site that links to your competitors is not your enemy. It is a map of where the links in your industry already live.

A realistic 90-day plan you can run yourself

Nine methods are a menu, not a to-do list. Pick two or three and run them properly. Here is a plan that fits into a few hours a month for a busy owner.

Days 1 to 30: the foundation. Set up Google Search Console and check your sitemap is submitted. Write down your consistent business name, address, and phone number. Pick your three target pages. Claim your Google Business Profile, Bing Places, Apple Maps, Yelp, Yellow Pages, and 411.ca listings, and complete them properly. Join or confirm your chamber of commerce and industry association listings. Email ten suppliers, partners, and customers about a link, and search for unlinked mentions of your business.

Days 31 to 60: one resource, plus outreach. Publish one genuinely useful resource page, such as a local guide or a price guide for your trade. Then tell people it exists: email the local sites and publications that link to similar pages, share it with your suppliers and partners, and pitch it as a replacement for one broken link you found. Follow up once with anyone who did not reply to your first round of emails.

Days 61 to 90: editorial links. Sign up for a journalist request service and answer two requests you are qualified for. Pitch one guest article to a publication your customers read. Repeat the unlinked mention search. Then check Google Search Console's Links report and write down how many referring domains you have gained, which pages they link to, and which of your target pages moved in the rankings.

That is a realistic first quarter. Expect a handful of new links, not hundreds. A local business that gains five to ten solid links in its first three months is doing well, and those links will keep working for years, unlike an ad that stops the day you stop paying.

What to avoid: the shortcuts that backfire

The link building industry sells shortcuts, and most of them will cost you more than they gain. Google's spam policies are explicit about what counts as link spam, and the penalties range from the links being ignored to a manual action against your site, which can take months to recover from.

Do not buy links in bulk, and do not pay a monthly fee for a package that promises hundreds of links. The links in those packages come from low-quality sites, link farms, and private blog networks that exist only to sell links, and Google is good at spotting them. If you pay for a placement on a real site your customers read, that can be legitimate advertising, but the link should carry the sponsored or nofollow attribute, and it should be judged on the visitors it sends, not on the ranking you hope it buys.

Do not join link exchanges, do not use automated tools to create links, and do not submit your site to every directory that emails you. Do not hide links, do not use keyword-stuffed anchor text, and do not create dozens of near-identical pages just to trade links between them. And never pay someone who guarantees a specific ranking or a specific number of links in thirty days. No honest provider can guarantee either, because rankings depend on your competitors and on Google's own changes.

If an offer sounds like a shortcut around earning trust, it almost always is. The money is better spent on real work that a local site would want to mention: a useful guide, a community sponsorship, or a better service page.

How to measure whether it is working

Link building fails quietly when nobody checks it. Once a month, spend thirty minutes on three numbers. First, open Google Search Console and check the Links report: how many websites link to you now, how many referring domains is that, and which of your pages are linked most. Second, check your rankings for the searches that matter to you, and note whether the target pages you chose are moving. Third, check the numbers that reflect real business: calls, contact form submissions, and enquiries, and whether any of them came from the sites that linked to you.

Do not judge a link by whether it moves a ranking in the first month. Links take time to be found, counted, and weighed, and their effect is usually visible over a quarter, not a week. What you are looking for is a slow, steady rise in referring domains from real, relevant sites, and a matching rise in enquiries from the pages those links point to. Keep a simple record of what you did and when, so you can tell which method produced which links, and put more effort into the method that works best for your business.

And if the numbers are flat after six months of honest effort, the problem is usually not the links. It is usually the pages they point to. Re-read your target pages with a customer's eyes: do they answer the question, do they make the next step obvious, and would you trust the business behind them? Fix the page first, then keep building links to it.

Frequently asked questions

How many backlinks does a small business website need?

There is no magic number, because it depends on your competitors. A business in a small town with few competitors online may rank well with ten to twenty good links. A business in a competitive Toronto market may need many more. The better question is how many your competitors have: check the top three businesses ranking for your main search, count their referring domains, and aim to match them over time with links of similar or better quality. Five strong local and industry links can move a small site further than fifty directory submissions.

How long do backlinks take to work?

Expect months, not days. Google has to find the link, crawl it, and weigh it against everything else it knows about your site, and that process is slow, especially for a newer site. Profile and directory links can show up in Search Console within weeks, but visible movement in rankings usually takes two to six months of steady work. Treat anyone who promises ranking improvements in thirty days as a warning sign.

Should I buy backlinks?

Buying links that pass ranking credit breaks Google's spam policies, and bulk link packages are the riskiest purchase in SEO. A paid placement on a real publication your customers read can be legitimate, but it should be judged as advertising, carry the sponsored or nofollow attribute, and be worth the money for the visitors it sends. If a seller promises ranking gains from paid links and will not explain where the links come from, walk away.

Can backlinks hurt my website?

Yes, bad ones can. Links from spam sites, link farms, and hacked pages can drag your site down, and Google can apply a manual action if it decides you participated in a link scheme. The good news is that Google says it can usually work out which links to ignore, so spammy links are often simply discounted rather than punished. You can also disavow links in Search Console if you find toxic ones pointing at your site, but for most small businesses the fix is simpler: stop buying bad links, and earn good ones instead.
