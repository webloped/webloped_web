What is schema markup? A plain-English guide for small business websites

You have probably seen search results with gold stars under a business name, or a price and stock status right in the listing, or a little box answering a question before you even click. Those extras come from schema markup. It is a small block of code that tells Google exactly what your page is about, in a format Google can read without guessing. This guide explains what it is, which types a small business actually needs, how to add it on the main platforms, and how to test it for free.

The short answer

What is schema markup? It is structured data: a small block of code, invisible to visitors, that labels the facts on a web page so search engines can read them precisely. Your page already shows your business name, address, opening hours, and prices in plain text. Schema markup repeats those same facts in a standard format, using a shared vocabulary from Schema.org, a project the major search engines maintain together.

Think of it this way. A human reading your contact page can tell that "12 King Street West, Toronto" is an address and "Open until 6 PM" is a closing time. Google reading the same page just sees text, and text can be ambiguous. Is "Mercury" a planet, a metal, or a person? Is "12 Park Road" an address or a product name? Schema markup removes the guesswork by labelling each fact: this string is an address, this number is a price, this is a review score.

The most common format is JSON-LD, a short block of script that sits in the page and is kept separate from the visible HTML. Google recommends JSON-LD, and most modern plugins and platforms output it automatically, so you will rarely need to write it by hand.

One honest note up front: schema markup is not a ranking switch. Google has said structured data helps it understand content and can make a page eligible for richer search results, but it does not promise higher positions. Its real value is clarity. A clearer page can earn a better-looking listing, and a better-looking listing gets more clicks. That is worth doing, as long as nobody sells it to you as a shortcut to position one.

What schema markup actually does for a small business

For a small business, schema markup produces two meaningful benefits, and both matter more than most owners expect.

1. Richer search listings. When Google understands a page clearly, it can show extra detail in the listing. A product page can show a price, availability, and review stars. A local business listing can show opening hours. A recipe can show a cooking time. These extras are called rich results, and they make your listing bigger and more useful than the plain blue link next to it. More useful listings get clicked more often, even at the same ranking position.

2. Clearer understanding for search and AI answers. Search engines and AI answer tools increasingly pull facts directly from pages to answer questions. A page whose facts are labelled is easier to quote accurately: your services, your prices, your location, your hours. Our guide on how to show up in AI search covers the wider picture, and schema markup is one of the practical pieces of it.

There is one limit worth knowing. Google stopped showing FAQ rich results for most websites in 2023, limiting them to well-known government and health sites, and retired HowTo rich results the same year. You can still add FAQPage markup and it can help Google understand the page, but do not expect a FAQ box to appear under your listing. Anyone promising FAQ rich results for a small business site in 2026 is working from old information.

Which schema types a small business actually needs

Schema.org defines hundreds of types. You will use only a few. The rule is simple: mark up what is actually on the page, and skip everything else. The table below covers the types that matter for most Canadian small businesses, and where each one belongs.

  • Organization: home page or about page. Defines your brand: name, logo, website, social profiles, and contact points. Use this when you serve customers online or across a wide area and have no single storefront that matters.
  • LocalBusiness (or a subtype such as Restaurant, Dentist, or HairSalon): home page and each location page. Adds address, phone number, opening hours, geo-coordinates, and area served. Use this when customers visit you, or when you serve a defined area from a real base. For a Canadian business, the address should match your Google Business Profile exactly, including the same spelling and postal code.
  • WebSite: home page. States the site name, which can influence the name Google shows in search results.
  • BreadcrumbList: every page. Describes the navigation path, such as Home, Services, Web Design, which Google can show in place of the plain URL in results.
  • Service: service pages. Describes a service you offer, its name, and the area it covers. Useful for trades, clinics, studios, and agencies whose pages are built around services rather than products.
  • Product: product pages only. Name, image, price in CAD, availability, and reviews you genuinely hold. Only use it where a price is shown on the page. Do not mark up products you do not sell online, or prices that are not visible to visitors.
  • Article or BlogPosting: blog posts and guides. Author, publication date, and headline. This is the type on every post in this blog, including this one.
  • FAQPage: pages with a real questions-and-answers section. Only for questions and answers that are actually visible on the page.

If you only do two things, add Organization or LocalBusiness on your home page and BreadcrumbList across the site. Those two cover the identity of your business and the structure of your site, which is most of what a small business gets from schema markup.

How to add schema markup, by platform

The right method depends on your platform and how much control you need. Most small businesses can work through the same steps in order: audit what you already have, pick one source of truth, fill in accurate details, then test.

1. Audit what you already have. Run your key pages (home, contact, a service page, a product page, a blog post) through Google's Rich Results Test and the Schema Markup Validator. Many themes and plugins already output some markup, and you may find you already have the basics. Fixing what exists is faster than adding a second layer on top of it.

2. WordPress. An SEO plugin such as Yoast or Rank Math outputs Organization, WebSite, BreadcrumbList, and Article automatically. Fill in your business details in the plugin settings, and it does the rest. The usual problem is duplication: your theme, your page builder, and your reviews plugin can each add their own Organization block, and Google sees three conflicting versions of your business. Pick one source and switch the others off.

3. Wix and Squarespace. Both include basic schema markup by default, and their SEO settings let you edit business details. You have less control than on WordPress, but for most small businesses the built-in output is enough. Check it with the Rich Results Test rather than assuming it is right.

4. Shopify. Themes output Product markup on product pages, and apps can add Organization and other types. As with WordPress, watch for duplication: a theme and an app adding the same Product block with different prices is a common error on stores.

5. Custom or hand-built sites. Your developer adds a JSON-LD block to each page template, so new pages inherit it automatically. The cleanest setup is one connected graph: give the home page Organization an identifier such as https://webloped.ca/#organization, then reference that same identifier as the provider on every Service page and the publisher on every Article. That way Google sees one business, not a dozen disconnected copies.

6. Schema generators. For a single page where a plugin does not apply, a free generator such as Google's Structured Data Markup Helper produces JSON-LD from a form. You fill in the fields, copy the output, and paste it into the page. Generators are a good way to learn what the code looks like, but do not paste generated markup into dozens of pages by hand. That is what templates and plugins are for.

The Canadian details that matter

Schema markup is the same worldwide, but a few details are specific to a Canadian small business.

Consistency is the whole game. Your name, address, and phone number in the markup must match what is visible on your site and what appears on your Google Business Profile, to the letter. If your site says "Webloped, Cambridge, Ontario" and your profile says something slightly different, Google has to guess which one is right, and the markup stops helping. Our guide on local SEO for small business covers the same consistency rule for listings and directories, and it applies here too.

Use CAD for prices. Product and Service markup includes a price currency. Set it to CAD for Canadian customers, and make sure the price in the markup matches the price shown on the page. A mismatch is one of the most common errors Google flags, and it can remove the rich result entirely.

Opening hours and holidays. LocalBusiness markup includes opening hours, and Google compares them with your Business Profile. Keep both updated for statutory holidays, which differ by province, and for summer or winter hours. A customer who drives to a shop that the search result said was open is a customer you will not get back.

Reviews must be genuine. Only mark up reviews that appear on your own page and that you genuinely collected. Do not copy review stars from Google into your own markup, and never mark up reviews you do not have. Google checks, and misusing review markup is one of the fastest ways to lose rich results. If you want more reviews to show honestly, our guide on how to get more Google reviews covers the practical steps, and our guide on adding Google reviews to your website covers the display side.

How to test schema markup for free

Never publish markup and assume it works. Two free Google tools check it, and they answer different questions.

Rich Results Test tells you whether a page is eligible for rich results, and which types Google detected. Paste in a page address or a block of code, and it lists every detected type with errors and warnings. Fix errors first. Warnings are worth fixing too, but they do not block the rich result the way errors do.

Schema Markup Validator checks every type, not just the ones that produce rich results. It is the tool for confirming that your Organization, Service, and BreadcrumbList blocks are valid even when they will never show as a fancy listing.

After testing, watch the enhancement reports in Google Search Console. Search Console shows how many pages Google found with each type, and flags errors across the whole site. Check it monthly, and after any redesign, new plugin, or new template, because each one can change what markup your pages output.

Common schema markup mistakes

Most schema problems on small business sites come from the same short list. Check for these before you add anything new.

  • Marking up invisible content. Only mark up facts that visitors can see on the page. Hidden prices, hidden reviews, or a phone number that exists only in the code will get the markup ignored, or worse.
  • Duplicate blocks. Two or three Organization blocks from a theme, a plugin, and a page builder, each saying something slightly different. Pick one source and turn the others off.
  • Stale details. Old prices, old hours, a previous address, or a phone number you changed two years ago. Markup is code, so it does not update itself when you edit the visible page. Update both, or generate the markup from the same source as the visible content.
  • Wrong type for the page. Product markup on a service page, or LocalBusiness on a national online store with no physical location. Match the type to what the page actually is.
  • Expecting instant results. Adding markup does not make rich results appear the same day. Google has to recrawl the page, process the markup, and decide the page is eligible. That can take days or weeks, and some pages will never get a rich result even with perfect markup. That is normal, and it is not a reason to keep adding more types.

A sensible priority order

If your site has no schema markup today, do it in this order. Each step takes under an hour, and you can stop after step two and still have covered most of the value.

  • Step 1: test your home page, contact page, and one service page with the Rich Results Test. Note what already exists.
  • Step 2: add Organization or LocalBusiness on the home page, with your exact name, address, phone, hours, and logo. Add BreadcrumbList across the site.
  • Step 3: add Service markup to your main service pages and Article markup to blog posts, if your plugin or theme does not already do it.
  • Step 4: add Product markup only where you sell priced items online, with CAD prices that match the page.
  • Step 5: re-test every template, fix errors, then check Search Console monthly. Re-test after any redesign or plugin change.

One honest note on expectations: schema markup will not, by itself, move a page from position 30 to position 3. Content relevance, your Google Business Profile, reviews, and links do the heavy lifting in local search. What schema does is make sure Google reads your facts correctly, and give your listing the best chance of showing the extra detail that earns the click. For a business that lives on phone calls and form submissions, that is reason enough. If you want a broader view of how search works for a small business, start with our guide on what SEO is.

Frequently asked questions

What is schema markup?

Schema markup, also called structured data, is a small block of code added to a web page that labels the facts on that page in a format search engines can read without guessing. It tells Google that a string of text is a business address, a price, an opening time, or a review score, rather than leaving Google to work it out from the page text alone.

Does schema markup improve Google rankings?

Schema markup does not directly raise rankings. Google has said structured data helps it understand content and can make a page eligible for rich results, such as review stars or product details in the search listing, which can improve click-through. It does not outweigh relevant content, a complete Google Business Profile, and genuine reviews.

How do I add schema markup to my website?

On WordPress, an SEO plugin such as Yoast or Rank Math adds the basics automatically. On Wix and Squarespace, basic markup is built in. On Shopify, themes and apps add Product markup. On a custom site, your developer adds a JSON-LD block to each page template. Test every page with Google's Rich Results Test and the Schema Markup Validator, both free.

What schema does a small business website need?

Most small businesses need only a few types: Organization or LocalBusiness on the home page, WebSite on the home page, BreadcrumbList across the site, Service on service pages, Article on blog posts, and Product only on pages that sell a priced item. Add only types that match content actually visible on the page.
