How to respond to a negative Google review

A negative review feels personal, but your reply is not really for the reviewer. It is for the next person reading your profile and deciding whether to trust you. A short, calm reply that owns what went wrong and moves the conversation offline does more for your business than a perfect record ever could.

Learning how to respond to a negative Google review starts with that shift in audience. The person who wrote the review may never come back, and that is fine. Dozens or hundreds of future customers will read the review and your reply side by side before they call you. They are not judging whether you were right. They are judging how you handle it when something goes wrong, because something will eventually go wrong for them too, and they want to know you will take care of it.

This guide gives you a reply formula you can use for almost any negative review, worked examples for the complaints Canadian small businesses see most often, and a clear process for the reviews that should be reported to Google instead of answered. It also covers what never to put in a public reply, and what to do after you reply so the same complaint does not keep appearing.

Step 1: Pause, then sort the review into one of four types

Do not reply in the first rush of feeling. Read the review twice, step away if you need to, and check the facts with your team before you write anything. A reply written while you are upset is the one that gets screenshotted and shared. A reply written an hour later, after you have checked what actually happened, is the one that helps.

Most negative reviews fall into one of four types, and the type decides your next move:

  • A fair complaint. The customer describes a real experience: a late arrival, a missed call, a service that did not meet expectations. This is the most common type, and the most valuable. Reply with the formula in Step 2, fix what you can offline, and fix the underlying problem if it keeps recurring.
  • A vague low rating. One or two stars with no text, or a few words like "Terrible" or "Would not recommend." There is nothing specific to fix, so your reply is short: thank them, say you would like to understand what happened, and give a direct way to reach you. Future readers mostly notice that you responded at all.
  • A mix-up. The review describes a service you do not offer, a location you do not have, or an experience that does not match your records. Do not accuse the reviewer of lying in public. Say calmly that you cannot find a record matching their description, invite them to contact you with details so you can check, and report the review only if you have reason to believe it was posted on the wrong listing.
  • A review that breaks Google rules. Fake reviews, reviews from competitors or current employees, spam, reviews containing personal contact details, hateful or profane content, or reviews about something unrelated to a customer experience. Report these through Google, as described in Step 5, and still post a brief, calm reply while you wait, because removal is never guaranteed.

If you are not sure which type you have, treat it as a fair complaint until you have evidence otherwise. A defensive reply to a genuine customer costs far more than a polite reply to a fake one.

Step 2: Use the five-part reply formula

A good reply to a negative Google review has five parts, in this order, and it is usually three to five sentences long. Longer replies start to sound like arguments, even when they are polite.

  • 1. Thank them by name. Use the name shown on the review. "Thank you for letting us know, Sarah" is warmer than "Dear customer," and it signals to readers that a real person is behind the business.
  • 2. Acknowledge the experience, without arguing the facts. "I am sorry the appointment ran late and you were left waiting" acknowledges what they felt. You do not need to agree with every detail, and you should not relitigate the visit in public. Acknowledging is not the same as admitting fault for everything claimed.
  • 3. Own the part that is yours. If you were late, if the phone was not answered, if the work was not up to your usual standard, say so plainly. "That is not the standard we hold ourselves to" works because it is specific about your standard, not vague about the problem. Readers trust businesses that can name a mistake.
  • 4. Move the conversation offline, with a named contact. Give a first name, a phone number or email address, and an invitation: "Please call me at this number so I can look into it properly." Do not ask the reviewer to send personal details in a public reply, and do not discuss refunds, account details, or employee matters where everyone can read them.
  • 5. Close with what changes. One sentence about the fix, if you have one: "We have changed how we confirm afternoon appointments so this does not happen again." Only promise a change you have actually made. An empty promise in a public reply is worse than no promise at all.

Reply in the language the reviewer used, if you can. If you serve customers in Quebec or in a bilingual community, a reply in French to a French review, or at least an offer to continue in French, shows the same care as the rest of the reply. Keep the tone the same as you would use face to face with a disappointed customer standing in your shop: respectful, specific, and brief.

Step 3: How to post the reply

Replies to Google reviews are posted from your Google Business Profile, not from a personal Google account. The exact screens change from time to time, but the path is consistent:

  • Sign in to your Google Business Profile. Use the business account that manages the listing, not a personal account. If someone else set up the listing, ask them to add you as a manager rather than sharing a password.
  • Open Reviews. In the Business Profile dashboard, choose Reviews to see all reviews for the listing, newest first.
  • Find the review and choose Reply. Write your reply in the reply box, read it once more, and post it. You can edit a reply afterwards, but the reviewer is notified when you reply, so aim to get it right the first time.
  • Check the reply on your public listing. Open your listing the way a customer would, in Google Search or Google Maps, and confirm the reply appears under the review and reads well on a phone.

Aim to reply within a day or two. Speed matters less than tone, but a review that sits unanswered for weeks tells readers that nobody is watching the listing. If the complaint is serious, or if you need to check records first, a short holding reply is better than silence, followed by a fuller reply once you know the facts. Step 4: Example replies for the complaints you will actually get

The examples below are templates, not real reviews. Adapt the specifics to what actually happened, keep the structure, and never copy a template word for word for every review. Readers notice identical replies, and identical replies suggest nobody read the complaint.

Late service or a missed appointment

Review: "Booked for 10 am, nobody showed up until 11. Wasted my whole morning."

Reply: "Thank you for telling us, and I am sorry we kept you waiting. You booked time out of your morning and we did not respect it, and that is not how we work. Please call me, Alex, at 555-0100 so I can find out what happened with your booking and make it right. We have changed our morning confirmations so a delay like this is flagged before it reaches you."

Why it works: it names the specific harm, the wasted morning, instead of a generic apology. It owns the scheduling failure without blaming traffic, staff, or the customer, and it gives a named person and a number.

Rude or unhelpful staff

Review: "Staff were rude and dismissive when I asked a simple question."

Reply: "Thank you for sharing this. I am sorry you were treated that way when you came to us for help. That is not the experience we want anyone to have here. I would like to hear more about what happened so I can address it with the team. Please email me at the address on our profile, and ask for Priya. I will personally follow up."

Why it works: it does not name or blame an individual employee in public, and it does not demand proof. Employee conversations belong offline. The reply shows the reviewer, and every future reader, that the feedback reached the person in charge.

The work or product did not meet expectations

Review: "Paid for a premium service and got a rushed job. Not worth the money."

Reply: "Thank you for the honest feedback, and I am sorry the work did not match what you paid for. We would rather know than have you leave feeling that way. Please contact me directly so I can review the job and discuss what we can do to put it right. You can reach me at the number on our listing, and I will answer personally."

Why it works: it does not argue about what "premium" should have included, and it does not offer a specific refund or discount in public. Compensation, if any is offered, is discussed privately, where the details can be handled properly.

A price complaint

Review: "Way overpriced. Charged way more than I expected."

Reply: "Thank you for letting us know. I am sorry the price came as a surprise, and I understand why that is frustrating. Our pricing is based on the time, materials, and warranty included in the job, and we should have made that clearer before we started. Please call me so I can walk through your invoice with you and answer any questions. I am happy to explain exactly what the charge covered."

Why it works: it explains the basis for pricing in general terms without publishing the customer's invoice or debating the amount in public. A price complaint is often a communication complaint, and this reply addresses the communication.

A one-star rating with no explanation

Review: One star, no text.

Reply: "Thank you for the feedback. I am sorry to see the one-star rating, and I would genuinely like to understand what went wrong so we can fix it. Please contact us at the number or email on our profile and ask for the owner. We would appreciate the chance to make it right."

Why it works: there is nothing to argue with, so the reply does not invent a story. It is short, it invites contact, and it leaves future readers with the impression of a business that pays attention even when no details are given.

What never to do in a public reply

  • Do not argue, correct, or contradict the reviewer in public. Even if you are right about the facts, a public argument reads as defensive to everyone else. State your position once, briefly, if you must, and move the detail offline.
  • Do not call the reviewer a liar, a competitor, or a fake. If you believe a review is fake, report it to Google, as described below. Accusing someone publicly, when you might be wrong, creates a second problem that is harder to fix than the review.
  • Do not share personal information. Never post a customer's address, phone number, order number, appointment details, health information, or anything from a private conversation. Apart from the privacy problem, it tells future customers that their details might end up in public too.
  • Do not offer payment, discounts, or freebies in exchange for changing a review. Offering something of value in return for a better review can breach Google review policies and, in Canada, can raise issues under misleading advertising rules. If you resolve a complaint privately, you can ask once, politely, whether the reviewer would like to update their review to reflect the outcome. You cannot pay them to do it, and you cannot pressure them.
  • Do not threaten legal action in the reply. If a review is genuinely defamatory, that is a matter for a lawyer, not a public comment. A legal threat in a reply escalates the situation, alarms future readers, and rarely leads to the review being removed. Get advice privately before you take any legal step.
  • Do not use the same reply twice. A template is a starting point. Change the name, the detail, and the fix every time. Identical replies under different complaints tell readers that nobody is actually reading.
  • Do not reply when you are angry. Draft it, leave it, and have someone else read it before you post. If no one else is available, read it aloud. If any sentence sounds like something you would not say to a customer in person, rewrite it.

Step 5: Fake reviews and reviews that break the rules

Google does not remove a review simply because it is negative, unfair, or bad for business. A business cannot delete a review itself. Google removes reviews that violate its review policies, and the bar is higher than most owners expect. The categories that matter most for small businesses are fake engagement (reviews from people who were never customers, or reviews posted to manipulate ratings), conflicts of interest (reviews from owners, employees, or competitors), spam, reviews containing personal information, profanity or hateful content, and reviews that are off topic, meaning they describe something other than a customer experience with the business.

To report a review, sign in to your Google Business Profile, find the review, choose the report option, and select the reason that fits best. Describe the problem factually and briefly: for example, that you have no record of the reviewer as a customer and the review describes a service you do not offer. Report a review once, with the most accurate reason, rather than reporting it repeatedly or under several reasons, which does not help. Google reviews reported reviews over a period that is usually days, and you can check the status of a reported review in your profile. If a report is rejected, there is an appeal option in the review management tools where you can provide more detail about which policy you believe applies.

While you wait, post the brief reply described in Step 1 for a mix-up: calm, factual, and inviting the reviewer to contact you. Do not write "This is a fake review" as your reply. If Google does not remove it, that reply stays on your profile permanently, and it will look like an accusation you could not support. The calmer version, "We cannot find a record matching this review, and we would like to look into it, please contact us," ages well either way.

If a review contains a threat, or if you believe someone is posting reviews to harass or extort your business, keep records of everything, report each review, and get advice from a lawyer or your local police non-emergency line if the behaviour crosses into threats or harassment. This guide is general information, not legal advice, and a lawyer can tell you what applies to your specific situation. Do not engage with the reviewer publicly beyond the brief reply, and do not pay anyone who offers to remove reviews for a fee. No third party can guarantee removal, and paying for review manipulation can get your own listing in trouble.

Step 6: What to do after you reply

The reply is the visible half of the work. The quieter half is what stops the next negative review from saying the same thing.

  • Resolve it offline, quickly. Call or email the reviewer within a day of replying, using the contact details you already have or the ones they provide. Listen first, fix what you can, and confirm the outcome in writing so there is no confusion about what was agreed.
  • Ask once whether they would update the review. If the problem is resolved, one polite ask is fair: "If you feel the outcome reflects the service we aim to give, you are welcome to update your review." Do not ask repeatedly, do not offer anything in return, and accept a no gracefully. Some reviewers update the review themselves, some add a follow-up comment, and some leave it. All three are fine.
  • Look for the pattern, not just the incident. One complaint about slow replies is an incident. Three in two months is a process problem. Tag negative reviews by cause, late arrival, communication, pricing surprise, quality, and review the tags monthly. Fix the most common cause first. Our guide to ranking higher on Google Maps explains why this matters beyond reputation: a listing with steady reviews, answered questions, and recent activity is the listing Google shows to nearby customers.
  • Keep earning positive reviews. A single negative review hurts far less when it sits among many recent, detailed positive ones. Ask every happy customer, at the moment they are happiest, with a direct link. Do not ask only your happiest customers and skip everyone else, and do not offer incentives for positive reviews. A steady, honest flow of reviews is the best long-term defence, and it is the system our reviews guide walks through step by step.
  • Reply to positive reviews too. If you only reply to complaints, your profile sends a strange message: complaints get attention, praise does not. A short, specific thank you to positive reviewers, naming what they mentioned, takes a minute and shows future readers that you notice good feedback as well as bad. Mixed reviews, three stars with both praise and a complaint, deserve a reply that thanks the reviewer for the praise and addresses the complaint, in that order.

A simple weekly routine that keeps reviews under control

Review management does not need software for most small businesses. It needs a routine and one person responsible for it. Twice a week is enough for most Canadian small businesses: check for new reviews, reply to any that need a reply, and note any pattern worth raising with the team. Turn on review notifications in your Google Business Profile so a new review reaches you the same day, rather than being discovered weeks later during a quiet moment.

Before posting any reply, run this checklist:

  • Did I use the reviewer's name and the specific thing they mentioned?
  • Is the reply three to five sentences, with no personal information and no employee names?
  • Does it move the conversation offline, with a named contact and a real phone number or email?
  • Would I be comfortable if this reply were shown to my next ten customers?
  • Has someone else read it, or have I read it aloud and slept on it if it upset me?

If the answer to any of those is no, fix it before you post. A reply is public and effectively permanent, even though Google lets you edit it. The reviewer is notified when you reply, and future customers will read the exchange as a whole. The goal is not to win the exchange. The goal is to leave every reader, including the reviewer, thinking this is a business that listens and fixes problems.

Frequently asked questions

Should I reply to every negative Google review?

Yes, reply to every negative review, and to positive reviews when you can. An unanswered negative review looks ignored, while a calm reply shows future readers that the business is active and accountable. The exception is a review you have reported as fake or policy-breaking: still post a brief, calm reply while you wait for Google, because removal is never certain, and silence while you wait can look like indifference.

Can I get a negative Google review removed?

Only if it violates Google review policies, such as fake engagement, a conflict of interest, spam, or personal information. You cannot remove a review because it is negative, unfair, or factually wrong in your view, and you cannot delete a review yourself. Report it through your Google Business Profile with the most accurate reason, check the status, and use the appeal option if it is rejected and you can explain which policy applies. For genuine complaints, a good reply and a resolved outcome help more than a removal request.

What if the review is fake, or from someone I have never served?

Check your records first, including bookings, invoices, and messages, under the name and details in the review. If you find nothing, reply calmly that you cannot locate a record matching the review and invite the reviewer to contact you with details so you can check properly. Report the review to Google if you believe it is fake engagement or was posted on the wrong listing. Do not accuse the reviewer publicly, and do not share any customer records in your reply to prove your point.

Should I offer a refund or discount in my public reply?

No. Keep compensation, refunds, and discounts out of the public reply, and discuss them privately once you have the facts. A public offer invites every future reader to expect the same deal, turns the reply into a negotiation, and can look like you are paying for a better review. The public reply should acknowledge the problem, own your part, and invite the reviewer to contact you directly. What you offer to resolve it is a private conversation.
