How much does a website redesign cost in Canada in 2026?

Ask three agencies and you will get three wildly different answers, and all three can be honest. The word "redesign" covers everything from a fresh coat of paint to a full rebuild. Here is what each kind costs in CAD, and how to tell which one you need.

When a Canadian business owner asks "how much does a website redesign cost in Canada?", the honest answer starts with a question back: what do you mean by redesign? One person means new colours and fonts on the same site. Another means tearing everything down and starting over. Those are two completely different jobs, and confusing them is how budgets get blown.

Industry pricing guides published in 2026 put the full spread at roughly $1,000 to $40,000+ CAD, and the difference between the bottom and the top is entirely about scope. This article breaks down the three kinds of "redesign", what each one typically costs in Canada, what drives the price up or down, and how to figure out which tier your site actually needs. These are published market ranges to help you budget, not quotes: any real price has to be scoped against your actual pages and features.

The short answer: redesign costs by scope

If you want the numbers up front, here they are. Each tier is explained in detail below.

  • Visual refresh: $1,000 - $3,500 CAD. Same site, same platform, same content. New colours, typography, hero images, and spacing. Best when the site works but looks dated.
  • Full front-end redesign: $2,500 - $8,000 CAD. Same platform and mostly the same content, but the design, layout, and navigation are rebuilt. Typical for a 5 to 15 page service-business site.
  • Strategic redesign: $8,000 - $25,000 CAD. Research-driven rebuild for 15 to 50 pages: new design system, buyer-journey planning, integrations like CRM or booking, and a formal SEO preservation plan.
  • Ecommerce or complex rebuild: $12,000 - $40,000+ CAD. Online stores, platform migrations, memberships, bookings, or deep integrations. Timelines and budgets scale with catalog size and feature list.

Note what is missing from these ranges: tax. In Ontario, web design services are subject to 13% HST, so budget for that on top of whatever number you land on. And if you are pricing a brand-new site rather than a redo of an existing one, our guide on how much a website costs in Canada covers the from-scratch numbers.

Type 1: the visual refresh ($1,000 - $3,500 CAD)

A visual refresh keeps everything structural exactly where it is: the same CMS, the same URL structure, the same back end, the same navigation. The work is surface-level: updated brand colours, new typography, fresh hero graphics, button styles, spacing, and imagery. Think of it as repainting the house without moving any walls.

This is the right tier when your site is fundamentally healthy but looks tired. Traffic is fine, the structure makes sense, the content is accurate, and customers can find what they need. It just looks like it was built in 2019, because it was.

Be honest about what a refresh will not fix. It will not repair bad navigation, speed problems, broken booking flows, or thin content. If the complaints you hear are about the site being confusing or slow rather than ugly, a new paint job just makes the problems prettier. Run through our checklist of the signs your business needs a new website first: if you are ticking several structural boxes, a refresh is money spent avoiding the real problem.

Type 2: the full front-end redesign ($2,500 - $8,000 CAD)

This is what most small businesses mean when they say redesign. The platform stays the same and most of the content stays too, but the design, page layouts, and navigation are rebuilt from scratch. New templates for the homepage, service pages, about page, and contact page. Mobile-first layouts. New or rebuilt forms. Basic on-page SEO baked in rather than bolted on.

A typical small-business scope is 5 to 15 standard pages: Home, About, Services, a few service detail pages, testimonials, and Contact. Published 2026 pricing data puts this band at $2,500 to $8,000 for service companies and local firms, and that matches what most Canadian freelancers and small studios charge for a competent job. At this tier you should expect a CMS you can actually edit, proper responsive design, and image optimisation as standard, not as upsells.

Watch for one common trap at this tier: paying for a redesign while keeping a broken foundation. If your site is slow because the theme itself is heavy, or the page builder is the foundation of every page, a front-end redesign on the same platform may not get you the speed you want. Our article on why websites are so slow explains how to tell whether the problem is the paint or the frame before you budget for the wrong one.

Type 3: the strategic redesign ($8,000 - $25,000 CAD)

This is where the work shifts from design to strategy. Before any layout is drawn, someone studies your buyers, your competitors, and your conversion paths. The site is rebuilt around how customers actually decide to buy from you: what they read first, what proof they need, and what the one clear next step is on every page.

Published pricing guides describe this tier as covering 15 to 50 pages with multiple custom templates for different service lines or locations, CRM integration (HubSpot, Salesforce, or similar), blog migration, and a structured SEO preservation plan so the redesign does not tank your Google rankings. Mid-market firms choose this scope when the website is their primary way of winning business, not just a brochure that exists.

This tier is overkill for a local plumber with a five-page site, and it is underpowered for a national ecommerce brand. That is the point of scoping: the right tier is the one that matches what the site has to earn for you.

Type 4: the platform migration, the rebuild in disguise ($2,500 - $40,000+ CAD)

A special case worth its own section: changing platforms. Moving from Wix or Squarespace to WordPress, or from an old custom site to Shopify, is sold as a "migration", but it is really a rebuild with your content poured back in. Your layouts live inside the old platform and cannot leave it, so what you are buying is new construction, not a move.

A Canadian migration guide published in 2026 breaks it down this way: a content-only lift (under 15 pages, theme-standard design, redirect map, DNS cutover) runs $1,200 to $2,500; a standard small-business rebuild with custom layouts runs $2,500 to $6,500; a design-led rebuild with a new design system runs $6,500 to $12,000; and ecommerce, memberships, or bilingual builds run $12,000 to $40,000 or more.

The migration question to ask yourself: does the new platform earn back the rebuild cost? Moving to Shopify makes sense if you are starting to sell online seriously. Moving from Wix to WordPress for "more control" only makes sense if you will actually use that control. Otherwise you have paid rebuild prices for a lateral move.

What moves the price up or down

Within any tier, a handful of factors decide whether you land at the low or high end. Understanding these helps you read quotes like a builder instead of a buyer.

  • How much content exists. Pages, blog posts, products, testimonials. Every page has to be audited, moved, reformatted, and QA-tested. A 40-page site costs more than a 6-page site for the same reason moving a five-bedroom house costs more than moving a studio.
  • Whether the platform changes. Same-platform redesigns reuse content, URLs, and hosting. Platform changes add migration work, redirect mapping, and re-training. That is usually the single biggest cost jump.
  • Reskin versus design system. Copying your old layout with new colours is cheap. Building a new design system (consistent components, new UX, new information architecture) takes real design hours, and that is where professional quotes climb.
  • SEO preservation. Redirect maps, keeping valuable URLs, migrating schema markup, carrying analytics over, monitoring rankings after launch. Skipping this is how redesigns lose half their Google traffic. Budgeting for it is cheap insurance; skipping it is expensive.
  • Integrations and features. Online booking, payment processing, CRM connections, email marketing hooks, member logins. Every integration is configuration, testing, and something that can break later. Our builder versus developer comparison covers which features push a project from DIY territory into professional territory.
  • Content and photography. If your text and photos are ready, great. If the redesign needs copywriting, new photography, or someone to wrangle ten years of scattered documents into coherent pages, that is real billable work. The content is usually the bottleneck that delays launches, not the code.
  • Accessibility. In Ontario, organisations with 20 or more employees are required to meet AODA web accessibility standards, and the practical standard everywhere is WCAG 2.0 Level AA: contrast, keyboard navigation, alt text, readable typography. Building it in during a redesign costs a little. Retrofitting it later costs a lot.

The hidden costs people forget to budget

The quote covers the build. These are the costs that ambush people after they sign:

  • Content production. Copywriting, photography, and video are almost always priced separately from design and development. Ask explicitly whether the quote includes them.
  • Redirect mapping and QA. Cheap quotes skip this; then old Google links 404 and rankings drop. Make sure the quote says "redirect map" in writing.
  • Downtime and launch risk. A bad launch on a busy week can cost you real revenue. Ask how the provider launches (staged, tested, reversible) and schedule it for a quiet period.
  • Training. You need to know how to update the site yourself. A handoff session or a short training video should be part of the deal, not a favour.
  • Maintenance after launch. The redesign is day one. Updates, backups, security, and monitoring start on day two. Our Canadian maintenance pricing guide breaks down what an ongoing plan should cost and include, so you can budget the full year instead of just the build.

How to keep a redesign on budget: five rules

  1. Decide the tier before you ask for quotes. "Redesign our website" gets you incomparable quotes. "Front-end redesign, same platform, 10 pages, keep all URLs" gets you apples-to-apples pricing. Use the tiers above to name your project correctly.
  2. Phase it. Redesign the high-impact pages first (homepage, top service pages, contact), then roll the new design system across the rest. You spread the cost, reduce launch risk, and learn what works before committing to the whole site.
  3. Bring your content ready. Final text and photos before design starts. Content delays are the number one cause of redesign overruns, and most providers charge for the extra rounds.
  4. Get three quotes against the same brief. Do not just compare the totals. Compare what is included, what is excluded, who owns the domain and accounts, what happens to SEO, and what support you get after launch. A quote that is 30% cheaper but excludes redirect mapping and training is not cheaper.
  5. Own everything. Your domain, your hosting account, your analytics, your CMS admin. If a provider wants to hold the keys, that is a red flag worth more than any discount they are offering.

FAQ

How often should a website be redesigned?

Every 2 to 4 years is the standard guidance from most design and pricing sources, or sooner if your conversion metrics, speed, or mobile experience have clearly declined. The calendar is not the real trigger, though. Redesign when the site no longer matches your business: new services, new positioning, new brand, or a mobile experience that embarrasses you when you show it to a customer. A site that still converts and loads fast does not need a redesign just because it is three years old.

Will a redesign hurt my Google rankings?

It can, but it should not. Rankings drop when redesigns change URLs without redirects, delete content that was earning traffic, or slow the site down. A competent redesign includes a full redirect map from old URLs to new ones, keeps the content that ranks, carries analytics and schema over, and monitors rankings for a few weeks after launch. This is one of the most important line items to confirm in writing before you approve a quote. Our guide on getting your business on Google explains the foundations a redesign needs to protect.

Why do two agencies quote such different prices for the same redesign?

Because they are almost never quoting the same job. One quote might be a visual refresh on your existing platform, another a full strategic rebuild with new information architecture, CRM integration, and copywriting. The prices look incomparable because the scopes are. Ask each provider to spell out exactly what is included: number of page templates, whether content is migrated or rewritten, SEO preservation steps, integrations, revision rounds, and post-launch support. Once you normalise the scopes, the prices usually start making sense.

Can I redesign my own website with a page builder instead?

For a simple site with a handful of pages, yes, and it can cost you nothing but your weekends. Page builders are genuinely good at producing decent-looking pages. They fall short when the site needs real strategy (conversion-focused layouts, SEO preservation, speed budgets, accessibility compliance) or when your time is worth more than the money you save.
