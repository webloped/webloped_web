How to get more Google reviews for your business

Most happy customers will leave a review if you ask - most never think to do it on their own. Here is a simple, honest system Canadian small businesses can use to get more Google reviews, without breaking Google's rules or the law.

The short answer: ask every customer, at the right moment, with a direct link. Create your review link in your Google Business Profile, send it by text or email within hours of a good experience, put a QR code where customers pay, and make asking a routine for your whole team. Reply to every review you get, and never pay for reviews, offer rewards for them, or only ask the customers you expect to be happy.

That is the entire system in one paragraph. The rest of this guide explains why it works, the rules that keep you safe in Canada, the exact setup steps, and copy-paste scripts for the ask itself. You can have all of this running by next week.

Why Google reviews matter so much

Google reviews do two jobs at once. First, they help customers choose you: most people check reviews before calling a plumber, booking a clinic, or trying a new restaurant. A business with 120 recent reviews and thoughtful replies feels alive; a business with three reviews from two years ago feels like a gamble.

Second, reviews help you rank. Google has said that local search results are shaped by three things: relevance, distance, and prominence. Prominence is essentially how well-known and trusted your business looks, and a steady flow of recent, genuine reviews is one of the clearest signals of it. That is why two similar businesses in the same neighbourhood can show up very differently in the map results - one keeps earning reviews every week, the other does not.

Reviews also answer questions your website cannot: what it is actually like to work with you, how you handle problems, and whether you are worth the price. Our guide to the pages a small business website should have suggests giving testimonials their own space on your site, and your Google reviews are the most believable testimonials you will ever have.

The rules: what Google and Canadian law actually allow

Before you ask anyone for anything, know the boundaries. This is not legal advice, but these are the rules worth knowing.

Google's policies. Asking customers to share a genuine experience is completely fine. What Google prohibits is:

  • Offering incentives for reviews: discounts, freebies, prize draws, gift cards. Google treats this as fake engagement.
  • Review gating: only asking the customers you think will be happy, or quietly discouraging unhappy customers from reviewing you.
  • Pressuring people to review you while they are standing at your counter.
  • Reviewing your own business, or having staff, friends, or family do it for you.

Businesses that break these rules can have their reviews removed and their profile suspended. The short version: real customers, real experiences, no bribes, and ask everyone.

Canadian law. Fake or misleading reviews fall under the deceptive marketing practices in the Competition Act, and the Competition Bureau has taken enforcement action against businesses that post or pay for fake reviews. If a review ever turns out to be incentivized, it must be clearly disclosed as such - though Google's own ban on incentives makes that a moot point for your Google profile.

Canada's anti-spam law (CASL). If you follow up by email or text, you are sending a commercial electronic message, and CASL requires you to have the recipient's consent first. Implied consent usually exists after a purchase or an existing business relationship, which covers most review requests sent shortly after a transaction. In-person asks, signage, and QR codes at the counter are not electronic messages and do not raise this issue. When in doubt, keep the request to customers who have actually done business with you, and include a simple way to opt out of further messages.

Step 1: get your profile ready

There is no point asking for reviews until your Google Business Profile is claimed, verified, and complete. A half-finished profile with wrong hours and no photos wastes every review you earn. Make sure the business name, address, hours, phone number, and category are correct, add real photos of the place and the work, and fill in the services or products section. If you have not claimed your listing yet, start with our guide on how to get your business on Google - it walks through the whole setup.

Step 2: make the ask frictionless

Most businesses lose reviews at the same point: the ask. "Could you leave us a Google review?" is work for the customer - they have to remember later, search for you, and find the review button. A direct link turns it into one tap.

Create your review link. Sign in to your Google Business Profile on a computer, open your profile, and look for the option to get more reviews (usually under the reviews section). Copy the link, and download the QR code Google generates for you.

Put the link everywhere it is useful:

  • Your email signature, especially for quotes, invoices, and booking confirmations
  • A printed QR code at the counter or on the receipt folder
  • The thank-you page after an online booking or purchase
  • Your follow-up text or email templates (more on those below)
  • A small card that goes out with deliveries or completed work: "How did we do? Scan to review us on Google"

If the link Google gives you is long and ugly, shorten it with a branded short link on your own domain. A link like yoursite.ca/review is easier to say out loud and looks trustworthy on printed materials. This is also the only website change the system needs - if your site is due for a refresh anyway, check the signs your business needs a new website first.

Step 3: ask at the right moment

Timing is the difference between a review and a forgotten intention. Ask within minutes or hours of the good experience - the finished haircut, the fixed leak, the delivered cake, the signed closing. A request that arrives a week later converts far worse, and the same-day text with a link beats everything else.

Train your whole team on one simple line. When a customer says something nice, the reply is: "Thanks so much - that means a lot. If you have a minute, a Google review helps other people find us. Can I text you the link?" Then send it right away. Asking everyone matters: cherry-picking only the happy customers is review gating, and it skews your ratings in a way customers can smell.

Use these templates as a starting point, and rewrite them in your own voice:

In person, at checkout

"Thanks for coming in. If you have a minute later, a quick Google review would really help us out - there is a QR code right on the counter."

Same-day follow-up text

"Hi [name], this is [your name] from [business]. Thanks for choosing us today. If you have a minute, a Google review would mean a lot: [link]. Either way, thanks again."

Follow-up email after the job

"Hi [name], just checking in now that the [service] is done - hope everything is working well. If you were happy with the experience, a Google review takes about a minute and helps a small business more than you would think: [link]. And if anything is not right, please reply to this email so we can make it right. Thanks, [your name]."

Notice that the email also gives unhappy customers a private channel. That is deliberate: you want problems to come to you first, not to the review box.

Do not send more than one follow-up per customer. One polite ask, then let it go - a second reminder starts to feel like pressure, and pressured reviews read badly anyway.

Step 4: reply to every review

Replying does three things: it shows future customers you are engaged, it shows the reviewer their words were read (which encourages others to write), and it signals to Google that your profile is actively managed. Aim to reply within a day.

For positive reviews, be specific. "Thanks for the five stars" is fine, but referencing the detail they mentioned is better: "Glad the rush order arrived in time for the wedding - congratulations again." Sign replies with your name or role so they feel human.

For negative reviews, stay calm and professional, no matter how unfair the review feels. Thank them for the feedback, acknowledge the specific issue without admitting things you dispute, and move the conversation offline: "I am sorry the wait was longer than expected on Saturday - that is not the experience we aim for. Please call me at [number] so I can make this right. - [Your name]." Never argue in public, never name-drop policy excuses, and never, ever retaliate. A gracious reply to a bad review converts more future customers than ten five-star reviews.

You cannot remove a bad review just because you disagree with it - Google only removes reviews that violate its policies (spam, fake content, offensive material). If a review is genuinely fake or contains prohibited content, you can report it through your profile. Otherwise, your reply is your response.

Step 5: make it a routine, not a campaign

Bursts do not work. A business that collects 20 reviews in one month and then goes silent for a year looks stale - customers notice old dates, and recency matters for both trust and ranking. The businesses that win treat reviews as a weekly habit:

  • Set a small, realistic target: five new reviews a week is plenty for most small businesses.
  • Decide who asks. Put the ask into existing workflows: the front desk mentions it at payment, the technician sends the text after the job, the booking software fires the email automatically.
  • Track it simply. A shared note of who was asked this week is enough - the goal is consistency, not surveillance.
  • Review your own reviews monthly. Patterns in complaints are free business intelligence; patterns in praise are free marketing copy.

If you use booking, invoicing, or point-of-sale software, check whether it has review-request automation built in. Many do, and an automatic same-day message is the closest thing to a hands-free review system. Just make sure the automation respects the consent rules above.

What not to do

A quick list of the shortcuts that backfire:

  • Buying reviews. They get removed, and the ones that survive read as fake. It also puts you on the wrong side of the Competition Act.
  • Discounts or gifts for reviews. Violates Google's policies even if you ask for honest ones - any incentive taints the review.
  • Review gating. Surveying customers and only sending happy ones to Google is against the rules, and customers are increasingly aware of it.
  • Staff writing reviews. Google detects patterns, and colleagues reviewing the business is against policy.
  • Reviewing competitors badly. Against policy, easily reported, and a terrible look if it comes out.

Frequently asked questions

Is it against the rules to ask customers for Google reviews?

No. Google's policies explicitly allow asking customers to share a genuine experience - most businesses simply never ask, which is why so few reviews exist. What crosses the line is incentives (discounts, gifts, prize draws), asking only happy customers, pressuring people, or faking reviews. A simple, neutral ask with a direct link is exactly what Google wants to see.

Can I offer a discount or a free coffee for a review?

No - not on Google. Incentivized reviews violate Google's policies and can lead to review removal or profile suspension. In Canada, fake or misleading reviews are also covered by the deceptive marketing provisions of the Competition Act. Earn reviews by doing good work and asking, not by paying for them.

How long does it take for reviews to help my Google ranking?

There is no fixed timeline. Reviews are one signal among many - relevance, distance, and overall prominence all shape local results. Most businesses see a gradual improvement as review count, recency, and rating climb over weeks and months. Treat it as compounding: a steady five reviews a week beats a one-time burst every time.

Can I get a bad review removed?

Only if it violates Google's policies - spam, fake content, conflicts of interest, or offensive material. A negative review from a real customer about a real experience stays up, even if you disagree with it. Report genuinely fake reviews through your Business Profile, and answer honest bad ones with a professional reply that shows future customers how you handle problems.
