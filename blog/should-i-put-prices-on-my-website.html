Should I put prices on my website? A Canadian small business guide

Price is usually the first thing a visitor looks for and the last thing many small business websites publish. This guide gives a straight answer to should I put prices on my website, with four honest formats that work in Canada, wording you can copy, and the mistakes that turn a helpful price into an argument.

The short answer

Should I put prices on my website? For most Canadian small businesses, yes. You do not always need one exact price. You need enough pricing guidance for a visitor to decide, in under a minute, whether you are likely to fit their budget. That can be an exact price for a standard service, a genuine starting price, a typical range, a package price, or a minimum fee plus the factors that affect a custom quote.

A page that only says contact us for pricing asks every visitor to phone or email a stranger before they know whether you cost hundreds or thousands of dollars. Some will. Many will open the next result in Google instead, especially if that competitor published a number. Publishing a price with clear context does not force you to quote blind. It filters out enquiries that were never going to work, reassures the people who can afford you, and starts the conversation from your number instead of a guess.

The rest of this guide shows how to choose the right format for your type of work, how to write the words around the number so it does not create disputes, and where to put prices so customers, Google, and AI search tools can actually find them.

Why visitors look for price first

When someone searches for a plumber in Hamilton, a hair salon in Calgary, a caterer in Halifax, or a web designer in Ontario, they are usually comparing two or three businesses at once. They want to know what you do, whether you serve their area, whether other customers trust you, and roughly what it costs. Price is not the only factor, but it is the fastest way to narrow the list.

When there is no price guidance at all, visitors fill the gap with an assumption, and the assumption is rarely in your favour. Some assume you are expensive and leave. Some cannot be bothered to call three businesses just to learn a starting point. Some call anyway, discover you are far outside their budget, and both of you lose an hour. None of that shows up in your analytics as a lost sale. It just looks like traffic that did not convert.

There is also a trust cost. Most people have dealt with a business that hid the price because the price was hard to defend. Fair or not, no price at all can read as evasive, even when your reason is simply that every job is different. A short, honest explanation of how your pricing works removes that suspicion without committing you to a number you cannot honour.

The case for hiding prices, and where it breaks down

The usual argument for hiding prices goes like this: if I get them on the phone, I can explain the value, handle objections, and close the sale. A number on a page has no salesperson attached, so a high number might scare people off before they understand what is included.

There is some truth in that. A bare number with no context can lose people who would have paid it once they understood the work. But hiding the number entirely loses a different, larger group: the quiet researchers. These are the people who check your site at night, decide you are in range, and book or enquire without ever needing a sales conversation. They do their own qualifying for free. Force them to call just to learn a ballpark figure and many will choose the business that answered the question on the page.

Hiding prices also costs you time. Every call that starts with how much do you charge for a standard job is a call your website could have answered at 2 a.m. If your team spends hours each week giving the same starting prices by phone and email, that is a sign the website should carry that information. None of this means you should publish a single fixed price for genuinely custom work. It means publish guidance. The four formats below let you be honest about price without pretending every job is identical.

Four ways to show prices without quoting blind

1. An exact price

Use an exact price when the service is standard, the scope is definable, and the work takes roughly the same effort each time. Examples include a 60-minute massage, a basic oil change package, a standard haircut, a one-hour consultation, or a product with a set price. Exact prices convert well because they remove uncertainty. The visitor can decide on the spot.

An exact price forces you to define the scope, which is healthy. Write what the price includes in the same breath as the number. For example, illustrative wording only: 60-minute therapeutic massage, $110 plus tax. Includes assessment and treatment plan. Direct billing available where your plan allows it. The second and third sentences do as much work as the number, because they tell the visitor what they are actually buying.

2. A starting price

Use a starting price when the work has a clear floor but varies above it. Illustrative wording only: Bathroom renovations from $12,000 plus tax. Final price depends on size, fixtures, and whether plumbing moves. We confirm your exact price in a written quote before work begins.

A starting price does three jobs at once. It tells someone with a much smaller budget to stop reading, which saves you both time. It tells someone who can afford you that you are a real business with real rates. And it frames the conversation before you ever speak, so the discussion starts from your floor instead of whatever the visitor imagined.

The rule that matters most: the starting price must be genuine. It should be a price a real customer could actually pay for the smallest, simplest version of the job. A from price that nobody has ever paid, used only to get the phone to ring, produces an angry conversation at quoting stage and damages trust. If your honest floor is higher, publish the honest floor.

3. A typical range

Use a range when most jobs land inside a predictable band. Illustrative wording only: Most kitchen refits are $8,000 to $14,000 plus tax. Cabinet quality and whether plumbing or electrical moves are what usually decide where you land. We confirm your price after an in-home visit, before you commit.

The second sentence is the important part. A range alone can feel like hedging. A range plus the factors that move you within it lets the reader place themselves. Keep the range tight enough to be useful. A range so wide that it covers almost any budget tells the reader nothing. If your honest range is very wide, split the service into two or three named packages, each with its own narrower range, instead of publishing one vague band.

4. Packages and worked examples

Packages and worked examples suit complex work best. Describe two or three typical job sizes, what was involved in each, and what each type of job usually costs. Label them clearly as illustrative examples of job sizes, not as quotes and not as past client results, unless they are real and you have permission to share them.

Illustrative format only, not a real client or a quote: Small job, single-storey home, eavestroughs cleared and downspouts flushed, photos provided, $210 plus tax. Medium job, two-storey home with a rear addition, guards removed and refit, about two hours, $385 plus tax. Large job, rural outbuilding, height access and heavy debris, half day, $640 plus tax. A reader can place their own home against those three descriptions far more easily than against a single from price.

Packages work the same way. A basic, standard, and premium package with named inclusions lets visitors self-select, and it gives you a clean way to say what is not included. If you sell online, this is also where product pricing, shipping, and returns information belongs. Our guide on starting to sell online in Canada covers the checkout side of that decision.

Which format fits your business

  • Standardized service: publish an exact price, with duration and inclusions. Salons, clinics, fitness, cleaning packages, and many productized services fit here.
  • Service with limited variations: publish packages or a starting price. Name the variation, such as hair length, vehicle size, home size, or number of pages, so the reader understands why the price moves.
  • Service with a predictable range: publish a typical range plus the two or three factors that decide where a job lands. Many trades and renovation jobs fit here.
  • Highly custom work: publish a minimum project fee or a consultation fee, plus the factors that affect the quote and a clear quote process. The visitor still learns whether you are in their league.
  • Work that needs an inspection first: publish the inspection or diagnostic fee if you charge one, what it covers, whether it is credited toward the job, and typical ranges for common repairs after inspection.

If you are not sure which group you are in, look at your last twenty invoices. If most jobs cluster in a band, you have a range. If they fall into three clear sizes, you have packages. If they are all over the map, you have custom work, and your page should explain the process and the minimum instead of guessing a number.

How to write pricing that does not create disputes

A price without scope is the beginning of a dispute. A price with scope is the beginning of a good enquiry. For every price on your site, cover these points in plain language near the number, not buried in a footer.

  • Currency: say CAD if there is any chance a visitor could assume US dollars. Canadian customers should never have to guess.
  • Tax: say whether the price is before tax or tax included, and name the tax treatment in general terms, such as plus HST where it applies, or plus GST and PST, or plus GST and QST, depending on where you operate. Keep the same wording on quotes and invoices. If you are unsure how tax applies to your service, ask your accountant rather than copying another business.
  • What is included: labour, parts, materials, travel within your service area, cleanup, warranty terms if you offer them, and anything else the customer would reasonably expect.
  • What is not included: permits, disposal fees, parking, after-hours work, materials beyond a stated allowance, or anything that commonly surprises customers.
  • What changes the price: name the main factors in one short sentence. Size, condition, access, distance, materials, and timeline are common ones.
  • Minimums and service area: if you have a minimum call-out fee, a minimum order, or a travel charge outside a defined area, say so before the visitor enquires.
  • When the final price is confirmed: one line such as, we confirm your exact price in writing before work begins, removes the fear of being ambushed partway through.
  • Date: add a simple line such as, prices current as of November 2026. It protects you when a visitor finds a cached page or an old screenshot a year later, and it reminds you to review the numbers.

Keep the language honest and specific. Canadian competition rules prohibit false or misleading pricing representations, and mandatory fees should not be hidden until the end of a checkout or quote. This guide is general information, not legal advice. If your industry is regulated, or you run promotions with conditions, check the official guidance for your sector or get professional advice before publishing.

Finally, make sure the numbers match everywhere. Your website, your Google Business Profile services, your booking page, your quotes, and your invoices should tell the same story. A visitor who sees one price on your site and a different starting price on your Google listing will trust neither. Our guide on getting your business on Google covers keeping those listings consistent.

Where to put prices on your website

Put pricing guidance where the decision happens, not only on a single pricing page that visitors have to hunt for. For most small businesses, that means four places.

  • Each service page: a price, starting price, or range near the top, with inclusions and the next step. Someone who lands on that page from Google should not have to click away to learn the ballpark cost.
  • A dedicated pricing page: the full picture in one place, with packages, minimums, tax treatment, and answers to pricing questions. Link to it from your main navigation if pricing is a common question.
  • Your booking or enquiry flow: repeat the relevant price or range before the visitor submits, so there is no surprise after they have filled in a form. Our guide on adding online booking to your website covers that setup.
  • Your FAQ: answer how much does it cost in plain language, with the same numbers and the same caveats as your service pages. FAQ answers are also what search and AI tools often quote.

Write prices as real text, not as text inside an image or only inside a PDF price list. Real text is readable on a phone, accessible to screen readers, and readable by search engines. An image of a price list fails on all three counts. What to do when every job really is different

Some work genuinely cannot be priced without seeing it. A renovation with hidden damage, a custom build, a legal matter, or a repair that needs diagnosis all fit here. The answer is not to publish nothing. The answer is to publish the process.

Explain, step by step, how a visitor gets an accurate price: what you need from them, whether you offer a free estimate or a paid consultation or inspection, how long the quote takes, how long the quote is valid, and what the quote includes. Add a minimum fee or a typical range for common versions of the job if you honestly can. Add illustrative examples of small, medium, and large jobs so the reader can self-place. Then make the next step obvious, with a phone number and a short form.

That page will not give an exact number, but it will answer the real question behind should I put prices on my website, which is: am I in the right ballpark, and what happens next if I contact you? A visitor who understands your process is far more likely to enquire than a visitor staring at contact us for pricing with no other information.

Pricing, SEO, and AI search

People search for prices directly. Queries such as how much does a website cost in Canada, how much does a haircut cost in Toronto, or plumber prices in Ottawa are how buyers start. If your site answers those questions clearly, with CAD numbers, inclusions, and local detail, you are eligible to rank for them. If your site hides every number, a competitor or a directory that publishes ranges will answer instead.

The same applies to AI search. Tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Google AI Overviews look for clear, factual, well-structured answers they can cite. A service page that states a price, what it includes, where you work, and when the price was current is far easier for those tools to use than a page with no numbers at all. Our guide on showing up in AI search covers that in detail, and our guide on how much a website costs in Canada is an example of a pricing page written for exactly these searches.

Do not chase every price keyword. Pick the services you actually want to sell, in the areas you actually serve, and answer those price questions properly. One honest, specific pricing page will outperform ten thin pages that repeat the same vague range.

Common pricing mistakes to avoid

  • A fake low anchor: a from price nobody pays. It gets calls and loses trust.
  • A range too wide to mean anything: if the band covers almost any budget, split the service into packages.
  • A number with no context: no inclusions, no tax note, no factors, no confirmation step.
  • Outdated prices: numbers from two years ago that you no longer honour. Date your pricing and review it when your costs change.
  • Prices only in an image or PDF: unreadable on phones, invisible to search, and hard to update.
  • Hiding prices behind a form: asking for contact details before giving any guidance, for a standard service, reads as a trap.
  • Copying US prices: a number copied from a US competitor, with no CAD conversion and no Canadian tax or service-area context, misleads your own customers.
  • Different numbers in different places: website, Google listing, social posts, and quotes that disagree with each other.

When not to publish a number

There are narrow cases where publishing a specific number would mislead. If a price depends on a regulated assessment, a tender process, or a diagnosis you cannot make without seeing the work, do not invent a figure to fill the space. Publish the consultation or diagnostic fee, the minimum, the process, and the factors instead. If you run a promotion, publish its conditions and end date with the price, not in fine print elsewhere. And if your costs are changing so fast that a published number would be wrong within weeks, publish a range with a current-as-of date and review it often, rather than a precise figure you cannot stand behind.

Even in those cases, publish something useful. The goal is never to trick a visitor into calling. The goal is to give them enough honest information to decide whether calling makes sense.

A simple test before you publish

  • Can a first-time visitor tell, in under a minute, whether you are likely to fit their budget?
  • Is every starting price a price a real customer could actually pay?
  • Does every number say what is included, what is not, and whether tax is extra?
  • Have you named the factors that change the price, in one short sentence?
  • Is there a line saying when the final price is confirmed, before work begins?
  • Are the numbers in CAD, dated, and the same on your site, your Google listing, and your quotes?
  • Are the prices written as real text that a phone visitor and a search engine can read?

If you can answer yes to those seven questions, your pricing page is doing its job. If not, fix the missing pieces before you worry about design. Clear pricing is copy and structure first, layout second.

Frequently asked questions

Should I put prices on my website?

For most Canadian small businesses, yes. You do not always need an exact price. Publish an exact price for standard services, a genuine starting price or a typical range for work that varies, or a minimum project fee plus the factors that affect the quote for custom work. Say what is included, whether tax is extra, and when the final price is confirmed. A page that only says contact us for pricing gives visitors no way to judge whether you fit their budget.

Will publishing prices scare customers away or help competitors?

A clear price with context usually filters out people who were never going to buy at your rate, which saves you time, and it reassures people who can afford you. Competitors can already ask for a quote or see your rates in other places, so hiding prices rarely protects you for long. What loses customers is a number with no explanation, or a low starting price that nobody actually pays. Pair every number with what is included and what changes it.

Should website prices in Canada include tax?

Say so clearly either way. Many Canadian service businesses show prices before GST, HST, PST, or QST and write plus tax where it applies. Retail and consumer-facing prices are often shown with tax treatment stated at checkout or in the price note. What matters is that a visitor is never surprised. State whether the number is before tax or tax included, name any mandatory fees, and keep the wording the same on your website, your quotes, and your invoices. If you are unsure how tax applies to your service, check with your accountant.

What if every job is different and I cannot give an exact price?

Publish guidance instead of a single number. Give a genuine starting price or a typical range, list the two or three factors that move the price most, show illustrative examples of different job sizes, and explain your quote process step by step, including whether there is a consultation or inspection fee. That gives visitors enough to decide whether to contact you, without pretending a custom job has a fixed price.
