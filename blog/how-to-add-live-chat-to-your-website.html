How to add live chat to your website

Most visitors who leave a small business website do not leave because they disliked it. They leave because they had one small question and nobody was there to answer it. Live chat puts a person in front of them at exactly that moment, and installing it takes about ten minutes.

Think about the last time you shopped for a service online. You found a plumber, a dentist, a landscaper, or a bookkeeper whose site looked fine, but you had one question before you were willing to call: do you serve my neighbourhood, do you take new patients this month, what does a job like mine usually cost. The contact form felt like sending a letter into a void. The phone felt like a commitment. So you went back to Google and clicked the next result. That is the gap live chat fills. It is the lowest-effort way for a visitor to ask a question, and the lowest-effort way for a business to answer one.

Adding live chat to your website is one of the simplest upgrades a Canadian small business can make. There is no rebuild, no developer required, and several good tools cost nothing to start. The install itself is genuinely a ten-minute job: sign up, copy a short snippet of code or install a plugin, and a chat bubble appears on your site. The part that decides whether chat actually brings you enquiries is everything after the install: who answers, how fast, what the greeting says, and what happens when nobody is online. This guide covers both halves, in order, with Canadian examples throughout.

What live chat is, and what it is not

Live chat is a small bubble, usually in the bottom corner of a website, that opens a conversation with a real person in real time. The visitor types a question, someone on your team replies within a minute or two, and the conversation ends with a booking, a quote, or a phone call. It is not email, which arrives hours later, and it is not a phone call, which many visitors avoid until they are already fairly sure they want to buy.

Live chat is also not the same thing as a chatbot, although the two often share a widget. A chatbot answers automatically, following rules or AI, and it is useful for questions that have the same answer every time: your hours, your address, your starting price. Live chat is a person. The distinction matters because visitors can tell the difference within one exchange, and they react differently. A person who is ready to book wants a person on the other end. A person with a simple factual question is often happy with a bot. Our guide on whether to add a chatbot to your website covers the automated side in detail. Many small businesses run both: live chat during business hours, and a bot or an away message after hours.

The simplest way to think about it is this. Live chat is the digital version of a shopkeeper who notices a customer hesitating near the counter and asks, "Can I help you find something?" It is not a sales pitch. It is a person being available at the moment of doubt, which is exactly when most small business websites lose people.

Is live chat worth it for a small business?

For most service businesses, yes, and the reason is not complicated. A meaningful share of your visitors are ready to buy but not ready to call. They have one question standing between them and an enquiry, and if answering it takes more effort than asking it, they leave. Chat lowers the effort on both sides. The visitor types a sentence instead of composing an email or making a call. You answer in a sentence instead of writing a formal reply or playing phone tag.

Chat also works outside your office hours, in a limited but useful way. Nobody is answering at 10 pm, but a visitor can still leave a message through the same bubble, and that message arrives in your inbox or your phone like an email with context: you can see which page they were on, what they asked, and when. That is a warmer lead than a bare contact form submission, because you know what they were looking at when they decided to write.

There are businesses where chat is a poor fit. If your work comes almost entirely from referrals and repeat customers, or if every job needs a site visit and a long conversation before you can say anything useful, chat will be quiet and that is fine. If you cannot answer within a reasonable time, chat can also hurt: a bubble that promises a reply and then leaves someone waiting for an hour is worse than no bubble at all. The test is simple. If visitors regularly ask you the same few questions by phone or email, and those questions have short answers, chat will earn its place. If you are a one-person business who is on a job site all day and cannot check a phone, start with a contact form and a clear promise of when you reply, and add chat when you have someone to watch it.

Cost is rarely the obstacle. Several good tools have genuinely free plans, and paid plans are modest. The real cost is attention: someone has to watch the inbox during the hours you advertise. For a one-person business that is usually the owner, with the mobile app on their phone. For a team of three or four, it is whoever is at the desk. Budget thirty minutes a day of answering time in the first month, then adjust once you see the real volume.

Step 1: Pick a chat tool

Every live chat tool works the same way at the core: you get an account, the tool gives you a widget, and messages arrive in an app on your phone or computer. The differences are in the free plan limits, the mobile app, and the extras such as canned replies, chat history, and integrations. For a small business, the shortlist is short, and any of these will do the job.

tawk.to is the most popular free option, and its free plan is genuinely free with no agent limit and no conversation limit. The trade-off is that the free version shows tawk.to branding on the widget, and removing it costs a small monthly fee. If your budget is zero and you want chat today, this is the default choice.

Tidio has a free plan that covers a small number of conversations a month, a good mobile app, and a clean setup flow. It is a strong pick if you like the idea of adding simple automated replies later, because its bot features are built into the same product. Check the current conversation limit on its pricing page before you commit, because free-tier limits change.

Crisp offers a free plan for two agents, which suits a business with an owner and one staff member answering together. Its interface is tidy and it handles shared inboxes well, so two people can see the same conversation without replying twice.

LiveChat is the paid option most small teams graduate to. Paid plans typically start around USD 19 to 25 per user per month, roughly CAD 26 to 35, and add features such as canned responses, detailed reporting, and more integrations. There is usually a free trial of about two weeks. Start free, and only move to a paid plan when you can point to a specific feature you need, such as a second agent, chat reports, or an integration with the tool you already use for bookings or email.

When choosing, check three things beyond price. First, does it have a mobile app for iPhone and Android that sends push notifications, so you actually see messages when you are away from your desk. Second, does it work with your website platform, either through an official plugin or a snippet you can paste. Third, does it let you set business hours and an away message, which is the feature that stops a 2 am message from sitting unanswered and looking ignored. If a tool fails any of those three, pick another one. Do not choose on the look of the widget alone. Your visitors will not remember the colour of the bubble. They will remember whether someone answered.

Step 2: Install the widget in about 10 minutes

The install is the easy part, and it is the same shape on every platform. You either install the tool's official plugin or app, or you paste a short snippet of code the tool gives you into your site. You do not need to write any code yourself. The snippet is generated for your account, so you only copy and paste it.

WordPress. Go to Plugins, then Add New, and search for your chat tool by name. Install the official plugin, activate it, and connect it to your account when it asks. tawk.to, Tidio, Crisp, and LiveChat all have official WordPress plugins. No code is needed, and the widget appears on every page.

Shopify. Install the tool's app from the Shopify App Store and approve the connection. The app adds the widget to your store theme for you. Tidio and Crisp both list Shopify apps, and the install takes a few minutes.

Wix and Squarespace. Neither needs a plugin. Copy the widget script from your chat dashboard and paste it into your site's custom code area. In Squarespace that is Settings, then Advanced, then Code Injection, in the footer box. In Wix it is the custom code section under Settings. Publish the site and the bubble appears.

A site you edit yourself, or a custom build. Paste the snippet just before the closing </body> tag in the template or layout file that every page shares, usually the footer. Placing it last means your own content loads first and the widget arrives after. If you use Google Tag Manager, you can add the snippet once as a custom HTML tag set to fire on all pages, and it deploys sitewide without touching your templates.

After installing, open your own site in a private browser window and send yourself a test message. Reply to it from the chat app on your phone, so you have seen a full round trip before a customer does. If the bubble does not appear, the usual causes are a caching plugin showing an old version of the page, or the snippet pasted into a template that only some pages use. Clear the cache, check a second page, and confirm the snippet is in the shared footer rather than on one page only.

Step 3: Set it up properly before anyone writes in

A widget with default settings is a missed opportunity. Spend twenty minutes on the settings below, because they decide what the visitor experiences in the first five seconds.

Set your business hours. Tell the tool when you are actually available, in your own timezone, and set an away message for the rest of the time. An away message should say when you will reply and offer an alternative, for example: "Thanks for writing. We are away from the desk until 9 am tomorrow and we reply to every message within one business day. If it is urgent, call us at the number on our contact page." A visitor who sees honest hours trusts you more than a visitor who writes at 9 pm and hears nothing until noon the next day with no explanation.

Write a greeting that sounds like you. The default greeting on most tools is a generic "Hi, how can I help?" Replace it with something specific to your business. A Toronto dental clinic might use "Hi, looking to book a cleaning or a checkup? Ask us anything and we will reply within a few minutes during office hours." A landscaping company in Cambridge might use "Hi, tell us about your yard and we will let you know if it is a job we can quote this week." A specific greeting does two things: it tells the visitor what kind of question to ask, and it signals that a person who knows the business is on the other end.

Write canned replies for your top five questions. After a week of answering chats, you will notice the same questions repeating: your price, your service area, your availability, whether you take new clients, how long a job takes. Write a short, friendly answer for each one and save it in the tool as a canned reply. Then answering takes one tap instead of a minute of typing, and the answer is consistent no matter who is on shift. Keep them short and human. A canned reply should read like a person wrote it, not like a policy document.

Decide who answers, and how fast. One named person should own chat during business hours, even in a small team, so messages are not everyone's job and therefore nobody's job. A reasonable target for a small business is a first reply within one to two minutes while you are online. You do not need to solve the problem in that first reply. A fast "Hi, thanks for writing, let me check that for you" keeps the visitor there while you find the answer. If you cannot reply that fast most of the day, shorten the hours you advertise as online, and let the away message set the expectation instead.

Ask for as little as possible up front. Some tools let you show a form before the chat starts, asking for a name, an email, and a phone number. Resist the urge to ask for all three. Every field is a reason to close the bubble. Ask for a name only, or nothing at all, and collect contact details naturally in the conversation when the visitor is ready to book. You can always ask for an email at the end, when they have already decided they want a reply.

Step 4: Decide where chat appears, and where it does not

Most small businesses put chat on every page, and for most that is fine. But chat earns its place on some pages more than others, and it can get in the way on a few.

Put chat on your homepage, your pricing or services pages, and your contact page. Those are the pages where a visitor is deciding whether to get in touch, and a quick answer at that moment is worth the most. If you run an online store, put it on product and checkout pages too, where a question about shipping, sizing, or availability is the difference between a sale and an abandoned cart.

Consider leaving it off your blog posts. A person reading an article is learning, not buying, and a chat bubble popping up over the text is a distraction that adds little. Some tools let you hide the widget on specific pages, or show it only after the visitor has been on the page for thirty seconds, which is a good compromise: the keen reader is not interrupted, and the visitor who lingers, which often means they are deciding, gets the invitation.

On mobile, check that the bubble does not cover anything important. On a phone it sits in the bottom corner, which is also where your call button, your booking button, or your cookie notice may live. Open your site on a real phone after installing chat and tap through it. If the bubble covers your phone number or your main button, move the button or change the bubble's position in the tool's settings. This is a two-minute check that many businesses skip, and it is the difference between a helpful widget and an annoying one.

Answering well: the part that actually wins customers

The install is ten minutes. The skill is in the answering, and it is mostly common sense applied consistently.

Answer like a person, not a script. Use the visitor's name if they gave it. Answer the question they asked before offering anything else. If they ask whether you serve Mississauga, say yes or no first, and then add the useful detail, such as which days you are there. Short replies beat long ones in chat. A visitor who wanted a paragraph would have sent an email.

Move the conversation toward a next step. Chat is not the place to do the whole job. Its purpose is to remove the one obstacle and then hand the visitor to the next step: a booking, a quote form, or a phone call. When you have answered their question, say what happens next and make it easy. "I can book you in for Thursday at 10 am, shall I hold that?" is better than "Let us know if you would like to book." Offer a specific time, a specific price range, or a specific form, and the visitor has something to say yes to.

Know when to move off chat. Some questions need a phone call: a complicated job, a price that depends on seeing the site, or a customer who is upset. Do not try to resolve those in a chat window. Say so kindly and offer the call: "This one is easier to sort out on the phone, can I call you at a time that suits you?" Customers respect that, because it shows you are trying to help rather than trying to keep them in a channel that suits you.

Save the conversations. Every chat tool keeps a history, and that history is market research you did not have to pay for. Once a month, read through the last few weeks of chats and write down the questions that came up more than twice. Those questions belong on your website: on the relevant service page, in your FAQ, or in a new blog post. If ten people asked whether you serve their town, your service area page is not clear enough. If five people asked about your price, your pricing page needs a range. Chat tells you what your website is failing to say, in your customers' own words.

Privacy in Canada: what to get right before you go live

Live chat collects personal information, and Canadian privacy law treats it seriously. A chat transcript can contain a name, an email address, a phone number, an address, and details about a person's health, home, or finances, depending on your business. That is personal information under PIPEDA, the federal privacy law that applies to most Canadian businesses, and under provincial laws in Quebec, British Columbia, and Alberta where they apply.

Three things cover most of what a small business needs to do. First, say so in your privacy policy: that you use live chat, what information it collects, why you collect it, and how long you keep it. If you do not have a privacy policy yet, adding chat is a good reason to write one. Second, use a chat tool that stores data in a way you are comfortable with, and check where it stores transcripts. Many popular tools store data on servers outside Canada, usually in the United States. That is not forbidden, but your privacy policy should say so, and you should know it before a customer asks. Third, keep transcripts only as long as you need them, and delete old ones on a schedule. A chat from two years ago about a quote that never turned into a job does not need to sit in your account forever.

One more caution: do not ask for sensitive information in chat. Never ask a visitor to send a credit card number, a password, a health card number, or a government ID through a chat window. If a customer volunteers that kind of detail, reply asking them to use a safer channel, and delete the message from the transcript if your tool allows it. This is not just good practice. It protects you if your chat account is ever compromised.

Live chat and site speed

A chat widget loads extra JavaScript on every page it appears on, and that code has to be downloaded and run before the page feels fully ready. On a well-built tool the effect is small, often a fraction of a second, but it is real, and on a site that is already slow it can be the extra weight that pushes a page over the line. Our guide to making your website load faster explains how to measure this properly, but the short version is to test before and after.

Run PageSpeed Insights on your homepage before you install chat and write the numbers down. Install chat, then run the test again. If the scores drop noticeably, you have options that do not involve removing chat. The simplest is to show chat only on the pages where it earns enquiries, such as pricing, services, and contact, and hide it on the blog and on long informational pages. Another is to delay the widget so it loads after the main content, which most tools support in their settings. A third is to pick a lighter tool: some chat widgets are much heavier than others, and if speed matters to your business, that is a fair reason to switch. Do not guess at the impact. Measure it, and keep the version that serves customers without making the site feel slow.

What to do in the first 30 days

The first month is a trial, and you should treat it like one. At the end of each week, look at three numbers: how many chats started, how many of those turned into a booking, a quote request, or a phone call, and how long visitors waited for a first reply. Your chat tool reports all three. If chats are starting but not converting, read the transcripts. The usual cause is a slow first reply, a greeting that asks the wrong question, or an answer that stops short of offering a next step. If almost no chats start, the bubble may be hidden behind another element on mobile, or the greeting may be too generic to invite a question. Fix one thing at a time and watch the numbers for a week before changing something else.

After a month you will know whether chat is earning its place. A healthy sign is a steady trickle of conversations that turn into real enquiries, even if the total is small. Five chats a week that produce two bookings is a good result for a small business. A hundred chats a week that produce nothing is a sign the widget is attracting questions it cannot answer, which usually means the website itself needs clearer pricing or service information. Either way, the data tells you what to fix next, and that is the real value of the first month.

If chat is working, the next step is to make it part of your routine rather than a novelty. Put it in the daily checklist alongside checking email. Add the top questions it surfaces to your website. And when you are ready, look at whether a simple automated reply for after hours would help, which is where a chatbot earns its place. Frequently asked questions

How do I add live chat to my website for free?

Sign up with a chat tool that has a free plan, such as tawk.to, Tidio, or Crisp. Then install its plugin or app if your site runs on WordPress or Shopify, or paste the one-line widget script into your site's footer code area on Wix or Squarespace. The chat bubble appears on your site within minutes, and you answer messages from the tool's mobile app.

How much does live chat cost for a small business?

Several good tools have genuinely free plans for a one-person business, including tawk.to, Tidio, and Crisp. Paid plans for tools like LiveChat typically start around USD 19 to 25 per user per month, roughly CAD 26 to 35, and add features such as more agents, canned replies, and reporting. Check the vendor's pricing page before you commit, because free-plan limits change.

Is live chat better than a chatbot for a small business?

They solve different problems. Live chat is a real person answering in real time, which is what visitors want when they are ready to book or buy and have one specific question. A chatbot answers common questions automatically when nobody is online. Many small businesses start with live chat during business hours and add a simple bot or away message for after hours.

Does live chat slow down my website?

It can, because the widget loads extra JavaScript on every page. The effect is usually small with a well-built tool, and you can limit it by showing chat only on the pages where it earns enquiries, such as pricing and contact, and by loading the widget after the page content. Test your site with PageSpeed Insights before and after installing chat to see the real difference.
