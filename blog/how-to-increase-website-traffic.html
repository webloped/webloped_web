How to increase website traffic

More visitors only help if they are the right visitors. This guide shows Canadian small businesses how to diagnose why traffic is low, then grow it channel by channel, starting with the fixes that cost nothing but time.

Learning how to increase website traffic usually starts with a frustrating feeling: you built a website, you know it looks good, and almost nobody is finding it. The instinct is to do more of everything at once. Post more on social media, start running ads, write blog posts, redesign the homepage. That scattergun approach burns time and money, and it makes it impossible to tell what actually worked.

A better approach is slower at the start and faster afterwards. First, find out what kind of traffic problem you actually have. Then pick one or two channels that match your customers and your budget, do them properly for a few months, and measure enquiries, not just visits. A plumber in Guelph does not need ten thousand visitors a month. A plumber in Guelph needs a steady flow of people in Guelph who have a leak, a renovation, or a question that leads to a quote. This guide walks through that process in order, with Canadian examples throughout.

Start by diagnosing the problem, not by buying traffic

Low traffic has different causes, and each cause has a different fix. Before you spend a dollar, answer three questions with real data, not guesses.

1. Can Google find and store your pages? Open Google Search Console, the free tool Google provides for website owners, and check whether your important pages are indexed, meaning Google has stored them and can show them in results. If your service pages, location pages, or product pages are not indexed, no amount of content or advertising advice will fix the underlying problem until that is sorted. Common causes include a page accidentally set to noindex, a new site that has never had a sitemap submitted, or a site that was rebuilt and left old pages returning errors. Our guide to why a website does not show up on Google walks through the causes and fixes in order.

2. Are people seeing your pages but not clicking? In Search Console, look at impressions and clicks for your main pages. Impressions mean your page appeared in search results. Clicks mean someone chose it. If impressions are healthy but clicks are low, your title and description may be vague, generic, or a poor match for what the searcher wanted. A title like "Services" tells a searcher nothing. A title like "Roof Repair and Replacement in Barrie, Ontario" tells them exactly what you do and where. That is a fix you can make in an afternoon, and it can lift traffic without a single new page.

3. Are visitors arriving but leaving without contacting you? Check Google Analytics 4 for your busiest pages, how long people stay, and where they go next. If people land and leave within seconds, the page may be slow, hard to read on a phone, or unclear about what to do next. That is not a traffic problem. It is a conversion problem, and sending more visitors to a page that does not convert only wastes the traffic you buy or earn. Fix the page first, then grow the audience for it. If you have not set up analytics yet, our guide to setting up Google Analytics covers the setup and the first reports worth checking.

Write down your starting numbers before you change anything: monthly sessions, how many came from search, social, email, and direct visits, and how many enquiries the website produced. Without a baseline, every change feels like progress and none of it can be proven. A simple note in a spreadsheet, checked once a month, is enough for most small businesses. Do not judge a channel after a week. Search and content need months. Paid ads and email can be judged in weeks, but only if you track enquiries, not just clicks.

Search traffic: answer the questions your customers already ask

Search is usually the most valuable traffic for a Canadian small business, because the visitor arrived with intent. Someone searching for "emergency plumber near me" or "how much does a deck cost in Ontario" is far closer to buying than someone scrolling past a post. The work is to be the clearest, most specific answer to those searches.

Start with the questions you hear every week. What do customers ask on the phone before they book? What do they worry about before they buy? What do they get wrong about your service? Each honest answer is a page, a section, or a post. A landscaping company might answer how much sod costs per square foot in Ontario, when to aerate a lawn in the Toronto climate, and whether a permit is needed for a retaining wall. A bookkeeping firm might answer what records a sole proprietor in Canada needs to keep, when HST registration becomes required, and how to separate business and personal expenses. These are the searches with clear intent, and they are far easier for a small business to rank for than a broad term like "landscaping" or "accounting."

Give each core service its own page. A single "Services" page listing everything you do cannot rank well for each service, cannot speak to each customer's problem, and gives a visitor no reason to stay. A separate page for each service, with the service area named in plain language, a description of the process, the questions customers ask, and a clear next step, does all three jobs. If you serve several towns, do not create near-identical pages with only the town name swapped. Write each location page from real knowledge of that area: the neighbourhoods you serve, the travel time, the kind of work you do there, and a local example. Thin, duplicated location pages help nobody and can hurt trust with readers who notice.

Write for the searcher, then check the basics. Put the main topic in the page title, the first heading, and the first paragraph, in natural language. Use headings that match follow-up questions. Say prices, service areas, hours, and policies in plain text on the page, not only inside an image or a PDF, so search engines and AI answer tools can read them. Link related pages to each other: a service page should link to the relevant guide, the contact page, and the next service a customer usually asks about. Internal links help visitors find what they need and help search engines understand how your site fits together. Our guide to writing website copy that converts covers how to turn those pages from information into enquiries.

Be realistic about timing. New and updated pages usually take weeks to be crawled and months to reach their full ranking. That is normal, not a sign that the work failed. What you can check sooner is whether pages are indexed, whether impressions are growing in Search Console, and whether the queries bringing people in are the ones you intended. If impressions grow but clicks stay low, go back to titles and descriptions. If clicks grow but enquiries do not, go back to the page itself.

Local traffic: be easy to find where nearby customers look

For a business that serves a local area, a large share of website traffic starts somewhere other than your website: Google Search and Google Maps. When someone searches for a service "near me" or in a named town, Google often shows a map with a short list of businesses before it shows website results. If your Google Business Profile is incomplete, out of date, or missing reviews, you lose those clicks before your website ever gets a chance.

Claim and complete the profile properly. Use the exact business name you use everywhere else, the correct primary category, and every relevant secondary category. List the services you actually offer, with descriptions, and keep hours accurate, including holiday changes. Add real photos of your work, your team, and your premises, not only stock images. Post short updates regularly, such as a seasonal service, a new offering, or a reminder about booking lead times. Answer questions in the questions section, because the answers you leave there are visible to every future searcher. Our guide to ranking higher on Google Maps covers categories, reviews, citations, and the website work that supports the profile, in detail.

Reviews are part of local traffic, not a separate chore. A steady flow of recent, detailed reviews with replies from the owner tells both Google and future customers that the business is active and trusted. Ask at the moment a customer is happiest, with a direct link, and make it part of every job, not a campaign you run once a year. Reply to every review, positive and negative, briefly and specifically. A profile with ten recent reviews and thoughtful replies will usually earn more clicks than a profile with fifty old reviews and silence. Keep your name, address, and phone number identical everywhere they appear online: your website, your profile, directories, and social accounts. Inconsistencies confuse customers and weaken the signals search engines use to match your business to local searches.

Think about the towns and neighbourhoods you actually serve, in the language customers use. People search for "dentist in Kanata," "snow removal in Orleans," and "catering in Mississauga," not for a postal code or a municipal boundary. Use those place names naturally in titles, headings, and body text where they are true. If you are a service-area business without a storefront customers visit, say so clearly on your website and profile, and define the area you cover. Clarity about where you work saves you enquiries from places you cannot serve, and it helps the right customers find you faster.

Make the traffic you already get work harder

Growing traffic is only half the job. Many small business websites already receive visitors who leave because the site makes the next step hard. Before you chase new channels, fix the leaks.

Check the site on a phone, on a real phone, not only by shrinking a desktop browser window. Most local searches happen on phones, and a page that is slow, cramped, or hard to tap will lose visitors who were ready to call. Test the three things a visitor must be able to do: find what you offer, see evidence that you are trustworthy, and contact you without hunting. The phone number should be tappable. The contact form should be short. The address and hours should be easy to find. If a visitor has to scroll past three screens of introduction before learning what you do, rewrite the top of the page. Our guide to why a website is slow covers the common causes, from oversized images to heavy scripts, and the fixes that matter most.

Rewrite titles and descriptions for your ten most important pages. A good title names the service, the place, and the business, in that order of importance. A good description is a short pitch, not a repeat of the title: what the visitor gets, why this business, and what to do next. Keep descriptions honest. A description that promises something the page does not deliver earns a click and a fast exit, which helps nobody. While you are there, check that every page has one clear next step, and that the step matches the visitor's stage. A first-time visitor reading a guide is more likely to download a checklist, read a related service page, or send a question than to book immediately. A visitor on a service page is ready for a quote, a call, or a booking link. Give each page the next step its reader is ready for, and link onwards instead of leaving a dead end.

Fix the pages that already rank. In Search Console, find pages that appear on the second page of results, roughly positions 11 to 20, for searches that matter to you. Those pages are close. Improving the content, adding the missing answer, tightening the title, and adding a relevant internal link can move them onto the first page, where most clicks happen. This is often the fastest free traffic win available to an established site, and it takes hours, not months. Do this before you write new content. New pages compete with the pages you already have; stronger existing pages compound.

Email: the traffic channel you own

Search and social traffic are rented. An algorithm change, a suspended account, or a budget cut can reduce them overnight. An email list is the one audience you can reach directly, on your own schedule, without paying per click. For most Canadian small businesses, email is also the cheapest way to turn one-time visitors into repeat visitors, and repeat visitors into customers.

Build the list honestly and slowly. Offer one clear reason to sign up, tied to a real benefit: a seasonal checklist, a price guide, first notice of booking openings, or a short email course that answers the questions new customers ask. Put the sign-up where it makes sense: at the end of helpful guides, on the contact page, and at checkout or booking confirmation. Do not buy email lists. A bought list reaches people who never asked to hear from you, damages your sender reputation, and can breach Canadian anti-spam law. Under Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation, known as CASL, commercial emails generally require consent and must identify the sender and include a working unsubscribe option. Keep records of how each subscriber gave consent, and make unsubscribing easy. A clean, consented list of two hundred local subscribers is worth far more than a bought list of ten thousand strangers.

Send on a schedule you can keep. For most small businesses, once or twice a month is enough: one useful piece of content, one timely reminder, and one clear link back to the website. A newsletter that only announces sales will be ignored. A newsletter that answers a seasonal question, shares a short customer story with permission, or explains a change that affects customers will be opened. Every email should give the reader a reason to visit the website, such as the full guide, the booking page, or the new service page. Track which emails bring visitors who stay and enquire, not only which ones get opened. Open rates are unreliable since Apple Mail Privacy Protection, but clicks and enquiries are real.

Use automation for the predictable moments. A welcome email that arrives when someone signs up, a follow-up that arrives a few days after a quote request with answers to common questions, and a reminder when a seasonal booking window opens are all emails a small business can set up once and benefit from all year. Keep automated emails short, personal in tone, and easy to reply to. A reply from a real person is often the start of a sale, and a "do not reply" address tells the customer not to bother.

Social media, video, and community: distribution, not just posting

Social media rarely sends a flood of website traffic on its own, but it earns its place as distribution for the content you already created. A guide that sits unread on your website helps nobody. The same guide, cut into a short video, a carousel, or a plain-language post that answers one question, can reach people who would never have searched for it, and a fraction of them will click through to read the full version.

Pick one or two platforms where your customers actually spend time, and be consistent there. A trades business might do well with short job videos and before-and-after photos on Instagram and Facebook. A business-to-business service might do better with practical posts on LinkedIn. A restaurant or salon might rely on Instagram and TikTok. The platform matters less than the habit: posting useful, specific content regularly, answering comments, and linking back to the full page on your website. Post the answer, not only the link. A post that says "New blog post, link in bio" gives a reader no reason to click. A post that answers the question in sixty seconds and ends with "the full checklist, with prices for Ontario, is on our website" gives them a reason.

Video deserves special attention because it is how many people now search. Short videos that answer one question, show real work, or explain a process in plain language can be found through YouTube search, TikTok search, and Instagram search, as well as through Google. You do not need expensive production. A clear voice, good light, captions for viewers watching without sound, and a title that states the question being answered will outperform a polished video about nothing in particular. End every video with one next step, and put the link where viewers can find it: the description, the profile, or a pinned comment.

Communities and local groups can bring highly relevant traffic, but only if you participate honestly. Answer questions in local Facebook groups, neighbourhood forums, and industry communities with genuinely helpful answers, and link to your website only when it is the best answer to the question asked. A plumber who answers a question about frozen pipes in a Barrie community group, with a link to a guide on preventing frozen pipes, is helping. A business that drops links in every thread without answering the question is advertising, and group members and moderators treat it that way. The same rule applies to directories and listings: keep profiles complete and consistent, on the directories your customers actually use, and do not pay for listings on sites nobody visits.

Paid traffic: buy speed, not a strategy

Paid ads are the fastest way to increase website traffic, and the fastest way to waste money if the website is not ready. Use them for speed and testing, not as a replacement for the slower channels above. Ads stop the moment the budget stops. Search, email, and a strong local profile keep working.

Google Ads suits high-intent searches: people actively looking for a service in your area. Start narrow, with a small set of searches that match your most profitable service, in the towns you serve, and send each ad to a page that matches the ad. An ad for furnace repair should land on the furnace repair page, not the homepage. A mismatch between the ad and the landing page is the most common reason small business ad campaigns fail: the visitor clicks, does not see what was promised, and leaves. Track enquiries, not just clicks, and give a campaign at least a few weeks of data before judging it, unless the spend is clearly going to the wrong searches. Our guide to how much Google Ads costs in Canada covers typical monthly budgets, what drives costs up or down, and how to tell whether the spend is working.

Meta ads, on Facebook and Instagram, suit a different job: reaching people who are not searching yet but match your customer profile, and reminding past visitors to come back. They work well for offers, seasonal services, and visual work, and they work best when the landing page asks for one simple action, such as booking, requesting a quote, or downloading a guide. Retargeting, showing ads to people who already visited your website, is usually the most efficient paid spend for a small business, because the audience already knows you. Keep retargeting audiences small and honest: show them something useful, not the same ad fifty times. Our guide to how much Facebook ads cost in Canada covers budgets, benchmarks, and the difference between boosting a post and running a proper campaign.

Set a test budget you can afford to learn from, and decide in advance what success looks like. A test might be a few hundred dollars in CAD over two to four weeks, aimed at one service, with a target number of enquiries and a maximum cost per enquiry you are willing to accept. If the campaign beats the target, increase the budget gradually. If it does not, fix the landing page, the offer, or the targeting before spending more. Never let an ad platform spend on broad, vague searches by default. Review the actual search terms your ads appeared for, add the irrelevant ones as negatives, and check that your ads are not showing outside the area you serve. A small, well-managed campaign will usually outperform a large, unmanaged one.

AI search and zero-click results: be the source, not the summary

More searches are now answered directly on the results page or by an AI assistant, without a click to any website. That does not make your website pointless, but it changes what kind of traffic to expect. Raw visit counts for simple questions will fall. The visitors who do click tend to arrive better informed and closer to buying, because they have already read the summary and want the detail, the price, or the booking page.

Make your pages easy to cite. State the answer to the main question clearly and early, in plain language, before the background and detail. Use headings that match real questions. Put prices, service areas, hours, and policies in text, not only in images. Keep facts consistent across your website, your Google Business Profile, and your social profiles, because AI tools cross-check sources and favour pages that agree with each other. None of this requires special software. It is the same clear, specific writing that has always worked for search, with more attention to the first paragraph of each page.

Do not chase tricks for AI visibility. There is no shortcut that reliably places a business in AI answers, and tactics that promise one should be treated with suspicion. The pages that AI tools cite tend to be the pages that already do the fundamentals well: clear answers, real expertise, consistent business information, and a good reputation in reviews and mentions elsewhere. Build those, and measure what matters: branded searches for your business name, direct visits, and enquiries that mention they found you through an AI answer or a Google summary. Those signals are harder to count than clicks, but they are closer to revenue.

What not to do

A few common shortcuts look tempting when traffic is low. All of them cost more than they earn.

  • Do not buy website traffic. Services that promise thousands of visitors for a few dollars deliver bots, not customers. The visits inflate your analytics, convert at zero, and pollute the data you need to make real decisions. Buy traffic only from real advertising platforms, where you pay for genuine people and can see what they searched for and what they did next.
  • Do not stuff pages with keywords. Repeating a search phrase in every sentence reads badly, helps nobody, and can hurt rankings. Use the phrase where a reader would expect it, then use natural variations and related terms. Write for the customer first, and check the search basics second.
  • Do not publish thin content at volume. Fifty short, generic posts will not outperform five thorough, specific guides that answer real customer questions. Every page should earn its place by answering something better, more specifically, or more honestly than what already ranks. If a page does not do that, improve it or remove it.
  • Do not copy competitors. Borrowing a structure or learning from a competitor's topics is normal. Copying their text, photos, or reviews is not, and it tells customers nothing about why they should choose you. Your own jobs, your own answers, and your own voice are the parts a competitor cannot copy.
  • Do not redesign the website to fix a traffic problem. A new design can help if the site is slow, hard to use on a phone, or unclear about what you offer. A redesign alone does not bring visitors. Fix the discoverability, the content, and the distribution first. Redesign when the evidence says the site itself is the bottleneck.
  • Do not judge a channel by vanity numbers. Followers, likes, and raw visits feel good and say little. A channel earns its budget when it brings enquiries from the right people at a cost you can afford. Judge every channel by enquiries, bookings, or sales, tracked back to the source that produced them.

A 90-day plan that fits a busy owner

Most small business owners do not have ten hours a week for marketing. The plan below assumes about three hours a week, focused on one job at a time. Adjust the pace to your schedule, but keep the order: fix, then publish, then promote, then pay.

Days 1 to 14: fix the foundation. Set up Search Console and Analytics if they are not running. Check indexing for your main pages. Rewrite the titles and descriptions for your ten most important pages. Complete your Google Business Profile and ask your last five happy customers for reviews. Test the site on a phone and fix the biggest usability problem you find. Write down your baseline numbers.

Days 15 to 45: publish answers. Write or improve one thorough page per week, starting with your most profitable service and the question customers ask most. Each page should answer the question completely, name the service area, and end with one clear next step. Update one older page that already ranks on the second page of results. Share each new page once on your chosen social platform, in the platform's own format, with the full answer summarized, not only a link.

Days 46 to 75: build the owned audience. Add a sign-up offer to your best guide and your contact page. Send your first useful email to the list you have, even if it is small. Start asking every customer for a review, every time. Publish one short video per week answering a single question, and link it back to the full guide. If you have budget for a test, start one small, narrow paid campaign to a matching landing page, and review the actual search terms weekly.

Days 76 to 90: measure and repeat. Compare your numbers to the baseline: sessions by source, clicks and impressions in Search Console, email clicks, and, most importantly, enquiries by source. Keep what produced enquiries. Pause or fix what produced only visits. Pick the next service page or question to answer, and start the cycle again. Traffic growth compounds when each cycle leaves the site stronger than the last: more answers, more reviews, more subscribers, and more pages that already rank.

Expect search and content results on a timeline of months, not days. Most small businesses that publish consistently see meaningful movement in three to six months, with local and less competitive searches moving first. Paid ads and email move faster, but they need the same foundation: a page worth landing on, and a way to measure whether the visitor became an enquiry. If after ninety days nothing has moved, the problem is usually one of the three diagnosis questions at the start of this guide: the pages are not indexed, the titles do not match what searchers want, or the pages do not convert the visitors they already get. Go back to those before adding a new channel.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to increase website traffic?

Paid search ads and an email to an existing list are the fastest, because both can put visitors on your site within hours. A complete Google Business Profile and fixing titles and descriptions on pages that already rank can also bring gains within days or weeks. For growth that lasts, pair those quick wins with search content and reviews, which take three to six months to build but keep working after the initial effort ends.

How can I increase website traffic for free?

Search content built around real customer questions, a complete Google Business Profile, steady reviews, fixing titles and pages that already rank, and email to a list you grow yourself are the main free channels. Free means paid in time rather than money. Each one works, but only with consistent effort over months, and only if the pages visitors land on are fast, clear, and easy to contact you from.

How long does it take to increase website traffic?

It depends on the channel. Paid ads and email can show results in days or weeks. Local profile improvements can show movement in weeks. Search content usually takes three to six months of consistent publishing to show meaningful growth, and competitive searches in large cities take longer. Judge progress by impressions and enquiries trending upward over months, not by a single week of numbers.

Should I buy website traffic?

Buy traffic from real advertising platforms such as Google, Meta, or LinkedIn, where you pay for genuine people and can track what they do. Never buy from services promising thousands of visitors for a few dollars. That traffic is bots, it converts at zero, and it pollutes the analytics you need to make real decisions. If a traffic offer sounds too cheap to be real, it is.
