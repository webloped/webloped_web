How much does a logo cost in Canada in 2026?

A logo can cost nothing or tens of thousands of dollars, and both prices can be right for different businesses. Here is what Canadian small businesses actually pay at every level, what you get for the money, and the ownership and tax details that catch owners out.

So, how much does a logo cost in Canada? For most small businesses, a professionally designed logo costs between $300 and $3,500 CAD. A junior freelancer will usually quote $300 to $1,500, a senior designer or small studio $1,500 to $3,500, and a full brand identity system, which is the logo plus colours, typography, and usage rules, runs $2,500 to $10,000 with an experienced freelancer or small studio. At the extremes, an AI logo generator costs $0 to $30, a basic Fiverr gig starts around $5, and a large agency rebrand can run past $25,000.

The honest answer is that you are not buying a picture. You are buying a decision-making process, a set of files that work everywhere from a business card to a truck door, and legal ownership of the result. The price depends on who does the work, how much thinking goes into it, what files you receive, and whether you actually own what you paid for. This guide breaks down every option in Canadian dollars, using typical market ranges published by Canadian designers and studios in 2026, not quotes. Every price here is before tax unless it says otherwise.

The short answer: logo costs in Canada at a glance

Most Canadian small businesses land in one of these bands. Find the one that matches your situation and you have a realistic budget.

  • AI logo generator: $0 to $30. A quick mark for a placeholder or an internal mockup. You get a PNG export and limited variations, and the result is rarely distinctive enough to build a brand on.
  • Fiverr, basic gig: $5 to $80. One or two concepts, JPG or PNG delivery. Fine for a side project with no budget, risky for a business you plan to keep.
  • Fiverr, premium seller: $150 to $500. Multiple concepts and sometimes vector files. A reasonable way to test a concept before committing to a proper identity.
  • Logo contest, for example 99designs Canada: $409 to $1,799 before tax, based on contest prices read in September 2026. You get roughly 30 to 90 concepts from many designers, then pick a winner.
  • Independent freelancer: $300 to $1,500, with senior freelancers reaching $1,500 to $3,500. Discovery, two to three concepts, and vector source files. This is where most small Canadian businesses land.
  • Boutique studio: $1,500 to $4,500 for a logo, or $2,500 to $10,000 for a full identity package with strategy and brand guidelines. Best for a growing business investing in brand equity.
  • Mid-size agency: $5,000 to $20,000. A team-based process with brand strategy and multiple rounds of revision. Suited to funded companies and rebrands with several stakeholders.
  • Global brand consultancy: $20,000 to $100,000 and up. Full brand architecture, research, and an identity system. Enterprise, IPO-stage, or national launch territory.

Two things matter more than the number itself. First, check whether the price is a fixed project fee or an hourly rate, and whether it includes tax. Second, check what you actually receive at the end, because a cheap logo that arrives as a single small JPG is not a bargain. It is a file you will have to replace.

What each option actually gets you

The price bands above look like a simple ladder, but the experience at each rung is very different. Here is what the money buys in practice.

AI logo generators are fast and almost free. You type your business name, pick an industry and a style, and get a mark in minutes. The problem is that the output is assembled from common shapes, icons, and fonts, so your logo can look strikingly similar to another business in your town. There is also no one to ask whether the mark works at the size of a favicon, in one colour, or reversed out of a dark photo. Use an AI mark as a placeholder while you test a business idea, not as the face of a business you want customers to remember.

Fiverr and gig marketplaces vary enormously. A $5 gig is usually a template with your name swapped in, delivered as a flat image file. A premium seller in the $150 to $500 range will often deliver a few concepts and vector files, and for a pre-revenue startup that can be a sensible stopgap. The risk is quality control and ownership: check reviews carefully, ask what file formats are included, and get the copyright transfer in writing before you pay. A marketplace average for simpler logo work in Canadian cities, reported by Bark, sits around $800 in Toronto, $725 in Calgary, and $650 in Quebec City, which is a useful anchor when a gig price looks too good to be true.

Logo contests flip the process around: dozens of designers submit concepts and you pick a winner. The 99designs Canada packages read in September 2026 ran from C$409 to C$1,799 before sales tax, for roughly 30 to 90 concepts. You see a lot of variety, which is genuinely useful when you do not know what you want yet. The downside is that no designer spends real time understanding your business, so the winning mark is often the most polished rather than the most strategic. You also need to check the contest terms for who owns the copyright and whether the designer transfers it to you.

An independent freelancer is the sweet spot for most Canadian small businesses. For $300 to $1,500 with a junior or mid-level freelancer, or $1,500 to $3,500 with a senior designer, you typically get a short discovery conversation, two to three concepts, a couple of rounds of revision, and the vector source files. The designer asks about your customers, your competitors, and where the logo has to work, and that thinking is what you are paying for. Ask for a fixed price in writing, a clear number of concepts and revision rounds, and a list of the files you will receive.

A boutique studio adds strategy and a system. In the $1,500 to $4,500 range for a logo, or $2,500 to $10,000 for a full identity, you get positioning work, a logo suite with primary, secondary, and icon versions, a colour system, typography, and a brand guide your team can actually follow. A studio in this band typically takes two to three weeks for a logo-only project and four to twelve weeks for a full identity. This is the right level when the logo has to carry a real business: a storefront, a fleet, packaging, or a website that has to look consistent everywhere.

Agencies and consultancies are for businesses where the brand itself is the asset. A mid-size agency at $5,000 to $20,000 brings a team, research, and stakeholder workshops. A global consultancy at $20,000 to $100,000 or more builds brand architecture across product lines and markets. Most small businesses do not need this, and spending at this level before you have customers is usually the wrong order of priorities.

A logo is not a brand identity, and the price shows it

People use the words logo and brand interchangeably, and designers charge very differently for them. A logo is one mark: the symbol and wordmark that identifies your business. A brand identity is the system around it: the logo suite, colour rules, typography, voice notes, and a brand guidelines document that tells anyone, from your web designer to your sign maker, how to use it all consistently.

A logo alone from a freelancer typically runs $500 to $2,500 in Canada, according to 2026 studio pricing guides. A starter identity from a studio, meaning the logo plus basic colour, type, and core files, runs $2,500 to $6,000. A full identity, with a complete logo suite, palette, typography, guidelines, and applications, runs $8,000 to $20,000. An agency rebrand with strategy, naming, and rollout starts at $25,000.

Which one do you need? If you are a sole trader or a new business testing an idea, a logo alone is enough, and you can build the identity later. If you are launching a business that will appear in many places at once, a website, social profiles, packaging, uniforms, a storefront, then the identity pays for itself quickly, because every supplier works from the same rules instead of guessing. The cheapest logo is the one you never have to buy twice, and the most common reason businesses pay twice is that the first logo came with no system around it.

Your website is where the logo lives most visibly, so the two decisions belong together. A finished logo and colour palette shorten the design phase of a website, and our guide to choosing a colour scheme for your website shows how the brand colours flow into the site. If you are budgeting for both at once, read our guide to how much a website costs in Canada and plan the two as one project, not two separate purchases.

What actually drives the price up or down

When two quotes differ by a factor of five, the difference is almost always in the scope. These are the levers that move the number.

  • Strategy and discovery. A designer who spends a day learning about your customers, competitors, and positioning charges more than one who opens a blank canvas and starts drawing. The strategy is also what keeps the logo from looking like a generic icon.
  • Number of concepts and revision rounds. Two to three concepts with two rounds of revision is standard at the freelancer level. Unlimited revisions sounds generous, but it usually means the designer has not scoped the work properly, and it can drag a project out for months.
  • Logo suite and applications. A single mark is cheap. A suite with a primary logo, a stacked version, a horizontal version, an icon, and one-colour and reversed versions costs more, because each one is designed, not just resized. Add mockups for signage, packaging, or social profiles and the price rises again.
  • File formats. Vector files, SVG, EPS, and PDF, can be scaled to any size without losing quality. Raster files, PNG and JPG, cannot. A logo delivered only as a PNG is a liability: it will blur on a sign and pixelate on a vehicle wrap. Insist on vector source files in the quote.
  • French versions. If you sell in Quebec, you may need a French version of a wordmark or tagline, and that is real design work, not a translation pasted into the same layout. Budget for it up front if Quebec is a real market for you.
  • Timeline. Two to three weeks is normal for a logo-only project. Six to ten weeks is normal for a full brand system. A rush job in a few days costs more, and it usually costs more again later when the rushed mark needs replacing.
  • Who you hire. A junior designer in a smaller city charges less than a senior designer in Toronto or Vancouver. That does not mean the cheaper designer is worse for your job, but it does mean you should compare portfolios at similar price points, not across them.

When you compare quotes, compare them on the same scope. Ask every designer for the same list: concepts, revision rounds, logo versions, file formats, copyright transfer, and timeline. A quote that looks expensive often includes the files and the ownership that a cheaper quote leaves out.

Who owns your logo? The part most owners skip

This is the most important section in this guide, and the one most small business owners never ask about until it is too late. Under Canada's Copyright Act, the designer owns the copyright in the logo unless it is assigned to you in writing. Paying for a logo does not automatically transfer ownership. You need a written assignment, and it should be part of the agreement before work starts, not an afterthought when you want to trademark the mark or sell the business.

Ask for three things in writing: a full assignment of copyright to your business, the vector source files, and confirmation that the designer has not used stock imagery, clip art, or a font they do not have a licence for. A logo built on a font or icon the designer does not own can create a problem years later, when a sign company or a trademark examiner asks where it came from.

Then there is the trademark question, which is separate from copyright. Owning the copyright means you own the artwork. A trademark registration with the Canadian Intellectual Property Office, CIPO, is what gives you the exclusive right to use the mark for your goods or services across Canada. Filing a trademark application online costs $491.06 for the first class of goods or services in 2026, and $149.04 for each additional class. Most small businesses file in one or two classes, so budget roughly $500 to $650 in government fees, plus legal fees if you use a trademark agent. You do not need to trademark on day one, but if the logo matters to your business, get a trademark search done before you spend money on signage, packaging, or a website built around it. Discovering that a similar mark already exists after launch is far more expensive than a search before it.

Hidden costs to budget for

The design fee is rarely the whole bill. These are the extras that catch Canadian owners out.

  • Tax. Designers and agencies registered for sales tax add 5% GST, or HST of 13% to 15% depending on the province, and Quebec adds QST on top of GST. A $2,000 logo in Ontario costs $2,260 after HST. Always ask whether a quote includes tax, and budget for it.
  • Font licences. A logo that uses a commercial font may need a licence, and the licence is usually paid by you directly to the foundry. Some fonts are free for commercial use, some cost a few hundred dollars, and some charge by the number of users or the size of the company. Ask whether the fonts in your logo are licensed and what the licence covers.
  • Trademark fees. As above, roughly $500 to $650 in CIPO filing fees for one or two classes, plus any agent fees. A trademark search before filing is a separate, smaller cost, and it is worth it.
  • Applications. The logo is only the start. Business cards, signage, vehicle lettering, uniforms, packaging, and a website all cost money to produce, and a logo that was designed without those uses in mind can need expensive rework. Tell the designer where the logo has to appear.
  • The rebrand you did not plan for. A logo that was too cheap, too generic, or delivered without vector files usually has to be redrawn within a year or two, and the second logo costs more than the first because now you also have to replace everything the first logo touched. A realistic budget the first time is cheaper than a cheap logo plus a rebrand.

How to get an accurate quote

Before you contact anyone, write down five things: what your business does and who it serves, where the logo has to appear, whether you need a logo alone or a full identity, how many concepts and revision rounds you expect, and when you need it. That one page of notes is the difference between a quote you can trust and a guess.

Then ask for a fixed price in CAD, before tax, with the deliverables listed: the number of concepts, the revision rounds, the logo versions, the file formats, the copyright assignment, and the timeline. Ask who owns the files, what happens if you want changes after the revision rounds are used up, and whether the price includes a brand guide or just the mark. Get it in writing.

Check the portfolio before you check the price. A designer whose past work looks like your industry and your ambitions is worth more than a cheaper designer whose work does not. Ask for references or past clients you can look up, and look at the logo at small sizes: a mark that is legible as a phone icon and on a business card is a mark that was designed properly. Our guide to choosing a web designer in Canada covers the same vetting questions, and most of them apply to logo designers too.

For a realistic starting point, a Canadian small business that wants a professional logo from a freelancer should plan on $500 to $2,000 CAD before tax, including the vector files and a written copyright transfer. If you also need the full identity system, plan on $2,500 to $6,000 from a studio. Both numbers assume a clear brief and a normal timeline, not a rush job.

When a cheap logo is fine, and when it is not

A cheap logo is a sensible choice when the business is still an idea. If you are testing whether anyone wants what you sell, a $30 AI mark or a $150 marketplace logo is enough to get a landing page and a social profile live. Spend the money on the logo after you have customers, not before. The same applies to a side project, a hobby business, or an internal tool that customers never see.

A cheap logo is a bad choice when the logo has to do real work: a storefront sign, a vehicle, packaging on a shelf, a website that has to convert visitors into enquiries. In those places, a generic or blurry mark quietly tells customers the business cuts corners, and no amount of advertising fixes that impression. It is also a bad choice when you plan to trademark the mark, franchise the business, or sell it one day, because the buyer will ask who owns the logo, and a marketplace gig with no written assignment is a weak answer.

The rule is simple: match the spend to the stakes. A logo that appears on one Instagram profile is a $100 problem. A logo that appears on everything your business owns is a $2,000 to $5,000 decision, and it is worth treating like one.
