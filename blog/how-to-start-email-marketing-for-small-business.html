How to start email marketing for small business: a practical guide for Canadian businesses

You already have the hardest part: customers and website visitors who know your name. Email marketing is simply the system that lets you reach them again, on your own terms, without paying for every message.

Picture a small bakery in Guelph. Every Saturday, the counter is busy, the website gets a steady stream of visitors checking the menu, and a few dozen people place orders online. Monday comes, the shop is quiet, and the owner has no way to tell Saturday's customers that a new seasonal pastry is out, or that the holiday pre-order window just opened. The customers are not gone. They just have no reason to think about the bakery until they happen to want a cake again, and by then they might search and find someone else first.

That gap is exactly what email marketing fills. It is not a newsletter for its own sake, and it is not about blasting promotions to strangers. It is a short, useful message sent to people who already chose to hear from you, at a moment when it helps them. The bakery's Saturday customer gets a Tuesday email about the holiday pre-order deadline. The landscaping client gets a March reminder that spring clean-up booking opens next week. The clinic patient gets a note that flu shot appointments are available. Each message is small, timely, and welcome, because the person asked for it.

Learning how to start email marketing for small business is mostly a matter of doing the steps in the right order. Many owners start by designing a beautiful newsletter, then realize they have no list to send it to, or they collect addresses first and then freeze, unsure what to write. This guide fixes the order: pick one goal, choose a simple tool, build your first list the legal way under Canada's anti-spam law, write a welcome series, and then send your first regular emails. No bought lists, no tricks, and no tool that needs a marketing degree to run.

The short answer

How to start email marketing for small business comes down to seven jobs, done in this order. First, pick one goal for your list, such as repeat bookings, repeat orders, or re-engaging past customers. Second, choose a simple email tool that handles forms, consent records, and unsubscribes for you. Third, build your first list from people who actually agree to hear from you, using a sign-up form on your website and an unchecked consent checkbox at checkout or booking. Fourth, write a short welcome email series that delivers what you promised and reminds new subscribers how often you will write. Fifth, plan your first regular emails around things your customers genuinely need to know: seasonal openings, reminders, useful tips, and occasional offers. Sixth, write each email like a short note to one person, with one clear next step. Seventh, measure clicks, replies, and bookings rather than chasing open rates. Do those seven things and you will have a working email system in about a month, built on rules that keep you on the right side of Canadian law.

One idea runs through all of it: an email list is a relationship, not an audience you rent. Social media followers are rented attention, because an algorithm decides how many of them see each post, and that share can drop overnight for reasons that have nothing to do with your business. Search traffic is similar, because a ranking you held for months can shift in one update. An email list is different. You own the list, you choose when to send, and every message goes to the inbox of someone who asked for it. That control is why email remains the most reliable way for a small business to fill a slow week, announce a seasonal opening, or bring back past customers.

Why email marketing is worth starting, even with a tiny list

Size is not the point, especially at the start. A list of 150 local people who chose to hear from a neighbourhood business is far more valuable than a list of 5,000 strangers who never asked. The small list opens, replies, books, and buys. The large cold list ignores you, marks you as spam, and damages your ability to reach anyone at all, including the people who genuinely want your emails. If your list grows by five to ten real subscribers a week, that is real progress for most small businesses, and it compounds. After a year, that is hundreds of warm contacts you did not have before.

Email also fits the way most small business sales actually happen. Few people hire a roofer, book a clinic, or choose a caterer on their first visit to a website. They research, compare, and come back later, or they forget. A short, useful email every few weeks keeps you present during that gap without requiring the visitor to remember you. When they are ready, you are not a stranger from a search result. You are the business that sent them the checklist they actually used.

There is also a cost argument, and it is simple. Most small business email tools offer a free tier for small lists, and even paid plans are usually a modest monthly amount compared with what a single month of ads costs. You do not need a big budget to start. You need a clear offer, a working form, and the discipline to send one useful email a month. The rest is refinement, and this guide covers that too.

Step 1: pick one goal for your list

Before you choose a tool or write a word, write one sentence that finishes this thought: "This list exists so that..." If you cannot finish that sentence clearly, your emails will not be clear either, and subscribers will drift away. Good answers are specific and tied to how your business actually makes money: repeat bookings for a service business, repeat orders for a shop, re-engaging past customers for a seasonal business, or filling quiet weeks for a clinic or studio. Bad answers are vague: staying in touch, building brand awareness, or sending updates. Vague goals produce vague emails, and vague emails get ignored.

One goal also keeps your first emails focused. A bakery whose goal is holiday pre-orders knows exactly what to send in November: the pre-order deadline, what is on the menu, and how to order. A landscaper whose goal is spring bookings knows what to send in March: booking opens next week, here is the spring checklist, here is how to reserve a slot. A list with one goal writes itself far more easily than a list that is supposed to do everything at once. You can add a second goal later, once the first one is working.

It also helps to decide who the list is for. A list for past customers can carry offers, reminders, and referral requests. A list for people still deciding needs helpful information first and selling second, because a hard pitch to someone who has not yet trusted you is the fastest way to lose them. You can serve both groups from one list by sending different emails to each group later, but at the start, be clear about the main audience. A form that says "Join 300 homeowners planning spring projects" speaks to a different person than one that says "Get offers and updates," and it will attract a different, better subscriber.

Step 2: choose a simple email tool

You need a proper email service, not a spreadsheet and your personal inbox. A real email tool handles sign-up forms, stores consent records, sends to many people at once without exposing everyone's address, and, critically, handles unsubscribes automatically. Sending a marketing email from a personal inbox to a list pasted into the blind-copy field is fragile, unprofessional, and hard to manage once the list grows past a handful of people. It also makes it easy to accidentally break the Canadian rules covered later in this guide, because nothing is tracking consent or unsubscribes for you.

When choosing a tool, check five things. First, that it lets you build sign-up forms and landing pages without hiring a developer. Second, that it records when and how each subscriber consented, because that record is your protection if consent is ever questioned. Third, that it handles Canadian requirements, such as including your business identification and an unsubscribe link in every commercial email. Fourth, that it can send automated emails, so a welcome message goes out the moment someone subscribes, even at 2 a.m. Fifth, that you can export your list at any time. Your list is your asset, and a tool that traps it is a bad partner, no matter how cheap it looks at first.

Well-known options for small businesses include Mailchimp, Kit, and Klaviyo, and most of them offer a free tier for small lists, so cost is rarely a reason to delay. Do not spend weeks comparing tools. Pick one that covers the five checks above, set it up, and start. The tool matters far less than the habit of sending one useful email a month. You can switch tools later, and a clean, exportable list moves with you. What you cannot recover is the year you spent choosing instead of sending.

One practical note: set up a proper sender address before your first send. Use an address on your own domain, such as hello@yourbusiness.ca, rather than a free personal address. Emails from your own domain look more professional, are more likely to reach the inbox, and show subscribers that the message comes from a real business. Most email tools will walk you through verifying your domain, and it takes only a few minutes. Our guide to choosing a domain name in Canada covers the domain side if you do not have one yet.

Step 3: build your first list the legal way

Your first subscribers should come from people you already reach: past customers, website visitors, and people you meet in person. The rule for all of them is the same. They must agree to receive your marketing emails, clearly and separately, before you send them anything. In Canada, that agreement is called consent, and the rules around it are strict enough to take seriously and simple enough to follow. The full rules are covered in the CASL section below. This step covers the practical side of collecting consent.

Start with your website. A short sign-up form on your homepage, at the end of blog posts, and in your footer will collect subscribers steadily from traffic you already have. The form should say plainly what the subscriber will receive and how often, and it should include an unchecked checkbox where the person agrees to receive marketing emails. The box must start empty, and the person must tick it themselves. A pre-ticked box does not count as consent, because the person did not actively agree. Our guide to collecting email addresses on your website covers form placement, lead magnets, and pop-ups in detail, and it is the natural companion to this guide. Collecting addresses and sending emails are two halves of the same system.

Next, ask at checkout and booking. These are high-trust moments, and they deserve care. A checkbox at checkout or after booking, clearly worded and unchecked by default, lets a customer choose to receive future emails. The wording should say plainly what they are agreeing to, such as "Yes, send me seasonal tips and booking openings by email." A separate checkbox for marketing emails, distinct from any terms-of-service agreement, is the clean way to do it. Never add a customer to your marketing list silently as a side effect of a purchase or booking, because that is not consent to marketing, even though you have their address for the transaction itself.

Then, ask in person. Every place you meet a potential subscriber is a place to collect an email, provided you ask clearly and record consent properly. A tablet at a counter with a short form works, and so does a simple paper sheet, as long as someone later enters those addresses accurately and only emails people who genuinely agreed. A QR code on a counter card, a flyer, or a business card can point to your sign-up page. Offline collection is slower than online, but the subscribers tend to be highly engaged, because they met you first.

Finally, record every consent. Note the date, the form or channel where the person signed up, and the exact wording they agreed to. Most email tools do this automatically when someone signs up through a form, which is another reason to use a proper tool rather than a spreadsheet. If consent is ever questioned, that record is your evidence. Keep it from day one, when the list is small and the habit is easy to build, rather than trying to reconstruct it later.

Step 4: write a welcome email series

Do not wait until you have a big list to write your first emails. Write a short welcome series now, set it to send automatically, and every new subscriber will receive it at the exact moment they are most interested: right after signing up. A welcome series is usually two or three short emails, sent over the first week or two, and its job is to deliver what you promised, introduce your business, and set expectations.

The first welcome email should arrive within minutes of sign-up. It delivers the promised offer, whether that is a checklist, a guide, a discount, or early access to booking. It reminds the subscriber what they signed up for and how often you will write. And it invites a reply. That last part matters more than it sounds. A reply from a subscriber tells their email provider that your messages are wanted, which helps future emails reach the inbox instead of the spam folder. Keep it short, warm, and human. Write it the way you would speak to a new customer standing in front of you.

The second email, a day or two later, can help the subscriber get value from what you sent. If the offer was a guide, point out the most useful part. If it was a checklist, explain the first step. This email can also answer the question new customers ask most often, or introduce the service that most first-time buyers choose. The third email, a few days later, can be a gentle introduction to what you do, a short story about a customer you helped, or an invitation to book, order, or visit. After that, the subscriber joins your regular emails, and the welcome series has done its job.

Test the whole series yourself before it goes live. Sign up with your own address on a phone, confirm each welcome email arrives, open every link, and check that the offer downloads or opens correctly. That ten-minute test catches most of the embarrassing failures: a broken link, a missing attachment, a subject line with a typo, or a form that adds people to the wrong list. A welcome series that works flawlessly builds trust at the exact moment a new subscriber is deciding whether to stay.

Step 5: plan your first regular emails

Once the welcome series is running, the question becomes what to send every month. The honest answer is that most small businesses do not need a complicated content calendar. They need a short list of email types that match their real business rhythm, repeated with fresh details. Four types cover almost every small business.

Useful and seasonal. The backbone of a good list. A seasonal checklist, a reminder that booking opens next week, a tip that solves a small problem your customers actually have, or a short guide tied to the time of year. A landscaper sends spring clean-up timing in March. A bakery sends holiday ordering deadlines in November. A clinic sends a reminder about flu shot appointments in October. These emails are welcome because they help the reader plan, and they position your business as the one that knows what is coming.

Announcements. New services, new products, new hours, a new location, or a seasonal opening. Keep announcements short and factual, and send them when the news is genuinely new. An announcement email that arrives months after the news is stale does more harm than good, because it tells subscribers you are not paying attention.

Proof and stories. A short story about a customer you helped, a before-and-after from a recent job, or a simple explanation of how a service works. These emails build trust without sounding like a sales pitch, because they show your work instead of describing it. Use real details and real outcomes, and never invent a testimonial or a result. A single honest story beats ten invented claims, and invented claims can get a Canadian business in real trouble under federal advertising law.

Offers, used sparingly. A discount, a bundle, a first-access window, or a limited-time booking slot. Offers work best when they are the exception, not the pattern. A list that receives an offer every week trains subscribers to wait for a discount and ignore everything else. A list that receives useful emails most of the time, and an offer occasionally, treats the offer as news worth opening. If your business genuinely competes on price or repeat purchases, offers can play a bigger role. Otherwise, lead with usefulness and let the offer be the bonus.

For frequency, start with once a month and only increase if subscribers respond well and unsubscribes stay low. Tell people the frequency when they sign up, and stick to it. A consistent monthly email that is genuinely useful will outperform a burst of weekly emails that trains subscribers to ignore you. If you have genuinely time-sensitive news, such as booking openings, a separate short announcement is fine, as long as it is the exception, not the pattern.

Step 6: write emails people actually open and read

Good email writing is simpler than most owners expect. Write like you are writing to one person, not to a list. Use short sentences, plain words, and the reader's language rather than your industry's jargon. A subject line should say what is inside the email in a few words, such as "Spring clean-up booking opens Monday" or "Your holiday pre-order deadline is Friday." Avoid subject lines that sound like a trick, such as "You will not believe this," because they erode trust and can push your emails toward spam filters.

Structure each email the same way. Start with the most useful point in the first two or three sentences, because many readers will decide in seconds whether to keep reading. Then add the detail: the dates, the steps, or the link. Then end with one clear next step, such as booking a slot, placing an order, or reading the guide. One email, one job, one button. An email that asks the reader to do five things will usually get them to do none of them.

Keep emails short. Most regular emails should be readable in under two minutes. If you have more to say, put it on your website and link to it, rather than pasting a long article into an inbox. Short emails are also easier to read on a phone, which is where most of your subscribers will open them. Before you send, open the email on your own phone and check that the text is readable, the button is easy to tap, and the images load quickly. A broken layout on a phone is the most common reason a good email gets deleted unread.

Personalize lightly and honestly. Using a subscriber's first name in the greeting is fine when you collected it properly. Sending different emails to different groups, such as past customers versus people still deciding, is better still, because each group gets a message that fits where they are. But do not pretend a mass email is a personal note, and do not use a misleading sender name. Subscribers can tell the difference, and the ones who feel misled are the ones who mark you as spam.

Step 7: measure what matters, and ignore what does not

Three numbers tell you most of what you need to know. First, your sign-up rate: new subscribers divided by visitors to the pages carrying your form. If a page gets good traffic and almost no sign-ups, the offer or the form is the problem, not the traffic. Second, clicks and replies on your regular emails. A click means someone found the email worth acting on. A reply means they trust you enough to talk back, and it helps your future emails reach the inbox. Third, bookings, orders, or enquiries that came from an email. Ask new customers how they found you, and note when they mention an email. That simple question, asked consistently, will tell you more about your list's value than any dashboard.

Do not chase open rates as a precise metric. Email providers now protect privacy in ways that make open tracking unreliable, and a reported open rate is at best a rough direction, not an exact count. Clicks, replies, bookings, and sales are far more meaningful. Watch unsubscribes and spam complaints too. A small number of unsubscribes after every send is normal. A spike is a signal to change something, usually frequency, relevance, or how sales-heavy the email was. If unsubscribes spike after a particular email, read it again honestly. It was probably too sales-heavy, too long, too frequent, or off-topic for the promise the subscriber signed up for.

Review the numbers monthly, change one thing at a time, and keep what works. If a subject line style gets more clicks, use it more. If a certain email type gets replies, send more of that type. If a frequency increase drives unsubscribes up, go back to monthly. A list built this way is slow to start and hard to beat once it is running. Canadian rules: CASL consent, in plain language

Canada's anti-spam legislation, known as CASL, sets the rules for sending commercial electronic messages, including marketing emails, to people in Canada. It has been in force since 2014, and it applies to businesses of every size. This section is general information, not legal advice, and if your situation is complicated, a short conversation with a lawyer is money well spent. But the core rules are straightforward, and most small businesses can follow them without legal training.

The central rule is consent. You need consent before sending a commercial email, and there are two kinds. Express consent means the person clearly agreed, for example by ticking an unchecked box that says they want your emails, or by signing up through a form that plainly describes what they are agreeing to. Express consent has no expiry date, although a person can withdraw it at any time by unsubscribing. Implied consent comes from an existing business relationship, such as a recent purchase, or an existing non-business relationship, such as a membership. Implied consent expires, generally two years after the last purchase or the end of a contract, and six months after an inquiry that did not lead to a purchase. It is narrower than express consent, and it should not be your main strategy for a marketing list. When in doubt, get express consent. It is clearer, lasts longer, and is easier to prove.

A few practices that fail the test are worth naming plainly. Buying an email list does not give you consent, because the people on it never agreed to hear from you, and emailing them can breach CASL and get your sending domain flagged as a spammer. Scraping addresses from websites or social profiles is not consent either. Pre-ticked boxes are not consent, because the person did not actively agree. Adding someone to your marketing list because they filled out a contact form or bought something, without a separate clear agreement to receive marketing emails, is not consent to marketing, even though you have their address for the transaction itself. Each of these shortcuts trades a short-term list boost for legal risk and a damaged sender reputation.

Every commercial email you send must also do three things: identify your business clearly, include valid contact information, and include a working unsubscribe option that takes effect promptly. The unsubscribe link must keep working for at least 60 days after the message is sent, and unsubscribe requests must be honoured within 10 business days. Keep records of how each subscriber consented, including the date, the form or channel, and the wording they agreed to. If a complaint ever arises, that record is your evidence. CASL penalties can be severe, reaching up to $10 million for organizations and $1 million for individuals, and the reputational cost of being flagged as a spammer can be worse than any fine for a small business. The rules are not there to stop you emailing customers. They are there to stop the businesses that email strangers, and a business that collects consent properly has nothing to fear from them. For the related question of what your website must disclose, see our guide on whether your website needs a privacy policy in Canada, since your sign-up forms should link to that policy.

One more Canadian detail: if your subscribers include people in Quebec, make sure your sign-up forms and emails are understandable to them. That can mean offering a French version of the form and the welcome email, or at minimum making sure the consent wording is clear. It is also good practice generally. A subscriber who understands exactly what they agreed to is a subscriber who stays.

What not to do

Most email marketing mistakes are versions of the same error: prioritizing list size over list quality. Do not buy a list, rent a list, or borrow a partner's list without clear, separate consent from every person on it. Do not email people who only contacted you about something else, such as a job application or a one-time support question, unless they separately agreed to marketing emails. Do not hide the unsubscribe link, make unsubscribing require a login, or keep emailing someone who unsubscribed. Do not send from a misleading sender name or a subject line that suggests the email is something it is not. Each of these tactics might produce a short-term bump in sends, and each of them damages the trust and deliverability that a good list is built on.

Also resist the temptation to email too often in the early excitement of having a list. A new list owner who sends four emails in the first week will lose subscribers faster than one who sends one thoughtful email a month. Start slow, watch unsubscribes and replies, and let subscriber behaviour tell you whether to increase frequency. And do not let the list go silent for months either. A subscriber who signed up in January and hears nothing until September will not remember who you are, and your email will look like spam. Pick a rhythm you can honestly keep for a year, put it on the form in plain words, and treat it as a promise.

A simple 30-day plan

Week one: write your one-sentence goal, choose a lead magnet you can produce in a few hours, and set up your email tool with a proper sender address, a sign-up form, and a short welcome email. Week two: add the form to your homepage, your footer, and the end of your most-visited blog post, and test everything yourself on a phone, including the welcome email and the download. Week three: add the offer to your booking or checkout flow as an unchecked consent checkbox, put the sign-up link in your social media bio, and, if you have a physical location, put a QR code or counter card in the shop. Week four: send your first regular email to the new subscribers, keeping it short and useful, and check your sign-up rate, welcome engagement, and unsubscribes. Then repeat what worked and fix what did not. In thirty days you will not have a huge list, but you will have a working system, and that system is the asset.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need permission before emailing someone in Canada?

Yes, for commercial emails you need consent under CASL, and express consent is the safest kind: the person clearly agreed to receive your marketing emails, for example by signing up through a form that plainly said what they were agreeing to. Keep a record of that consent. Implied consent from an existing business relationship is narrower and expires, so it should not be your main strategy for a marketing list.

How much does email marketing cost for a small business?

Most small business email tools offer a free tier for small lists, so you can start at no monthly cost beyond your domain and website, which you already pay for. As your list grows, paid plans are usually a modest monthly amount, and the cost depends on the size of your list and the features you use. The real cost at the start is your time: an hour or two to set up the tool and the welcome series, and an hour a month to write and send one useful email.

How often should I email my list?

Start with once a month and only increase frequency if subscribers respond well and unsubscribes stay low. Tell people the frequency when they sign up and stick to it. A consistent monthly email that is genuinely useful will outperform a burst of weekly emails that trains subscribers to ignore you. If you have genuinely time-sensitive news, such as booking openings, a separate short announcement is fine, as long as it is the exception, not the pattern.

Can I send marketing emails from Gmail?

You can send a personal email from Gmail, but you should not send marketing emails to a list from a personal inbox. A proper email tool handles consent records, unsubscribes, and delivery, and it sends from your own domain, which looks more professional and is more likely to reach the inbox. Sending bulk marketing mail from a personal account also risks having your account restricted, and it makes it hard to prove consent or honour unsubscribes properly under CASL.
