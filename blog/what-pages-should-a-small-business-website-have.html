What pages should a small business website have?

A small business website needs five core pages: Home, About, Services, Contact, and a Privacy Policy. Here is what each page must actually say and do, the extras worth adding, and the pages you can safely skip.

When a business owner asks for a website quote, the first question back is usually "how many pages do you need?" Most owners pause. They know they need a website, but the page list feels like something they should already know, so they guess. Home, about, contact... and then it gets fuzzy.

This is one of those questions with a surprisingly clear answer. Most small business websites need five core pages, a small number of supporting pages depending on the business, and nothing else. Every page beyond that should earn its place. This guide walks through what pages a small business website should have, what each one must contain, and the pages that sound important but usually are not.

The five pages nearly every business needs

This is the core of the site. If your website only had these five pages, it would be complete: clear, useful, and easy for a visitor to understand in under a minute.

1. Home

The homepage is not a company brochure. It is a five-second test. Within seconds of landing, a visitor should know what the business does, who it is for, and where it operates. That means:

  • A headline that says the actual thing. "Plumbing services in Cambridge, Ontario" beats "Welcome to our website" or "Quality you can trust." The first tells a visitor they are in the right place. The second could be any business.
  • One clear next step. Call now, get a quote, book online. One primary action, repeated in the same wording, not five competing buttons.
  • A short summary of services. Not the full detail - that lives on the services pages. Just enough that a visitor knows the range.
  • One reason to trust you. A review snippet, years in business, a local detail. One is enough on the homepage.
  • Your service area. Canadian visitors are quick to check this. "Serving the Waterloo Region" saves a lot of wasted calls from people you cannot help.

The homepage should not try to say everything. Its job is to orient the visitor and send them deeper.

2. About

The About page is where the business becomes a person. Visitors check it when they are deciding whether they trust you, and trust is what small businesses sell.

  • The story, in a few honest paragraphs. When the business started, why it started, who runs it. Real details - a photo of the actual people helps more than any slogan.
  • What you stand for. Not buzzwords. One or two plain sentences about how you treat customers and do the work.
  • Credentials that matter. Licences, certifications, trade memberships, insurance. For trades, food, finance, and health businesses in Canada, visitors actively look for these.

Skip the "mission, vision, values" wall of text. Nobody reads it, and it makes every business sound identical.

3. Services or Products

This is the page that earns the sale. Whether you offer services, sell products, or both, this page needs to be specific enough that a visitor can picture hiring you.

  • What exactly is included. "Drain cleaning" is vague. "Drain cleaning with camera inspection and a written report" is a service someone can say yes to.
  • Plain-language pricing or price ranges. You do not have to publish exact prices if your quotes are custom, but a range filters out visitors who cannot afford you and builds trust with everyone else. ("Most of our kitchen projects land between $8,000 and $15,000.")
  • Photos of the real work. Stock photos of smiling people hurt more than they help. Phone photos of your actual work beat them every time.
  • Individual pages for major services. If you offer three or four distinct services, each one deserves its own page. It reads better, and each page can rank on Google for its own search terms - "furnace repair Cambridge" instead of one generic services page trying to rank for everything.

4. Contact

The Contact page has one job: make reaching you effortless. The most common failure on small business websites is a contact page that makes people work for it.

  • A short form. Name, email or phone, and what they need. Every extra field costs you enquiries.
  • Your actual details. Phone number, business email, and address or service area. A real phone number on the page does more for trust than any testimonial.
  • Hours and response expectations. "We reply within one business day" tells a visitor what happens after they hit send.
  • A map if you have a location. Embedded maps help visitors and signal to Google that you are a real local business.

Put the phone number and a contact link in the header of every page too, so no one has to hunt for it.

5. Privacy Policy

This one is not optional. If your site collects any personal information - a contact form counts, so does Google Analytics - you need a privacy policy. In Canada, federal privacy law (PIPEDA) and provincial rules require businesses to explain what information they collect and how it is used. A basic policy covering what the form collects, who sees it, and how long you keep it is enough for most small sites. Your web developer can set up a template, but it should reflect how your business actually handles data.

Pages worth adding, depending on the business

These are not universal, but they earn their place for many businesses:

  • Reviews or testimonials. Real reviews with names and locations, or embedded Google reviews. Trust works best when it is verifiable. If you have fewer than a handful, collect a few before making a whole page of it - an empty testimonials page is worse than none.
  • FAQ. If customers keep asking the same five questions - pricing, timelines, whether you travel to their area, what to prepare - an FAQ page answers them once and saves you the repeated phone calls. It also catches long-tail search traffic nicely.
  • Portfolio or gallery. Essential for photographers, renovators, landscapers, designers, and any business where the result is visual. Organize it by project type so a visitor can find work like theirs.
  • Blog. A blog is how a small site grows on Google over time - each post is a new chance to answer a question customers search for. It only works if you actually publish, so commit to one post a month or skip it.
  • Booking or ordering. If customers book appointments or order online, the booking flow or shop needs its own dedicated space. Do not bury booking inside the Contact page.
  • Terms and conditions. Worth having for e-commerce, memberships, and booking systems where money changes hands or commitments are made. A developer or lawyer can advise on what yours needs to cover.

Pages you can usually skip

These show up on sitemaps out of habit, but most small businesses never need them:

  • A "Welcome" page. The homepage already says hello. A separate welcome page just adds a click between the visitor and what they came for.
  • A page per team member. Lovely for agencies and firms with several specialists. Unnecessary when the business is you and maybe a partner - put those people on the About page.
  • News or press pages you never update. A "Latest News" page with a single post from 2023 tells visitors the site is abandoned. If you are not publishing news regularly, do not make the page.
  • Sitemap pages. A visitor-facing HTML sitemap made sense in 2010. The real sitemap is the XML file search engines read, which is invisible to visitors.

What about a one-page website?

One-page sites - everything on a single scrolling page - are tempting because they are cheaper and simpler. They work fine for a very specific case: a business with one offer, one audience, and one call to action, like a landing page for a single service. For most businesses they are a bad trade. Search engines rank pages, not websites, so one page gives you exactly one title, one headline, and one chance to match what people search for. Your "furnace repair" and "AC installation" searches need different pages to rank well. More importantly, mixing everything into one long scroll makes the site harder to navigate, not easier - a visitor looking for pricing has to scroll past your entire story to find it.

The practical rule: if your business has more than two or three distinct services, or you want customers to find you on Google, build real pages.

Before you ask for a quote

Knowing your page list before you talk to a developer saves real money, because it lets them quote accurately instead of padding for uncertainty. You do not need the final text - just answers to these questions:

  1. What are your services or products, in plain words?
  2. Which of them deserve their own pages?
  3. What do customers always ask you? (That becomes your FAQ.)
  4. Do you take bookings, orders, or payments online?
  5. Do you have photos of real work, real staff, and a real location?
  6. What is the one action you want visitors to take?

A developer who knows this can quote the project properly and build a site that fits. A developer who has to extract it from you will bill you for the extraction.

FAQ

How many pages should a small business website have?

Most small business websites work best with 5 to 10 pages: the five core pages (Home, About, Services, Contact, Privacy Policy) plus individual service pages and supporting pages like an FAQ or portfolio. Quality beats quantity - five pages that are clear and specific will outperform twenty thin ones.

Is a one-page website enough for a small business?

Only for a single-offer business where visitors take one action, like booking one service. For most businesses a one-page site hurts search visibility and makes navigation harder. If you have more than a couple of distinct services, or you want to be found on Google, you need separate pages.

Do I need a privacy policy on my website in Canada?

Yes, if your site collects any personal information, including through a contact form or analytics. Canadian privacy law requires you to explain what you collect and how you use it. A basic, honest policy is enough for most small businesses.

Should my services have separate pages or all be on one page?

If you offer several distinct services, give each major one its own page. It is easier for visitors to read, and each page can rank on Google for its own search terms. A single services page only works when the services are closely related variations of the same thing.
