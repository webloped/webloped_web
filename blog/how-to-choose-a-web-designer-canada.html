How to choose a web designer in Canada: a practical guide for small businesses

Choosing a web designer is one of the most expensive decisions a small business makes, and most owners make it with no framework at all. Here is how to judge portfolios, what to ask before you sign, and the red flags that should send you walking.

There is no shortage of web designers in Canada. Freelancers, small studios, full-service agencies, offshore teams, and website builders all compete for the same job, and their prices range from a few hundred dollars to tens of thousands. The cheapest option can be the right one, and the most expensive one can be a waste of money. Price alone tells you almost nothing.

The real problem is that most business owners evaluate designers the wrong way: they look at pretty portfolios first and ask about price second, and they never ask the questions that actually predict whether the project will go well. A beautiful website that does not bring in enquiries, cannot be found on Google, and cannot be updated without calling the designer is a bad website, no matter how good it looks in a screenshot.

This guide gives you a practical framework for how to choose a web designer in Canada. It covers what to decide before you contact anyone, the types of designers available, how to read a portfolio, the exact questions to ask, the red flags to watch for, and what to sort out about ownership and maintenance before you sign anything.

Step 1: decide what the website needs to do

Before you look at a single portfolio, write down the job the website has to do. A designer cannot give you a sensible quote or a sensible design until they know this, and if they do not ask you about it, that is your first warning sign.

Most small business websites fall into one of a few jobs. A brochure site exists to prove you are legitimate and to collect enquiries: services, about, contact, done. A lead-generation site actively wins customers: it needs clear calls to action, trust signals, and pages built around the searches your customers actually run. An e-commerce site has to sell, which means product pages, checkout, payment processing, and shipping logic. A booking site has to take appointments without phone tag. Each job needs a different build, a different budget, and a different kind of designer.

Write down your answers to four questions and bring them to every conversation: How do customers find and choose you today? What should a visitor do on the site - call, book, buy, request a quote? What pages and features are essential on day one, and what can wait? And who will update the site after launch - you, an employee, or the designer? If you cannot answer these yet, that is fine, but a good designer will help you work through them in a discovery call. A bad one will skip straight to colours and fonts.

Step 2: understand the types of web designer

There are four main options, and each suits a different situation.

  • Freelancer. One person, lower overhead, direct communication, and usually the lowest price for custom work. A good freelancer is excellent for a defined, one-off project. The risk is availability: one person can get sick, get busy, or move on, and there is little ongoing support after handoff unless you arrange it.
  • Small studio or boutique agency. A small team with a designer, a developer, and often someone handling SEO or copy. This is the sweet spot for most small businesses that want custom work and a real relationship: you get more than one skill set, and someone is still there after launch. The upfront investment is higher than a freelancer.
  • Full-service agency. A larger team that can handle strategy, branding, development, SEO, and advertising under one roof. The right choice for complex projects and larger budgets, but usually more process and more cost than a small business needs for a straightforward site.
  • Website builder (DIY). Wix, Squarespace, Shopify, or an AI builder you use yourself. Genuinely the right call for a simple site on a tight budget and a short timeline. Our comparison of website builders vs hiring a developer lays out when a builder wins and when it costs you more in the long run.

Most Canadian small businesses land somewhere between a freelancer and a small studio. The right choice usually comes down to how important the website is to your revenue, how much involvement you want in the process, and whether you need someone available after launch.

Step 3: read the portfolio properly

A portfolio is not a gallery. It is evidence, and you should read it like evidence. Click through to the live websites, not just the screenshots. A screenshot cannot tell you whether the site loads fast, works on a phone, or is easy to navigate.

Look for work for businesses like yours, or at least for businesses with a similar kind of buying decision. A designer who builds beautiful sites for restaurants may not be the right fit for a law firm, and vice versa. You are not looking for your exact industry, you are looking for evidence they understand your type of customer and your type of sale.

Then check three things on the live sites: Does the site make it obvious within a few seconds what the business does and what to do next? Does it work properly on your phone - readable text, tappable buttons, a form you can actually use? And does the site show up when you search for the business by name and by what it does in its city? A designer whose own clients cannot be found on Google is telling you something about their SEO work, whether they mean to or not.

Finally, ask what their role was on each project. Some portfolios show work the designer only touched lightly, or team projects where they handled one piece. An honest answer sounds like "I designed and built this one end to end" or "I handled the design, and a developer on my team built it." Vague answers are a reason to dig deeper.

10 questions to ask before you hire a web designer

These ten questions will tell you almost everything you need to know. Ask them on the first call, and compare the answers across every designer you talk to.

  • 1. Can I see live websites you built for businesses like mine? Live URLs, not screenshots, ideally for a similar business size or buying decision.
  • 2. What is your process from start to launch? Look for a clear sequence: discovery, structure, design, development, review, launch. No defined process usually means the project is made up as it goes.
  • 3. What platform will you build on, and will I own it? Ask what happens if you leave them. Can another developer take over the site? Is your domain registered in your name?
  • 4. Is SEO included, or is it an extra? SEO should be built in from day one - page titles, heading structure, fast loading, and a site Google can read - not bolted on after launch as a separate service.
  • 5. Who writes the copy? Many projects stall because nobody owns the words. Find out whether copywriting is included, whether you are expected to provide it, and what happens if you do not have it ready.
  • 6. Who is actually doing the work? Some agencies sell the project and hand it to a junior or an overseas contractor. You deserve to know who builds your site.
  • 7. What is the fixed price, and what exactly does it include? Get the scope in writing: number of pages, revisions, features, and what costs extra. A quote that cannot be explained clearly will grow.
  • 8. What is the timeline, and what do you need from me? Ask for milestones and a launch date, and be honest about how fast you can provide content and feedback. Our guide to how long it takes to build a website explains where delays usually come from.
  • 9. What happens after launch? Who handles updates, backups, and security? What does support cost? A website nobody maintains starts decaying the day it launches.
  • 10. Can I talk to a past client? One short conversation with a previous client is worth more than any portfolio page. If every reference is unavailable, treat that as information.

Pay attention to how they answer, not just what they say. A good designer welcomes these questions and answers them directly. A designer who gets defensive, rushes you, or answers everything with "it depends" without ever explaining what it depends on is showing you how the project will feel.

Red flags that should send you walking

Some warning signs are obvious in hindsight and easy to miss in the moment. Walk away, or at least slow down and get a second opinion, if you see any of these:

  • No fixed-price proposal, only hourly estimates. Without a fixed scope, a modest estimate can quietly double. Insist on a written scope and a fixed price for that scope.
  • They will not put ownership in writing. If the domain, the hosting account, or the website itself would live in the designer's name or on a platform you cannot leave, you are renting, not buying. That may be acceptable for a builder, but it should never be a surprise.
  • They promise first place on Google. Nobody can guarantee a ranking. A designer who promises one is either inexperienced or dishonest, and the same goes for guaranteed traffic or guaranteed sales.
  • No contract, no timeline, no milestones. A professional project has all three. If the arrangement is a handshake and a deposit, you have no recourse when things drift.
  • They need full payment upfront. A deposit to start is normal - commonly a third to a half - with the balance tied to milestones or launch. Full payment before any work is not normal.
  • They cannot explain their price. If a designer quotes you a number but cannot say what it includes, or what would make it go up, expect the number to go up.
  • Their own website is slow, broken, or hard to use. A web designer's own site is their best portfolio piece. If it fails the basics, that is the standard they work to.
  • Pressure to sign today. A discount that expires tonight is a sales tactic, not a reason to choose a designer. A good fit will still be a good fit next week.

What it costs to hire a web designer in Canada

Canadian pricing varies widely, and any honest guide says so upfront. As a rough picture: a simple brochure site from a freelancer often starts in the low thousands of CAD, a custom small business site from a small studio typically lands in the mid thousands, and larger agency projects go higher still. What matters more than any range is the fixed quote for your specific project, with the scope written down.

Three Canadian pricing details catch people out. First, confirm the currency. Many freelancers, tools, and platforms bill in USD, so a quote that looks reasonable can cost 30 to 40 percent more on your credit card statement after the exchange rate. Ask for the price in CAD, in writing. Second, ask what is included after launch. A quote that excludes hosting, updates, backups, and support is not cheaper, it is incomplete. Third, budget for content. If the designer expects you to provide copy and photos and you do not have them, either the project stalls or you pay extra later. Our breakdown of how much a website costs in Canada goes into the ranges and the ongoing costs in more detail.

Do not choose on price alone, in either direction. The cheapest quote often means corners you will pay for later - no SEO basics, no mobile testing, no ownership, no support. The most expensive quote does not automatically mean the best work. Choose the designer whose process, portfolio, and answers give you the most confidence, then check that the price is fair for the scope.

The ownership checklist: sort this out before you sign

This is the section most business owners skip, and it causes the most expensive problems later. Before you sign a contract or pay a deposit, confirm all of the following in writing:

  • Your domain is registered in your name, in an account you control. Never let a domain live inside a designer's account.
  • You own the website files, the content, and the design, and you have admin access to the site and the hosting.
  • You know what platform the site is built on, and whether another developer could take it over.
  • You have a copy of, or access to, everything needed to move the site: files, database, and login credentials.
  • Hosting, domain renewal, SSL, backups, and updates are all assigned to a named owner - you, the designer, or a care plan - so nothing falls through the cracks.
  • If the site collects customer information, the privacy and consent basics are covered. Our guide on whether you need a privacy policy in Canada explains what applies.

Changing designers is normal and should be easy. If leaving a designer would mean losing your website, your domain, or your content, the arrangement is wrong, no matter how good the design is.

Local vs remote: does location matter?

For most Canadian small businesses, location matters less than it used to. A designer in another province can build an excellent site, and remote collaboration - video calls, shared documents, screen shares - is normal now. The best designer for your project might be in your city, or they might be across the country.

Local still helps in a few cases. If you want in-person meetings, if the designer knows your local market and your competitors, or if your customers search for services in your city and the designer understands local SEO, a local designer has a real edge. A designer who has built sites for other businesses in your area will also have a better feel for what your customers expect.

Judge the designer on their work, their process, and their answers first, and treat location as a tiebreaker. A great remote designer beats a mediocre local one every time - but if two designers are otherwise equal, the one who knows your city is the better bet.

How to make the final decision

Talk to two or three designers, not ten. More than three and the comparisons blur; fewer than two and you have no baseline. Give each one the same brief - the job the website has to do, the pages and features you need, your budget range, and your timeline - so you are comparing like with like.

Then score each one on four things: strategic fit (did they ask about your business before talking about design?), evidence (live sites for similar businesses, a past client willing to talk), transparency (a fixed price in CAD, a written scope, clear ownership), and the relationship (do you trust them, and would you enjoy working with them for a month?). The designer who scores best on those four is the right hire, even if they are not the cheapest quote on the table.

And if every designer you talk to feels wrong, trust that feeling. A website is a multi-year asset. Waiting two weeks to find the right person beats spending a year with the wrong one.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to hire a web designer in Canada?

It varies widely with scope. A simple brochure site from a freelancer often starts in the low thousands of CAD, a custom small business site from a small studio typically lands in the mid thousands, and larger agency projects go higher. The number that matters is the fixed quote for your specific project, in CAD, with the scope written down. Always confirm whether the quote is in CAD or USD, and what is included after launch.

Should I hire a local web designer or can I work with someone in another province?

For most small businesses, location matters less than it used to. A designer in another province can build an excellent site, and remote collaboration is normal now. Local helps when you want in-person meetings, when the designer knows your local market, or when your customers search for services in your city and the designer understands local SEO. Judge the designer on their work and process first, and treat location as a tiebreaker.

Who owns my website after a designer builds it?

You should. Before you sign anything, confirm in writing that your domain is registered in your name, that you own the website files and content, and that you have admin access to the hosting and the site itself. If a designer builds on a platform where you cannot take the site with you, that is a lock-in trade-off you should understand and accept deliberately, not discover later.

How long does it take a web designer to build a small business website?

A straightforward small business website usually takes two to six weeks from kickoff to launch, depending on the number of pages, how quickly you provide content and feedback, and whether custom features are involved. The biggest cause of delays is usually not the designer - it is waiting on photos, copy, or approvals from the business owner. Ask for a timeline with milestones before the project starts.
