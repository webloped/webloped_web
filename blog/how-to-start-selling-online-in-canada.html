How to start selling online in Canada in 2026

You do not need a big budget, a warehouse, or a tech team to sell online in Canada. You need a product people want, a store they can trust, a way to take payment, and a plan for shipping. Here is each step, in order.

Learning how to start selling online in Canada is less about technology than most guides make it sound. The technology part is solved: a small business can open an online store in a weekend. The parts that actually decide whether you make money are choosing the right way to sell, pricing honestly, shipping reliably, and getting in front of the first customers.

This guide walks through those decisions one by one, with the Canadian specifics (taxes, shipping, payment options) that generic guides skip.

Step 1: Decide what you are selling, and to whom

Before you pick a platform, write down three things: what you sell, who buys it, and why they would buy it from you instead of from Amazon or a big-box store. If you cannot answer the third question, that is not a reason to stop, but it is a reason to pause. Small Canadian sellers win on things big retailers cannot copy easily: local knowledge, handmade or niche products, personal service, and a story customers connect with.

Also decide early whether you are selling physical products, digital products, or services. The choice changes everything downstream. Physical products need shipping and inventory. Digital products (courses, templates, downloads) need almost no logistics but need strong marketing, because anyone in the world can compete with you. Services booked online need a booking flow more than a shopping cart, which is a different setup entirely.

Step 2: Choose how to sell - marketplace, platform, or your own website

There are three realistic routes, and many Canadian businesses end up using two of them at once.

Marketplaces: fastest to first sale

Amazon.ca, Etsy, and Facebook Marketplace put you in front of buyers who are already shopping. Setup is quick, and you do not need to build traffic from zero. The trade-offs are real: you pay a fee or commission on every sale, you have little control over how your shop looks, and your customers belong to the marketplace, not to you. Marketplaces are a great place to test whether a product sells before you invest in your own store.

Hosted platforms: the most common choice

Shopify, which is itself a Canadian company, is the default choice for most Canadian small businesses selling physical products. You pay a monthly subscription plus a transaction fee, and in return you get a store, checkout, payment processing, and shipping tools in one place. WooCommerce (built on WordPress) is the main alternative: more control and no monthly platform fee, but you manage hosting, updates, and security yourself. Our guide to Shopify costs in Canada breaks down what each plan actually costs in CAD.

Your own website: full control

A custom-built store gives you complete control over design, checkout, and customer data, and no platform takes a cut beyond payment processing. It costs more up front and takes longer to launch, so it usually makes sense once you know the product sells, or when your brand experience is the product. Many businesses start on a hosted platform and move to a custom store later, once sales justify it.

A practical rule of thumb: if you are testing an idea, start on a marketplace. If you are building a brand, start on a hosted platform. If you already have steady sales and want full control, invest in your own store.

Step 3: Sort out the business basics

Selling online still means running a business, and a few boring admin steps save painful fixes later:

  • Register your business name with your province if you are operating under a name other than your own. In Ontario, for example, this is done through the provincial business registry.
  • Open a separate business bank account. Mixing personal and business money makes bookkeeping miserable and tax time worse.
  • Understand GST/HST. You generally must register for GST/HST once your taxable sales pass $30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters (the small supplier threshold). Below that you may still choose to register voluntarily, which lets you claim input tax credits on your business expenses. Tax rules vary by province and product type, so confirm your situation with an accountant before you launch rather than after your first busy month.
  • Get a domain name. A .ca domain signals a Canadian business to customers and is managed through CIRA. Pick something short, easy to spell over the phone, and free of hyphens. Our guide to choosing a domain name in Canada covers the details.
  • Write the legal pages. A privacy policy, terms of sale, shipping policy, and return policy are not optional extras. They protect you in disputes and they make customers more willing to buy. Our guide on privacy policies for Canadian websites explains what the law actually requires.

Step 4: Accept payments Canadians actually use

Your checkout should accept credit cards at minimum, and ideally the payment methods your customers prefer. In Canada that means Visa and Mastercard, plus PayPal for buyers who prefer not to share card details, and increasingly Interac for domestic buyers. Shopify, Stripe, Square, and Moneris all operate in Canada and support CAD payouts, so you are not forced into USD pricing or cross-border fees.

Price in Canadian dollars if your customers are Canadian. Showing USD prices to Canadian shoppers causes abandoned carts, because people do the conversion in their heads and assume the worst. Also decide your policy on payment fees now: absorbing card fees into your price is simpler and looks better than adding a surcharge at checkout. Our guide to accepting payments on your website in Canada compares the main options and their real CAD fees.

Step 5: Figure out shipping before your first order

Shipping is where new Canadian sellers lose money most often, because Canada is big and shipping across it is not cheap. Work this out before you launch:

  • Weigh and measure your products packed, not unpacked. The box, padding, and packaging count. A product that seems light can jump into a higher price band once boxed.
  • Get real quotes. Canada Post, Purolator, UPS, and FedEx all offer small business shipping programs with discounted rates. Compare them for your typical parcel size and your most common destinations, not just for one example.
  • Decide your shipping policy deliberately. Free shipping is not free, it is a cost you absorb into the product price. Flat-rate shipping is simpler for customers to understand. Charging exact shipping at checkout is the most honest but can cause sticker shock on a small order. Many small sellers start with flat-rate shipping within Canada and revisit once they have real order data.
  • Offer local pickup if you have a physical location. It costs you nothing, it brings foot traffic, and some customers prefer it.
  • Set expectations on delivery times. A customer in Vancouver ordering from Halifax will wait longer than one in Toronto. State realistic ranges on the product page, not just at checkout.

If you sell across the border, remember that US customers may face duties and brokerage fees on delivery. State clearly who pays them, or you will get angry emails about a surprise charge you did not impose.

Step 6: Build product pages that actually sell

Your product page does the job a salesperson used to do, so it has to answer every question a buyer would ask in a store. For each product, include:

  • Photos that show the product honestly. Multiple angles, a sense of scale (a hand, a familiar object, or a person for scale), and the product in use. Phone photos in good natural light beat studio shots that hide flaws.
  • A description written for the buyer, not for search engines. Lead with what the product does for them, then the specifications. Dimensions, materials, care instructions, and what is included in the box all belong here.
  • Price, shipping cost, and delivery estimate together. Do not make people reach checkout to discover shipping. Surprise costs are the top reason carts get abandoned.
  • Trust signals. A clear return policy, a contact method that a human answers, and genuine customer reviews. New stores can offer a short satisfaction guarantee instead of reviews they do not have yet.

One page per product, one clear call to action per page, and no clutter competing with the buy button. That is the whole formula.

Step 7: Get your first customers

Nobody finds a new store by accident. Your first customers almost always come from people who already know you, or from a channel you actively work:

  • Your existing audience. Email your past customers, post to your social accounts, and tell your in-store customers. A simple launch offer to people who already trust you beats any ad campaign.
  • Google Business Profile. If you have a physical location or serve a local area, a complete profile helps local buyers find you. It is free, and it is often the first thing a nearby customer sees.
  • Search. Write product descriptions and pages around the words customers actually search for, not industry jargon. This takes months to pay off but compounds, and it is the cheapest traffic you will ever get.
  • Social media. Pick one channel where your buyers actually spend time and post consistently there, rather than spreading thinly across five. Short videos showing the product in use outperform polished ads for most small sellers.
  • Paid ads, carefully. A small, tightly targeted ad budget can test whether a product sells, but do not scale ad spend until you know your profit per order. If you lose money on every sale before ads, ads only lose it faster.

A realistic first goal is ten orders from people you did not personally convince, because those ten prove the store works without you standing next to it.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Launching with too many products. Ten excellent listings beat fifty half-finished ones. Start small, learn what sells, then expand.
  • Underpricing to compete. If your price does not cover product, packaging, shipping, fees, and your time, you have a hobby, not a business. Price for profit and compete on something other than being cheapest.
  • Ignoring mobile. Most of your visitors will arrive on a phone. If checkout is painful on a phone, nothing else matters. Test every page on your own phone before launch.
  • No plan for returns. Decide your return policy before the first return arrives, and write it on the site. A clear, fair policy actually increases sales, because it removes the fear of being stuck with a bad purchase.
  • Waiting for perfect. A store that is good enough and live beats a perfect store that never launches. Launch, watch what real customers do, and improve from there.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to register a business to sell online in Canada?

Not always to make your first sale, but you should register a business name if you operate under a name other than your own, and you must register for GST/HST once your taxable sales pass the $30,000 small supplier threshold. A separate bank account and basic bookkeeping are worth setting up from the start regardless of your legal structure.

Should I sell on Amazon, Etsy, or my own website?

Marketplaces are fastest for testing a product, because buyers are already there. Your own website gives you control, better margins, and a direct relationship with customers. Most successful small sellers use both: marketplaces for discovery, and their own store for repeat customers and higher-margin sales.

How much does it cost to start selling online in Canada?

A marketplace listing can cost almost nothing to start beyond per-sale fees. A hosted store on a platform like Shopify adds a monthly subscription plus transaction fees, and a custom-built store costs more up front but has no platform fees beyond payment processing. The bigger costs are usually product, packaging, and shipping, not the website itself.

Do I charge GST/HST on online sales?

Once you are registered for GST/HST, you charge the tax rate of the province where the customer is located, which is why rates differ between, say, Ontario and Alberta. Below the registration threshold you are not required to charge it. Because tax rules depend on what you sell and where your customers are, confirm your setup with an accountant before you scale.
