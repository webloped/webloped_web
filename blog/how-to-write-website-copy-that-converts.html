How to write website copy that converts

Most small business websites do not fail because of bad design. They fail because the words are vague, self-centred, and hard to act on. Here is how to write website copy that converts visitors into enquiries, calls, and sales - no copywriting degree required.

The short answer

How to write website copy that converts? Write for the visitor, not for yourself. Say exactly what you do, who you do it for, and what they get out of it, using specific words and numbers instead of buzzwords. Then tell them the one next step you want them to take, and make it impossible to miss.

That is the whole game in one paragraph. Everything below is technique for putting it into practice: the five-second headline test, the customer-first rule, specificity, button text that actually gets clicked, and a page structure that walks a visitor from curiosity to contact. If you work through this guide and apply it to your homepage, services pages, and contact page, your site will sell harder than a redesign ever would on its own.

Why most small business website copy does not convert

Open a random sample of small business websites in any Canadian city and you will see the same four problems, over and over:

  • The headline says nothing. "Welcome to our website." "Your trusted partner in excellence." "Quality you can count on." A visitor reads it, learns nothing, and leaves. Headlines like these could be swapped between a plumbing company, a law firm, and a bakery with no difference.
  • The copy is about the business, not the customer. Paragraph after paragraph of "we," "our history," "our mission," "our team." The visitor is looking for a solution to their problem, and every sentence is about someone else.
  • Everything is vague. "Innovative solutions." "Cutting-edge services." "Best-in-class results." These phrases feel professional while saying nothing at all. If any competitor could put the same sentence on their site, it is not copy, it is decoration.
  • There is no clear next step. Pages that end with no call to action, or buttons that say "Submit" or "Learn more" without saying what happens when you click. The visitor has to guess what to do, so most do nothing.

These are all fixable with a keyboard. You do not need a new design, a new platform, or a copywriter on retainer. You need to rewrite the words with the visitor in mind. Let us go through the techniques one by one.

Step 1: Pass the 5-second headline test

Most visitors decide whether to stay on your homepage in about five seconds. That means your headline - the big text at the very top - has one job: tell a first-time visitor what you do, who you do it for, and why it matters, before they scroll.

A useful formula: [what you do] + [who you serve] + [the main outcome].

  • Vague: "Welcome to ABC Plumbing." Specific: "Same-day plumbing repairs in Ottawa - licensed and insured."
  • Vague: "We help businesses grow." Specific: "Bookkeeping for Ontario contractors - your books done by the 5th of every month."
  • Vague: "Professional cleaning services." Specific: "House cleaning in Cambridge, Ontario - book online in under two minutes."

Notice that the specific versions answer three questions at once: what (plumbing repairs), for whom (Ottawa homeowners, Ontario contractors), and what outcome (same day, done by the 5th, under two minutes). Add a subheadline under the headline that expands the promise with one concrete detail: a price starting point in CAD, a response time, a service area, a guarantee.

Then test it: show your homepage to someone who has never seen it for five seconds, take it away, and ask what your business does. If they cannot say it back clearly, your headline is not doing its job.

Step 2: Flip every "we" into a "you"

This is the single highest-impact habit in learning how to write website copy that converts. Go through your homepage and service pages and count how many sentences start with "we," "our," or "us" versus how many start with "you" or "your." Most small business sites are heavily weighted toward "we," and most of those sentences can be flipped.

  • We-centred: "We are a full-service landscaping company with 15 years of experience." You-centred: "You get a yard that looks professionally kept, season after season - backed by 15 years of local experience."
  • We-centred: "Our advanced scheduling system makes booking easy." You-centred: "Book your appointment online in under two minutes, with confirmation by text."
  • We-centred: "We use only premium materials." You-centred: "Your new deck is built with materials rated for Canadian winters, so it still looks new after five freeze-thaw seasons."

The facts stay the same. Only the perspective changes. The visitor does not care about your scheduling system; they care that booking takes two minutes. They do not care about your materials; they care that their deck survives February in Ontario. Write about the outcome, then attach the feature as the proof.

This rule applies hardest on the About page, which is where "we" copy usually concentrates. Visitors do read About pages before contacting a business, so rewrite the first paragraph to lead with the customer: what you do for them, why you are good at it, and what working with you feels like. The founding story and credentials can come lower on the page, where they support trust instead of blocking it.

Step 3: Replace adjectives with specifics

Vague words are where copy goes to die. "High quality," "great customer service," "affordable," "professional" - every business claims them, so no business is believed. Specifics are believable because they can be checked.

  • Instead of "fast service," say "most repairs completed within 48 hours."
  • Instead of "affordable pricing," give a starting price: "House cleaning from $120 per visit."
  • Instead of "trusted by many," say who: "Serving over 300 homes in the Waterloo region since 2015" - but only if that is actually true. Never invent numbers; a small true number beats a big invented one, and invented claims can get a Canadian business in real trouble under federal advertising law.
  • Instead of "expert team," describe what makes you expert: "Two licensed electricians, both ESA-certified, with a combined 22 years of residential work."

Run a find-and-replace on your draft for the words innovative, cutting-edge, world-class, revolutionary, best-in-class, solutions, leverage, and synergy. If one survives, it needs to earn its place by being followed by a fact. "Innovative" followed by "the first shop in Hamilton to offer same-day screen replacement" is fine. "Innovative solutions" on its own is not.

Step 4: Give every page one job, and one clear call to action

Every page on your site should exist to move the visitor one step closer to contacting you. If a page does not have a job, the visitor will not invent one for it.

The call to action (CTA) is the button or link that carries the job. Three rules make CTAs work:

  • One primary CTA per screen. A hero section with "Get a quote," "Call us," and "Read our story" buttons is three asks competing with each other. Pick the one action that matters most and repeat it. Secondary options can live in the navigation.
  • Say what happens when they click. "Get my free quote" beats "Submit." "Book my appointment" beats "Contact us." "See pricing" beats "Learn more." The button text should describe the outcome, so clicking feels safe and specific.
  • Reduce the friction around it. Next to the CTA, remove a worry in one short line: "No obligation." "Replies within one business day." "Free estimates." If your form asks for information, ask for as little as possible - every extra field costs you conversions.

Then close the page the same way it opened. Repeat the primary CTA at the bottom of the page, reworded slightly so it does not feel like a copy-paste. The visitor who scrolled to the end is your most interested reader; do not make them scroll back up to find the contact button.

Step 5: Structure the homepage like a sales conversation

A converting homepage is not a brochure. It is a conversation in the order a real salesperson would use it. A section structure that works for most service businesses:

  1. Hero: headline, subheadline, one CTA. Who you serve, the outcome, the next step. Covered in steps 1 and 4.
  2. The problem, in the visitor's words. One short section that names the frustration they arrived with: "Tired of contractors who never call back?" This proves you understand them, and it is what earns the next scroll.
  3. How you solve it. Three to four services, each with a one-sentence outcome description and a link to a dedicated page. Not feature lists - results.
  4. Proof. Real testimonials with names and specifics, a before-and-after, a count of completed projects, years in business. If you are just starting out, proof can be process: photos of real work, a clear description of how you work, and any certifications or insurance. Honest and thin beats impressive and invented.
  5. How it works. Three simple steps: "1. Request a quote. 2. We visit or video-call. 3. The work gets done." Making the process look simple is one of the strongest conversion moves there is, because complexity is what keeps people from starting.
  6. Closing CTA. Repeat the primary action with a friction-remover. This is the moment to link to your contact page, which should restate the promise and keep the form short.

Visitors scan before they read, so format for scanning: short paragraphs, descriptive subheadings, bolded key phrases, and lists like this one. Dense walls of text get skipped, not studied.

Step 6: Write service pages that sell outcomes

Your service pages are where serious buyers land, often straight from Google. Our guide on getting your business on Google covers the search side; this is the content side. Each service page should follow the same pattern:

  • Headline naming the service, the area, and the outcome: "Furnace repair in Cambridge - most fixes done same day."
  • Two or three sentences on the result the customer gets, in their language.
  • A short list of what is included, with specifics: "90-minute service window, upfront pricing in CAD, 2-year parts and labour warranty."
  • One or two customer questions answered directly on the page (pricing ranges, timing, what to prepare).
  • Proof: one testimonial about this exact service, with a name and neighbourhood if the customer permits.
  • The CTA again, matched to the service: "Book furnace repair," not generic "Contact us."

One page per service also helps search engines match you to specific queries - "furnace repair Cambridge" is a far easier phrase to rank for than "HVAC services." Keep each page focused on one service so the headline, copy, and CTA all point the same way.

The 20-minute copy audit you can run today

You do not have to rewrite the whole site at once. Open your homepage and run this audit:

  1. Read only the headline and subheadline. Could a stranger tell what you do, who it is for, and the main outcome? If not, rewrite the headline with the formula from step 1. This alone is the biggest win available.
  2. Count "we" versus "you." Highlight every sentence starting with "we" or "our" in the first two screens. Rewrite the top five to start with "you" or "your" and lead with the customer outcome.
  3. Check every CTA button. If any say "Submit," "Contact us," or "Learn more" with no context, rewrite them with the outcome: "Get my free quote," "Book my appointment," "See starting prices."
  4. Hunt one page of buzzwords. Pick your most-visited service page and delete or replace every vague adjective with a fact, number, or specific promise.

Those four changes take an afternoon and require no developer, no designer, and no budget. Do them before you spend a dollar on ads or a redesign - sending traffic to a site with a vague headline is the most expensive mistake in small business marketing. Frequently asked questions

How long should my homepage copy be?

Long enough to answer the visitor's questions, short enough that they do not leave first. For most small business homepages, 400-800 words of well-structured copy works: headline, subheadline, problem, services, proof, how-it-works, and closing CTA. The mistake is not length but density - long pages of scannable sections convert better than short pages of dense paragraphs, because visitors skip what they do not need.

Should I write the copy myself or hire a copywriter?

Write the first draft yourself. You know your customers' real words - the phrases they use on the phone, the questions they repeat, the worries they voice. A copywriter sharpens; only you can supply the raw material. If you hire help, give them your customer notes and the framework above, and judge their draft by the same test: does the headline pass the five-second rule, is it about "you," and are the claims specific? For more on hiring well, see what to ask a web designer before hiring.

Can AI write my website copy for me?

AI is a decent first-draft assistant and a poor final copywriter. It writes fluent, generic copy - the exact "innovative solutions" style this guide warns against - because it predicts average words, not your customers' words. Use it to brainstorm headline variations or tighten a paragraph, then replace every vague claim with a specific fact about your business. And never let it invent numbers, reviews, or credentials about you: fabricated claims on a business site can violate Canadian advertising rules and destroy trust when a customer checks.

How do I know if my new copy is actually working?

Track one number before and after: the conversion rate of the page. For most small business sites that means contact form submissions, phone calls, or quote requests divided by visitors. Set up a free Google Analytics or Search Console account, record the baseline for a month, make your copy changes, then compare the next month. A headline rewrite that lifts enquiries from 2% to 3% of visitors is a 50% increase in leads from the same traffic - and you will never see that if you do not measure it.
