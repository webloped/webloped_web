How much does a website cost in Canada in 2026?

Website quotes in Canada range from a few hundred dollars to tens of thousands. Here is what actually drives the price, and how to get a number you can trust.

Ask three developers what a website costs and you will get three wildly different answers: $500, $15,000, and a monthly subscription pitch. Nobody is necessarily wrong. They are quoting different products built by different people with different levels of craft.

This guide breaks down what a small business website costs in Canada in 2026, in Canadian dollars - what drives the price and how to get a number you can trust.

Why quotes vary so wildly

A "website" is not one product. It can mean a five-page brochure site on a pre-built template, or a fully custom store with online checkout, booking, and automation behind it.

The price mostly reflects two things: how much of the site is custom-built for you, and who is building it. A solo freelancer has lower overhead than a full agency with designers and project managers on payroll, so they can charge less for similar-looking work. That does not make one better than the other - it makes them suited to different jobs.

Be suspicious of any quote given without questions. An accurate quote needs to know what you sell, who the site is for, how many pages you need, and whether money changes hands on the site.

The three common tiers

Almost every quote you will see in Canada falls into one of these three tiers. The ranges below are typical market ranges you will commonly see - not our prices - meant to orient you, not to pin anyone down.

Tier 1: DIY website builders

Platforms like Squarespace, Wix, or Shopify let you build a site yourself from templates, typically $20 to $60 per month depending on the plan and whether you need e-commerce. Domain registration is usually extra, and the real cost is your own time.

This route makes sense when your budget is near zero and your needs are simple (a few pages, a contact form). The trade-offs: your site looks like thousands of others, advanced SEO and speed optimization are limited, and you rent rather than own - stop paying and the site disappears.

Tier 2: Freelancer or small studio custom build

Hiring an independent developer or a small shop is where most Canadian small businesses land. For a typical small business site - five to ten pages, a contact or quote form, basic on-page SEO - freelance custom builds commonly start in the low four figures and go up from there, depending on design quality and functionality.

You get a site designed around your business instead of a template, and you usually own everything outright. At this tier, references and a written scope matter more than price.

Tier 3: Full agency build

Agencies bring a team: strategy, design, copywriting, development, and often ongoing marketing. For small businesses, agency projects typically start in the five figures and climb with complexity. E-commerce, membership areas, custom integrations, and multilingual builds push the number higher.

You pay for process and polish: professional copywriting, accessibility standards, and a project manager keeping everything on track. If your website is your main sales channel and the budget exists, an agency is often worth it. If it is a credibility site that supports word-of-mouth referrals, it is usually more than you need.

What actually drives the cost

Within any tier, these are the levers that move a quote up or down:

  • Number of pages. Every page needs design, copy, and testing. Ten pages cost noticeably more than five.
  • E-commerce or online checkout. Payments, taxes, shipping, and product management add real complexity - and real cost.
  • Integrations and automations. Booking systems, CRM connections, and email automation each take setup and testing time.
  • Copywriting. Hand over polished text and the build moves faster. If the developer has to write or heavily edit your copy, expect it in the quote.
  • Photography and imagery. Stock photos are cheap; a proper shoot of your team and workspace costs more, but does more for trust than anything else on the page.
  • SEO setup. Basic on-page SEO (titles, meta descriptions, site structure) belongs in a good build. Ongoing SEO as a service is a separate, recurring cost.
  • Timeline pressure. "I need it live in two weeks" costs more than "I need it live next quarter."

The ongoing costs people forget

The build price is not the total price. Every website has running costs:

  • Domain name: roughly $15 to $25 per year in CAD for a .ca or .com. Register it yourself so you own it.
  • Hosting: anywhere from a few dollars a month for shared hosting to $30 or more for managed hosting with backups and security.
  • Maintenance and updates: software updates, security patches, content edits, and backups - handled yourself or through a monthly plan.
  • Premium plugins or services: booking tools, email marketing, and some SEO tools carry their own subscriptions.

Ask every developer this before you sign: "What will this site cost me per year after launch, not counting the build?" If they cannot answer clearly, that is a red flag.

How to get an accurate quote

You will get far better quotes if you prepare a short brief first - a page of notes covering these points:

  1. Your goal. What should the site make happen - phone calls, quote requests, online sales, bookings?
  2. Your pages. List them: Home, Services, About, Contact, plus any extras like a blog or gallery.
  3. Examples you like. Three websites you admire, with one sentence each on what you like. This one step prevents most miscommunication.
  4. Your content status. Do you have logo, photos, and text ready, or do you need help creating them?

Then ask for a fixed quote, not an hourly estimate. Hourly billing transfers all the risk to you: if the project runs long, you pay more. A fixed quote forces proper scoping up front, and it lets you compare quotes apples to apples. Scope changes get re-quoted separately instead of silently inflating the bill.

Finally, confirm what "done" includes: launch, basic SEO setup, analytics, training on how to edit content, and a warranty period for bugs. Get it in writing.
