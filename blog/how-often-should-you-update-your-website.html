How often should you update your website?

Small business owners launch their website and then never touch it again. Here is how often you should really update your site: a simple monthly, quarterly, and annual schedule that keeps it accurate, trustworthy, and ranking on Google - without turning it into a second job.

The short answer

How often should you update your website? For most Canadian small businesses, the honest answer is: a small meaningful update every month, a deeper check-in every quarter, and a full review once a year. Technical maintenance (backups, security updates, broken-link checks) should run weekly or at least monthly, even if you never touch a word of copy. That is a far cry from what most business owners actually do, which is launch the site, admire it for a week, and not touch it again until something breaks.

The good news is that "updating your website" rarely means what owners fear it means. You do not need a redesign every year, and you do not need to blog every week. Most of the updates that matter are small, quick, and boring: fixing your hours after a holiday, adding a testimonial a customer just sent you, swapping out a 2022 photo of your team. Those quiet touches are what keep a site trustworthy for visitors and healthy for Google. This guide breaks down exactly what to update, how often, and why each one matters.

Why Google notices when you stop updating

Search engines want to recommend websites that are current, relevant, and useful. When a site sits untouched for a year or more, Google has good reason to suspect the information is stale, even if your services have not changed. That does not mean Google punishes every unchanged page - a well-written service page can rank for years - but for queries where freshness matters, regularly updated competitor content will eventually overtake a page frozen in time.

There is also a more mechanical effect. Pages that update regularly tend to get crawled more often, which means your changes get indexed faster. When you publish a new service page or fix a major error, Google picks it up quickly if the site shows signs of life. On an abandoned site, that same change can sit unnoticed for weeks. And beyond Google entirely, freshness affects humans: a visitor who sees "Copyright 2021" in the footer, last year's prices, and photos of a location you moved out of two years ago will not trust you with their money.

The realistic update schedule

Here is a cadence that works for real businesses with limited time. It is roughly 2 to 3 hours a month, not a second job:

  • Monthly: one meaningful content update. Publish a short post answering a customer question, add a recent review or testimonial, refresh a project gallery with new photos, or update a service page with new details. One genuine improvement beats four thin blog posts written just to fill a calendar.
  • Monthly: a 15-minute health check. Click through your contact forms to confirm messages still arrive. Check for broken links and pages that load wrong. Confirm your hours, phone number, and prices are still accurate. Small decay here quietly costs you leads.
  • Quarterly: a messaging review. Step back and read your homepage and top service pages like a stranger. Does the copy still describe what you actually do? Has your pricing, service area, or team changed? Businesses evolve; the site should keep up. This is also a good moment to check your analytics for pages with declining traffic, which often signal outdated content.
  • Annually: a full review. Once a year, look at everything: design, speed, mobile experience, security, SEO basics. Technology and design expectations shift; a yearly review catches the drift before it becomes a rebuild. If the site is more than 3 to 4 years old and the review turns up a long list, you may be due for a refresh rather than more patches.
  • Immediately: business changes. New phone number, new address, new hours, discontinued service, new service, price change - these update the same day they happen. Outdated business facts do direct damage: they send customers to the wrong place and destroy trust.

What counts as an update?

Owners often imagine updates as big projects, but most of what Google and visitors value are small, honest changes:

  • Adding a testimonial, review, or project photo a customer just shared
  • Refreshing a blog post or service page with current information, prices, or examples
  • Rewriting a headline or call-to-action that is not converting
  • Publishing a short article that answers a real customer question
  • Fixing broken links, typos, and outdated contact details
  • Updating images, team bios, and service areas
  • Refreshing seasonal content (holiday hours, seasonal services, local references)

None of these requires a developer. If your site was built well, you should be able to handle all of them yourself in a simple content editor - which is exactly why choosing the right platform matters. The updates you never see (but must happen anyway)

Everything above is content you control. Underneath it sits the technical layer, and this is where neglect gets expensive. Software updates, security patches, backups, uptime monitoring, and SSL certificates need to happen on a weekly or monthly rhythm whether or not anything visible changes. A hacked site, a lapsed certificate, or a failed backup restore will cost you far more in emergency fixes and lost business than routine maintenance ever would.

This is the layer most owners sensibly hand off. A maintenance plan that keeps software patched, backups tested, and small issues fixed quietly in the background typically runs a few hundred dollars a year for a small Canadian site. What happens if you do nothing?

Nothing dramatic happens on day one. That is what makes neglect so common. But the costs compound quietly:

  • Lost trust. Outdated prices, old photos, and stale copyright dates tell visitors the business might not be paying attention - or might not still be operating.
  • Lost rankings. Competitors who keep their content current gradually outrank pages frozen in time, especially for local and seasonal searches.
  • Lost leads. Broken forms, wrong phone numbers, and confusing outdated copy silently send enquiries to competitors.
  • Emergency bills. Deferred security updates turn into hacked sites and malware cleanups that cost many times what routine maintenance would have.

If your site has been untouched for years and the list of problems is long, it may be more honest to ask whether you need updates or a redesign. Make it a system, not a chore

The owners who succeed at this do not have more discipline; they have a simpler system. Put a 30-minute recurring block on your calendar on the first Monday of each month. Keep a running note of update ideas as they come: customer questions worth answering, photos worth posting, details that changed. When the block comes, you are never staring at a blank page. And if even that sounds like too much, that is exactly what a maintenance plan is for: your site stays current, accurate, and secure while you run the business.

FAQ

How often should I update my website for SEO?

Aim for at least one meaningful content update per month. Google crawls active sites more frequently, and fresh content competes better for queries where freshness matters. Just as important: review service pages every 4 to 6 months and the homepage every 3 to 4 months. Refreshing an existing page that already ranks is often more valuable than publishing something brand new. For the full picture of ongoing SEO work, see how much SEO costs in Canada.

Does updating my website content improve my Google rankings?

Usually, yes, but not by magic. Updates help because they fix the things that actually hurt rankings: outdated information, declining relevance, and pages competitors have surpassed. An update that makes a page genuinely more useful for the searcher can move it up; cosmetic edits for their own sake do very little. Track your Search Console impressions on pages you refresh - declining impressions on a page untouched for over a year is a strong signal it needs attention.

What is the difference between updating my website and redesigning it?

Updates change content, features, or performance while the overall structure stays the same. A redesign overhauls the layout, branding, and user experience from the ground up. Most sites need updates constantly and a redesign every 3 to 5 years. If a yearly review keeps surfacing the same structural complaints - slow everywhere, painful to edit, looks dated on phones - you are past updates and into redesign territory.

Can I update my website myself, or do I need a developer?

Content updates - text, images, testimonials, prices - are firmly DIY on any modern site, and a good developer will train you to do them before handoff. Technical updates - software patches, security hardening, performance tuning, backups - are worth delegating if you are not comfortable with them, because getting them wrong has real consequences. The middle ground works well: you handle content, a maintenance plan handles the machinery.
