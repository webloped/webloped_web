How to add Google reviews to your website

Your best reviews are sitting on your Google profile while your website says nothing about them. Here are five ways to put Google reviews on your website, from a free map embed to a widget that updates itself, plus where to place them and the rules to follow.

The short answer: the easiest free option is Google's own map embed, which shows your star rating, review count, and location in a few minutes. The most effective option for most small businesses is a review widget that pulls your reviews onto your site automatically and keeps them fresh. A simple review link button is the fastest way to grow your review count, and a manual testimonials section costs nothing if you are willing to update it by hand. The right choice depends on your platform, your budget, and how often your reviews change.

Before any of these methods work, two things need to be true: your Google Business Profile must be claimed and verified, and you need at least one public review. If you have not claimed your listing yet, start with our guide on how to get your business on Google. If you have a profile but almost no reviews, work through how to get more Google reviews first - five or ten recent reviews make the display worth building, while a single review from two years ago does the opposite of what you want.

Why put Google reviews on your website at all

Visitors decide whether to trust you in seconds, and your own claims about your work carry far less weight than what real customers say. A review on your website answers the question a visitor is already asking: what is it actually like to hire this business? A dentist, a plumber, a landscaper, or a bookkeeper all sell the same thing in the end - confidence - and a row of recent, specific reviews builds it faster than any paragraph you could write about yourself.

Reviews also help at the exact moment a visitor is deciding. A quote request form next to three recent five-star reviews converts better than the same form on a bare page, because the visitor does not have to leave your site, open Google, and come back to decide. That is the whole point of displaying reviews: keep the proof next to the decision.

One honest note: putting reviews on your own site does not make star ratings appear next to your pages in Google search results. Google does not show review stars for reviews about your own business that you publish on your own site. The value is on the page itself, in the eyes of the visitor, not in search. That is still worth a lot.

Method 1: the free Google Maps embed

This is the fastest option, it costs nothing, and it uses only Google's own tools. The trade-off is that it shows your business on a map with your star rating and review count, not the full text of your reviews. Visitors click through to Google to read them.

How to do it:

  1. Open Google Maps in your browser and search for your business name.
  2. Click Share, then choose the Embed a map tab.
  3. Pick a size, copy the iframe code Google gives you, and paste it into your website where you want the map to appear.

Where to paste it. On WordPress, add a Custom HTML block. On Wix or Squarespace, use an Embed or Code block. On Shopify, add a Custom HTML section. On a plain HTML site, paste the iframe directly into the page. Put it on your contact page, where visitors are already looking for your location, and on your homepage if your address matters to customers.

Good for: a free, zero-maintenance display that never goes stale, because the rating and review count come live from Google. Not good for: showing what customers actually wrote, which is the part that persuades people.

Method 2: a review link button

This method does not display your reviews at all - it sends visitors to leave one. It belongs in this guide because it is the simplest thing you can add today, and it makes every other method better by growing the review pool they draw from.

Every Google Business Profile has a review link that opens the review form for your business. To find it, sign in to the Google account that manages your profile, search for your business on Google, and select the option to ask for reviews (in Google Maps, open Customer reviews and select Get more reviews). Copy the link.

Add it to your site as a plain button or text link: "Review us on Google" in your footer, on your thank-you page after a booking or purchase, and at the bottom of your contact page. A short link on your own domain, such as yoursite.ca/review, is easier to say out loud and looks trustworthy on printed materials - you can set one up as a simple redirect to the Google link.

One rule to remember: Google prohibits offering incentives, like discounts or gift cards, in exchange for reviews, and prohibits asking only the customers you expect to be happy. The button is fine. A bribe attached to it is not.

Method 3: a review widget that updates itself

A widget is a small embed from a third-party tool that pulls your reviews from your Google Business Profile and displays them on your site in a layout you choose - a slider, a grid, or a badge with your star rating. New reviews appear automatically, so the display never goes stale.

How it works:

  1. Create an account with a widget tool and choose the Google Reviews widget.
  2. Connect your business by entering your business name, Google Maps link, or Google Place ID. If the search finds the wrong listing, the Place ID is the reliable way to point at the right one.
  3. Pick a layout, match the colours and fonts to your site, and set any filters - for example, showing only reviews with four stars or more.
  4. Copy the embed code and paste it into an HTML or Embed block on your site, then publish.

Tools worth looking at. Elfsight has a free tier and paid plans starting around $5 USD a month, and works across most platforms. Trustindex is a strong fit for WordPress sites. EmbedSocial and Tagembed work well if you want to pull reviews from more than one place, such as Google and Facebook together. Prices change, so check the current plan before you commit - and check what the free tier actually limits, since some tools cap the number of reviews shown or add their own branding.

A note on filtering. Most widgets let you hide low-rated reviews. Showing only five-star reviews looks tidy, but a mix reads as more believable - visitors are used to seeing a four-star review among the fives, and a wall of perfection can look curated. Keep the link to your full Google profile visible, so anyone who wants the unfiltered picture can get it in one click.

Good for: most small businesses that want reviews on their site without touching code or updating anything by hand. Watch out for: the widget is a third-party script, so it adds a small amount of load to your page. Test your page speed after adding it, and if the widget slows things down noticeably, check our guide on how to make your website load faster.

Method 4: a manual testimonials section

If you want a free option with full control over the design, add the reviews by hand. Pick your best recent reviews, quote them exactly as written, and show the reviewer's first name and the date. A simple grid of three to six quotes, with star icons and a link to your full Google profile, is all it takes.

The rules for doing this honestly:

  • Quote the review word for word. Do not tidy up the grammar, cut out the awkward parts, or merge two reviews into one.
  • Show the date of each review. A quote with no date could be from last week or five years ago, and visitors know it.
  • Link to your Google profile so visitors can verify the reviews are real.
  • Update the section every month or two. Reviews older than three months start to look stale, and a testimonials section that never changes quietly tells visitors the business has gone quiet too.

Good for: a free setup with complete control over how the section looks. Not good for: anyone who will not maintain it. A manual section that goes stale is worse than no section at all.

Method 5: the Google Places API (for developers)

If you have a developer, or your site is custom-built, Google's Places API lets you pull review data and build any display you want. The catch is that the API returns a maximum of five reviews per place, and Google chooses them by relevance rather than by date, so you may not get your newest reviews. Building and maintaining the integration costs developer time, and the API itself is a paid Google Cloud service with a free usage tier. For most small businesses, a widget (method 3) gives the same result with far less work. Use the API only if you need a display that no widget can produce.

Where to place reviews on your website

Placement matters as much as the method. Reviews belong where visitors make decisions, not on a page nobody visits.

  • Homepage, below the hero. A short row of three reviews, or a badge with your star rating and review count, right under your main headline. It sets the tone for everything below it.
  • Service pages. Reviews that mention the specific service on that page are the most persuasive kind. A roofing page with a review about a roof replacement beats a generic testimonial every time.
  • Next to contact and quote forms. This is where doubt is highest and the decision is made. A couple of reviews beside the form is worth more than a whole testimonials page.
  • Pricing pages. If you publish prices, reviews help visitors feel the price is justified. See our guide on whether to put prices on your website.

Do not hide your reviews on a dedicated testimonials page and nowhere else - few visitors ever click through to it. Our guide to the pages a small business website should have suggests giving testimonials their own page, but the reviews themselves should also appear where the decisions happen.

What to do about negative reviews on your site

Sooner or later a negative review will appear, and the instinct is to hide it. Resist that instinct on your website, too. A business with nothing but five-star reviews looks filtered, and visitors trust a 4.8 with a few honest four-star reviews more than a perfect 5.0.

If a widget lets you filter, set the floor at four stars rather than five, and keep your full Google profile one click away so nothing looks hidden. More importantly, reply to the negative review on Google itself - a calm, professional reply that shows how you handle problems converts more future customers than the review costs you. Our guide on how to respond to a negative Google review walks through exactly what to say. You cannot remove a bad review just because you disagree with it; Google only removes reviews that violate its policies, such as spam or fake content.

Frequently asked questions

Is it free to add Google reviews to my website?

Yes, if you use Google's own tools. The Google Maps embed and a plain review link button cost nothing. A manual testimonials section is also free, apart from your time to keep it updated. Review widgets usually have a free tier, with paid plans starting around $5 USD a month for more layouts, more reviews, or removal of the tool's branding. A custom build with the Places API costs developer time.

Do I need a Google Business Profile to show Google reviews on my site?

Yes. Every method in this guide pulls from your Google Business Profile, so it needs to be claimed, verified, and have at least one public review. If you have not set one up yet, start with our guide on how to get your business on Google.

Can I choose which Google reviews show on my website?

With a widget or a manual section, yes - you can filter by star rating or hand-pick the reviews you display. With the free Google Maps embed, no - visitors see your overall rating and review count, and read individual reviews on Google. Whatever you display, quote reviews exactly as written, show their dates, and link to your full profile so visitors can see the complete picture.

Will adding Google reviews to my website improve my Google ranking?

Not directly. Reviews on your own site do not make star ratings appear in search results, and they are not a ranking factor for your website by themselves. What they do is convert more of the visitors you already get, because the proof sits next to the decision. The reviews that help your local ranking are the ones on your Google Business Profile itself - which is why asking for more reviews, covered in our guide on how to get more Google reviews, matters just as much as displaying them.
