How to make your website mobile friendly: a guide for Canadian small businesses

Most of your customers will meet your business on a phone, often standing in a parking lot, on a job site, or on the bus. If they have to pinch, zoom, and wait to find your number, they will call the next business instead. This guide shows how to make your website mobile friendly in practical steps, without a full rebuild in most cases.

The short answer

How to make your website mobile friendly comes down to four things a visitor can feel in the first few seconds. The page fits the screen without sideways scrolling. The text is readable without zooming. Buttons and links are big enough to tap with a thumb. And the page loads fast enough that someone on a phone connection does not give up.

Under the hood, that means one responsive website that adapts to any screen width, body text at 16 pixels or larger, tap targets around 44 by 44 pixels with space between them, compressed images, short forms, and your phone number, hours, and main next step visible right away. Get those right and you have covered most of what mobile friendly means for a small business site. The rest of this guide walks through each fix, in order of impact, with Canadian examples for trades, clinics, shops, and service businesses.

What mobile friendly actually means in 2026

A mobile friendly website is not a shrunken desktop site. It is a site designed so a person using one thumb, on a mid-range phone, on an ordinary connection, can find what they need and act on it in seconds. Well over half of web visits now come from phones, and Google crawls and indexes the mobile version of your site first. Since July 2024, if your site is not accessible on mobile, it may not be indexed at all. That makes mobile friendliness a search issue as well as a customer experience issue.

For a Canadian small business, the stakes are concrete. A homeowner with a leaking pipe searches on a phone and calls the first plumber whose number is easy to tap. A patient books a clinic appointment from a phone on the GO train. A customer checks your hours and directions from your Google listing, taps through to your site, and decides in seconds whether you look open, nearby, and trustworthy. A site that fails on phones does not just look dated. It loses the call, the booking, and the sale to a competitor whose site works.

The good news is that most mobile problems are fixable without starting over. If your site uses a modern theme or builder, you are probably closer than you think. The fixes below are ordered so you can do the highest-impact work first, test, and then decide whether you need a bigger rebuild.

Step 1: Start with responsive design, not a separate mobile site

Responsive design means one website whose layout reflows to fit whatever screen it lands on. Columns stack on a phone, images shrink, menus collapse into a button. You maintain one set of pages, one set of content, and one set of search rankings. A separate mobile site, often on an m. subdomain, doubles your maintenance and splits your SEO. It was common a decade ago. It is rarely the right choice now.

How to check what you have: open your site on your phone and rotate it sideways. A responsive site adjusts smoothly. A fixed-width site will show tiny text, sideways scrolling, or a layout that only makes sense on a desktop monitor. Also check the code for the viewport setting that tells phones to render at device width: <meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1.0">. Most modern themes and builders include it by default. If yours does not, that one line is the first fix.

If you are on WordPress, a modern responsive theme solves most layout problems in one move. On Squarespace, Wix, or Shopify, responsive layouts are built in, but you still need to check how your specific pages, images, and forms behave on a phone, because a responsive template can still hold unresponsive content, such as a wide table or a huge image. If your site was built years ago on a fixed layout and cannot be made responsive with reasonable effort, that is usually a sign to plan a redesign rather than patch it. Step 2: Make text readable without zooming

If a visitor has to pinch to read your hours, you have lost them. On mobile, keep body text at a minimum of 16 pixels. Smaller text looks fine on a desktop monitor and becomes a chore on a phone. Headings should be clearly larger than body text so the page structure is obvious at a glance. Keep paragraphs short, usually two to four sentences, and use lists for anything a visitor needs to scan, such as services, prices, or steps.

Line length matters too. On a phone, a full-width paragraph with no margins is hard to follow. A little padding on the sides and generous line spacing make text feel calm and readable. Contrast matters just as much: light grey text on a white background may look elegant on a large screen and disappear in sunlight on a phone. Aim for strong contrast between text and background, which also helps you meet accessibility standards. Our guide on AODA compliance for Ontario websites covers contrast and readability in more detail.

A practical test: hold your phone at arm's length in bright light and try to read your services page. If you squint, your customers will too. Fix the font size and contrast before you worry about anything fancier.

Step 3: Design for the thumb, not the mouse

On a desktop, a visitor clicks with a precise cursor. On a phone, they tap with a thumb, often one-handed. Buttons and links should be at least around 44 by 44 pixels, roughly the pad of a thumb, with generous space between them so a tap cannot hit the wrong target. This applies to your call button, your booking button, your menu items, and any link inside your text. Two links stacked tightly together in a paragraph are a classic mobile trap.

Put the important actions in the thumb zone. People hold phones and reach with their thumb, which comfortably covers the lower and middle part of the screen. A sticky call or booking button at the bottom of the screen works well for trades and clinics, as long as it does not cover content. Keep one primary call to action per page. On a small screen, competing buttons slow people down. Choose the action that matters most, such as Call now, Book online, or Get a quote, make it large and descriptive, and repeat it at natural points down the page.

Simplify your navigation menu for mobile. A long desktop menu with dropdowns becomes a maze on a phone. Aim for five to seven top-level links with clear labels, grouped under a hamburger menu, and move the rest into the footer or into links within the content. Test the menu with one thumb, without looking closely. If you cannot open it, find Services, and tap your phone number in under ten seconds, simplify it.

Step 4: Make it fast on a phone connection

Speed is a mobile friendliness issue, not a separate technical hobby. A page that loads in two seconds on your office wifi can take ten seconds on a phone in a basement, on a job site, or on a train with a weak signal. Most visitors will not wait. Google also uses mobile speed and stability as part of how it judges page experience, so a slow site ranks lower and converts worse.

The biggest wins are usually images. Resize images to the size they are actually displayed at, compress them, and use modern formats where your platform supports them. A photo straight from a phone camera can be several megabytes. On your website it rarely needs to be more than a few hundred kilobytes. Remove plugins, pop-ups, and scripts you no longer use. Each one adds weight that a phone has to download. Keep redirects to a minimum, enable caching where your host offers it, and choose hosting that is fast for Canadian visitors.

Test with Google PageSpeed Insights, which reports mobile performance and specific fixes in plain language. Aim for useful content to appear within a couple of seconds. If speed is your main problem, our full guide on how to make your website load faster walks through the fixes in order, and our guide on why your website is slow helps you diagnose the cause.

Step 5: Put contact details where a phone visitor expects them

Mobile visitors are often closer to acting than desktop visitors. They are searching near your business, checking hours, or ready to call. Make the next step obvious. Your phone number should be a tap-to-call link, visible in the header or near the top of the page, not buried in a footer image. Your address, service area, and hours should match your Google Business Profile exactly, so a customer who finds you on Maps sees the same details on your site. If you take bookings, the booking button should be reachable without scrolling through your whole homepage.

Keep forms short and thumb-friendly. Every extra field costs you enquiries on a phone. Name, contact detail, and one question about the job is usually enough for a first enquiry. Use the right keyboard for each field: email fields should bring up the email keyboard, phone fields the number pad, date fields a date picker. Label every field clearly, show errors in plain language next to the field that needs fixing, and confirm success with a thank-you message the visitor can see without scrolling. If you need a booking flow, our guide on adding online booking to your website covers the setup.

Watch pop-ups carefully on mobile. A pop-up that covers the whole screen and is hard to close will send phone visitors away and can hurt your search performance. If you use pop-ups for offers or email sign-ups, delay them, keep them small, make the close button large, and never show them before a visitor has seen your main content. Our guide on collecting email addresses on your website covers pop-ups that do not annoy visitors.

Step 6: Fix the content that breaks on small screens

Some content types break on phones even inside a responsive template. Wide tables force sideways scrolling. Fix them by stacking the information, using a simple list, or allowing the table to scroll within its own box with a clear hint. Long unbroken words, such as email addresses or URLs in text, can overflow the screen. Break them with links instead. Embedded maps, videos, and social feeds need to shrink with the screen. Test each one on a phone, because an embed that looks fine on desktop can push your whole page sideways on mobile.

Images need the same attention. Every image should scale down to fit the screen width, never force horizontal scrolling, and include alt text that describes it for visitors who cannot see it. Avoid text baked into images, such as a menu or price list uploaded as a photo. On a phone that text becomes tiny and unreadable, and search engines cannot read it either. Type the information as real text and use images to support it, not replace it. If you run a restaurant, salon, or clinic, this one change often makes the biggest difference to mobile visitors.

Check your footer too. Footer links that are small and tightly packed on desktop become a thumb trap on a phone. Give footer links enough size and spacing to tap comfortably, and keep the most important ones, such as Contact, Pricing, and Book, easy to find.

Step 7: Test on real phones, not just your computer

Desktop previews and browser tools are useful, but they miss real-world problems: a button hidden behind a sticky header, a form that jumps when the keyboard opens, text that disappears in sunlight, a menu that works with a mouse but not a thumb. Test on at least one iPhone and one Android phone if you can. Ask a friend, a family member, or a customer. Give them three tasks: find your phone number, find your hours, and send an enquiry or make a booking. Watch without helping. Where they hesitate is where you need to fix.

Then use the free tools. Google PageSpeed Insights reports mobile speed and usability for any page. Google Search Console includes a Mobile Usability report that lists pages with tap target, viewport, or content-width problems. Run your homepage, your main service page, and your contact page through both. Fix the issues they name, then test again on a real phone. Make this a habit after every significant change to your site, not a one-time project. Our guide on setting up Google Search Console shows you how to get the report.

Also test the unglamorous paths: tap your phone number and confirm it dials the right number. Submit your form and confirm the message reaches your inbox, with the visitor details intact. Tap your address and confirm it opens maps with the right location. A mobile friendly site that loses enquiries is not friendly at all.

Mobile friendliness and SEO: what Google actually rewards

Google's mobile-first indexing means the mobile version of your site is the version Google uses for ranking. If your mobile pages hide content that your desktop pages show, Google may not count that content. If your mobile pages are slow or unstable, that counts against you. The practical takeaway is simple: your mobile site should contain the same useful content as your desktop site, presented clearly, loading quickly, with no content hidden behind taps that a crawler cannot reach.

Beyond indexing, mobile experience affects whether visitors stay, enquire, and buy, which affects the signals Google watches over time. A site that is easy to use on a phone earns more calls, more bookings, and more completed forms from the same traffic. That is the real return on mobile work: not a badge or a score, but more of your existing visitors becoming customers. When to fix, and when to rebuild

Fix your current site when it already uses a responsive theme or builder, loads reasonably well, and mainly needs text size, button size, image compression, form shortening, and testing. Many Canadian small business sites can be made properly mobile friendly in an afternoon of focused work, or a few hours with a developer.

Plan a rebuild when your site uses a fixed-width layout that cannot adapt, when it depends on plugins or features that do not work on phones, when it is so slow that compression and cleanup do not help, or when you cannot edit the mobile view without breaking the desktop view. In that case, patching costs more over time than rebuilding once, properly. A rebuild is also the moment to fix the related issues mobile visitors notice most: unclear services, missing prices or price guidance, no online booking, and contact details that are hard to find.

A useful decision rule: if a customer on a phone cannot complete your main action, call, book, buy, or enquire, in under a minute without frustration, the site is costing you business every day it stays that way. Fix the blockers now, and schedule the rebuild if the foundations are the problem.

A mobile friendly checklist you can run today

  • Open your site on a phone. No sideways scrolling, no pinch-to-read text.
  • Body text is 16 pixels or larger, with strong contrast and short paragraphs.
  • Buttons and links are about 44 by 44 pixels, with space between them.
  • Your phone number is tap-to-call and visible without scrolling.
  • Hours, address, and service area match your Google Business Profile.
  • Images are resized and compressed. The homepage loads in a few seconds on mobile data.
  • Forms are short, labelled, and tested end to end on a phone.
  • Pop-ups, if any, are small, delayed, and easy to close with a thumb.
  • Tables, maps, videos, and embeds fit the screen without breaking the layout.
  • PageSpeed Insights and Search Console show no major mobile issues on your key pages.
  • A real person who has never seen your site can call, book, or enquire in under a minute.

Work through the list in order. Most sites fail two or three items, not all eleven, and fixing those two or three is usually enough to feel the difference in calls and enquiries.

Frequently asked questions

How do I make my website mobile friendly?

Start with a responsive design that adapts to any screen width, keep body text at 16 pixels or larger, make buttons at least 44 by 44 pixels with space between them, compress images so pages load in a few seconds, keep forms short, and test on a real phone. Put your phone number, hours, and main call to action where a visitor sees them without scrolling. Those fixes cover most of what mobile friendly means in practice.

How do I test if my website is mobile friendly?

Open your site on your own phone and try to call, book, or send a form with one thumb. Then run the page through Google PageSpeed Insights, which reports mobile speed and usability issues, and check the Mobile Usability report in Google Search Console. Finally, ask two or three customers to find your phone number and hours on their phones. Real devices catch problems that desktop previews miss.

Does a mobile friendly website help SEO?

Yes. Google uses mobile-first indexing, which means it crawls and ranks the mobile version of your site first. A site that is slow, hard to read, or hard to tap on a phone will rank lower and convert fewer visitors. Mobile friendliness is not a separate trick. It is part of how Google judges page experience, along with speed, security, and clear content.

Do I need a separate mobile website or an app?

For most Canadian small businesses, no. One responsive website that works well on phones and computers is cheaper to maintain than a separate mobile site or an app, and it keeps your search rankings in one place. A separate app only makes sense when customers need to log in often, use device features, or work offline. Our guide on mobile app vs website covers that decision in detail.
