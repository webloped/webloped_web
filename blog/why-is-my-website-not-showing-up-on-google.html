Why is my website not showing up on Google?

The short answer: either Google has not found and stored your pages yet, or it has found them but ranks them too low for the searches you tried. Here is how to tell which problem you have, and the fix for each cause, in the order you should check them.

If you are asking why your website is not showing up on Google, you have probably already done the obvious test: you typed your business name or your service into Google, scrolled through the results, and found nothing. That is frustrating, especially after you paid for a website and waited for customers to find it. The good news is that most visibility problems have a specific, fixable cause, and you can usually diagnose it in about fifteen minutes with two free tools.

Before changing anything, it helps to understand what "showing up" actually involves. Google does three separate things, and a problem at any step keeps you invisible. First it crawls your pages, meaning its software visits and reads them. Then it indexes them, meaning it stores them in its database. Only then can it serve them, meaning it shows them when someone searches. A page can fail at any of those steps, and the fix is different for each one. A site that is not indexed needs a technical fix. A site that is indexed but ranks on page six needs better content and stronger local signals. Treating those as the same problem is how owners waste months rewriting pages when a single setting was blocking Google all along.

Step one: find out which problem you have

Start with the quickest test. Go to Google and type site:yourbusiness.ca, using your real domain name and no spaces after the colon. If pages from your site appear, Google has indexed at least some of them, and your problem is ranking, not indexing. Skip ahead to the ranking causes below. If nothing appears, or only a stray page shows up, Google either has not indexed your site yet or something is actively blocking it. That is where the first fixes below come in.

Next, set up Google Search Console if you have not already. It is free, and it is the only place Google tells you directly what it thinks of your site. Add your site, verify ownership, and use the URL Inspection tool on your homepage: paste the full address and it will tell you whether the page is indexed, and if not, why. It will also show crawl errors, mobile usability problems, and any manual actions against the site. Our guide to setting up Google Analytics covers the same kind of verification step, and Search Console pairs with it: Analytics tells you who visited, Search Console tells you whether Google can find you at all.

One more check before you diagnose anything: search while signed out, or in a private browser window, and do not just search for your business name. Google personalises results based on your own browsing history, so a site can look invisible to you and perfectly visible to a stranger, or the other way around. And searching for your exact business name is a much easier test than searching for a service like "plumber in Cambridge". Passing the first test and failing the second is normal for a new site. It does not mean the site is broken.

1. Your site is too new, and Google has not found it yet

This is the most common cause for a brand new website, and the most boring one. Google does not keep a master list of new websites. It finds pages by following links from other pages and by reading sitemaps, and Google itself says crawling can take anywhere from a few days to a few weeks. A brand new domain that nothing links to yet sits at the slow end of that range. Two weeks with nothing is normal, not a sign that something is wrong.

The fix is to give Google a clear path in. Submit your sitemap in Search Console, link to your site from your Google Business Profile and your social profiles, and ask any business directories or partners that already list you to link to the new address. Do not pay anyone who promises to "submit your site to 500 search engines". There is no such service worth buying. Search Console is free, and it is the only submission route Google offers. Once you have done the basics, the honest move is to wait a couple of weeks before changing anything else. Constantly changing URLs and settings while Google is still discovering the site only restarts the clock.

2. A noindex tag or a robots.txt rule is blocking Google

This is the cause that wastes the most time, because everything looks fine to a human visitor while Google is being told to stay away. A noindex tag is a small instruction in a page's code that tells Google not to show that page in results. A robots.txt file can do the same thing for a whole site. Both are legitimate tools during development: a designer will often block search engines while a site is being built so a half-finished version does not go public. The problem is when that block is never removed at launch.

To check, view the source of your homepage in your browser and search the page for "noindex". If it is there, remove it. In WordPress, Wix, Squarespace, and Shopify, the same setting usually appears as a checkbox like "discourage search engines from indexing this site" or a per-page SEO setting. Uncheck it. Then check your robots.txt file by visiting yourdomain.ca/robots.txt. If it says "Disallow: /" for all visitors, that blocks your whole site, and it needs to change to allow Google's crawler. After fixing either one, use Search Console's URL Inspection tool to request indexing again for your key pages. One caution: do not fix robots.txt and noindex at the same time in a contradictory way. If robots.txt blocks a page, Google cannot crawl it to see that you removed the noindex tag, so the old instruction can linger. Fix the robots.txt block first, then the noindex tag.

3. The site is still on staging, password-protected, or marked "coming soon"

Closely related to the last cause, and easy to miss if someone else built your site. If your real site lives at a staging address, if the live domain still shows a "coming soon" page, or if the whole site sits behind a password, Google cannot index what it cannot see. This also happens when a business launches a new site but the old domain still points at the old host, so visitors and Google are both looking at the wrong place.

Check by opening your domain in a private browser window, exactly as a stranger would see it. If you see a login screen, a maintenance page, or an old version of the site, that is your answer. Confirm the domain points to the right place with whoever manages your hosting or domain, and make sure the staging copy is not the version Google is indexing. If the staging site is publicly reachable, password-protect it or block it, so Google does not index the test copy instead of the real one.

4. You have no sitemap, and nothing links to you

A sitemap is a simple file that lists your pages for Google. Most website builders and WordPress generate one automatically, usually at yourdomain.ca/sitemap.xml. If yours does not exist, or if it lists old pages that no longer exist, Google has to discover your pages by following links alone, which is slower and less reliable, especially for a site with few incoming links.

Check that your sitemap exists, that it lists your current pages, and that you have submitted it in Search Console. Then look at the other direction: who links to you? A local business does not need hundreds of links, but it does need some. Your Google Business Profile, your social profiles, local directories like Yelp and Yellow Pages, your chamber of commerce listing, and any suppliers or partners who list you all count. Each one is both a way for customers to find you and a path for Google to discover your site. Inconsistent business details across those listings also hurt local rankings, so use the same business name, address, and phone number everywhere.

5. You are searching for the wrong keywords

This is the cause that feels like a technical problem but is really an expectations problem. A new site will almost never rank for a broad, competitive phrase like "plumber" or "web design" in its first months. Those searches are contested by businesses that have spent years building content, reviews, and links. If you only test those phrases, you will conclude the site is invisible when it is actually ranking for more specific searches you never tried.

Test more precisely. Search for your exact business name, then for your service plus your city, then for a specific service you offer in your town. A Cambridge bakery should test "bakery in Cambridge Ontario" before it worries about "bakery". If the site appears for specific searches but not broad ones, the site is working and the work ahead is content and authority, which takes time. That is the difference between indexed and competitive, and it matters: an indexing problem can be fixed in an afternoon, while a competitiveness problem is a months-long project. We explain that timeline honestly in our guide to how long SEO takes, because confusing the two is how owners either panic too early or wait too patiently on a site that is actually blocked.

6. Your pages are too thin, duplicated, or mismatched to the search

Once Google can find your pages, it still has to decide they deserve to show. Pages with a paragraph of generic text, pages copied from a supplier or a competitor, and pages that all say roughly the same thing give Google very little to rank. The same goes for pages that do not match what the searcher actually wants. A page titled "Services" that lists ten services in one sentence will lose to a competitor's dedicated page that explains one service properly, with pricing guidance, service areas, and answers to common questions.

The fix is unglamorous but reliable. Give each service its own page, written for the customer who searches for that service in your area. Say what the service includes, who it is for, what it costs or what affects the cost, and what happens next. Answer the questions customers actually ask before they call. That is also how you earn the specific, lower-competition searches from the last section. One strong, specific page beats five thin ones, and adding a page for every city you serve, each with genuinely local detail rather than the same text with the city name swapped, is one of the most effective moves a Canadian service business can make.

7. Your Google Business Profile is missing, unverified, or incomplete

For a local business, this cause is separate from your website and just as important. Many "my business is not showing up" complaints are really about the map results, not the website results. If your Google Business Profile is unverified, incomplete, suspended, or duplicated, you will not appear in the local map pack even if your website is perfectly indexed. Customers searching on their phones see the map first, so this one costs real calls.

Claim and verify your profile, fill in every field, choose accurate categories, add photos, list your services and hours, and keep the name, address, and phone number identical to your website. Then earn reviews steadily and reply to all of them. Our guide to getting your business on Google walks through the setup in order, and our Google Maps ranking guide covers what moves you up once you are listed. If your profile was suspended, Search Console will not explain it: check the profile itself for the suspension notice and follow Google's reinstatement steps rather than creating a duplicate listing, which makes the problem worse.

8. The site is slow, broken on mobile, or full of errors

Google judges how real visitors experience your site, and a site that loads slowly, breaks on phones, or throws errors will rank lower even when its content is good. Most Canadian customers now search on their phones, so a site that looks fine on a desktop and falls apart on a phone is losing the searches that matter most. Common culprits are oversized images, too many plugins or apps, cheap shared hosting, and pages that redirect in circles after a redesign.

Run your homepage through Google's free PageSpeed Insights and Search Console's mobile usability report. Fix the biggest items first: compress images, remove anything you do not use, and check that buttons, menus, and forms all work on a small screen. We covered the most common culprits in our guide to why websites load slowly. After a redesign, also check for broken links and missing redirects: if old pages return errors instead of redirecting to their replacements, Google treats the new site as less trustworthy, and visitors who followed old links hit dead ends.

9. A penalty, a hack, or a past problem is holding the site back

This is the least common cause, but it is worth checking because it is invisible from the outside. A manual action in Search Console means a Google reviewer decided the site broke its guidelines, often because of spammy links, hidden text, or copied content. A hacked site can also be flagged, and Google may warn visitors away from it. Either one will suppress rankings until it is fixed and, for a manual action, until a reconsideration request is accepted.

Check Search Console's manual actions report and its security issues report. If either shows a problem, fix the underlying cause first, whether that is removing bad links, cleaning hacked files, or rewriting copied content, and only then submit a reconsideration request explaining what changed. Do not buy bulk "link packages" or cheap SEO services that promise fast rankings: those are the most common way a small business earns a penalty in the first place. If you bought a domain that someone else used before, its history can also follow it, which is one more reason to check Search Console on day one rather than months later.

What to fix first, in order

If you do nothing else, work through this checklist in order and stop as soon as you find your problem. First, confirm the site is actually live and visible in a private browser window, not just to you while signed in. Second, run the site: search to learn whether you have an indexing problem or a ranking problem. Third, check Search Console's URL Inspection tool and its manual actions and security reports. Fourth, check for noindex tags and robots.txt blocks, and confirm your sitemap is submitted. Fifth, claim and verify your Google Business Profile if you serve local customers. Only after those checks come back clean should you move to content, speed, and link building, because no amount of new blog posts will fix a site Google is blocked from reading.

And set honest expectations. Some fixes show up in days: removing a noindex tag, verifying a profile, or fixing a sitemap can change what Google shows within a week or two. Others take months: ranking for a competitive service in a city like Toronto or Vancouver is a project measured in quarters, not days, and it depends on content, reviews, and links compounding over time. If a fix has not changed anything after a few weeks, re-check Search Console rather than assuming it failed. Google's reports will usually tell you whether the page is now indexed, and whether something else is still blocking it.

One last Canadian note. If you serve customers in Quebec, or anywhere with French-speaking customers, consider whether your key pages should exist in French as well. A site that only ranks in English is invisible to a large share of searches in those markets, and that is a content gap, not a technical failure. The same logic applies to any second language your customers actually use: rank for the searches your customers make, in the words they use.

If you have worked through the checklist and still cannot find the problem, that is a reasonable point to get a second pair of eyes on it. A technical audit can find the block or error that a checklist misses, and it is usually faster than guessing for another month. Frequently asked questions

How long does it take for a new website to show up on Google?

Google says crawling a new page can take anywhere from a few days to a few weeks, and ranking for competitive searches takes longer, usually months. If your site is more than a month old, is submitted in Search Console, and still does not appear in a site: search, something is probably blocking it, and the causes above are where to look. If it appears for your business name but not for broad service searches, that is normal progress, not a failure.

Can I pay Google to make my website show up?

You cannot pay for organic rankings, and Google says it does not accept payment to crawl a site more often or rank it higher. You can pay for Google Ads, which puts you at the top of results with an "Ad" label, and that is a legitimate way to get visible while organic rankings build. Anyone selling guaranteed organic rankings, or a fast-track fee to get you listed, is selling something Google does not offer.

Why does my competitor show up but my website does not?

Usually because the competitor has been doing the fundamentals longer: an indexed site, a complete and verified Google Business Profile, steady reviews, service pages that match real searches, and links from local directories and partners. None of that is secret, and none of it is instant. Compare their profile and their service pages with yours side by side, and the gap usually becomes obvious. It is rarely one trick; it is the accumulation of basics done consistently.

Why does only my homepage show up, and not my other pages?

Google often indexes a homepage first and takes longer to discover deeper pages, especially if those pages have no internal links pointing to them and are not listed in your sitemap. Link to every important page from your homepage or main navigation, make sure your sitemap lists them, and use Search Console's URL Inspection tool to request indexing for the pages that matter most. Pages with very little content of their own are also slower to be indexed, so give each page a reason to exist.
